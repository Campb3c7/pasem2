## Slide 2

- Definition of Shock
- A state of cellular and tissue hypoxia due to
  - Reduced oxygen delivery
  - Increased oxygen consumption
  - Inadequate oxygen utilization
  - Combination of the above
- Circulatory failure manifested as reduction in tissue perfusion
- Initially reversible, but quickly progresses to irreversible organ dysfunction

## Slide 3

- Types of Shock
- Four main types of shock:
- Cardiogenic shock (today’s focus)
- Hypovolemic shock
- Obstructive shock
- Distributive shock

## Slide 4

- Types of Shock

## Slide 5

- Causes of Shock
- Hypovolemic shock
  - Hemorrhage
  - Fluid loss due to vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration, pancreatitis
  - Fluid loss due to 3rd spacing (burns)
- Obstructive shock
  - Pulmonary embolism
  - Tension pneumothorax
  - Cardiac tamponade
  - Aortic dissection
- Distributive shock
  - Sepsis
  - Neurogenic shock due to loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone
  - Anaphylaxis

## Slide 6

- Continuum of Shock
- Regardless of the type, there is a physiologic continuum
- Begins with an inciting event, such as a myocardial infarction, an infection, or an injury (i.e. gunshot wound)
- Produces a systemic circulatory abnormality that progresses through complex and intertwined stages:
  - Pre-shock
  - Shock
  - End-organ dysfunction
- Can culminate in irreversible end-organ damage and death

## Slide 7

- Stages of Shock: Pre-Shock
- AKA: “warm shock” or “compensated shock”
- Characterized by rapid compensation for diminished tissue perfusion by various homeostatic mechanisms
- Compensatory mechanisms may allow an otherwise healthy adult to be asymptomatic despite a 10% reduction in total effective arterial blood volume
- Most immediate of compensatory mechanisms are sympathetic and renin systems, which maintain cardiac output and blood pressure
  - Tachycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction may be the only clinical signs of shock during this pre-shock phase

## Slide 8

- Stages of Shock: Shock
- Eventually, compensatory mechanisms become overwhelmed
- Signs and symptoms of organ dysfunction appear
  - Tachycardia
  - Hypotension
  - Tachypnea
  - Anxiety
  - Diaphoresis
  - Metabolic acidosis
  - Oliguria
  - Cool, clammy skin

## Slide 9

- Stages of Shock: End-Organ Dysfunction
- Progressive end-organ dysfunction leads to irreversible organ damage and patient death
- Urine output may decline further
  - Acute renal failure
  - Anuria
- Decreased cardiac output
- Altered mental status
  - Anxiety evolves into delirium (irrational behavior and agitation), obtundation, and coma

## Slide 10

- Response to Shock
- Carotid baroreceptors sense a decrease in volume and trigger a sympathetic mediated response
- Surge of vasoactive hormones are released to maintain cardiac output and blood pressure
  - Epinephrine
  - Norepinephrine
  - Cortisol

## Slide 11

- Response to Shock
- Increase in heart rate
- Increase in cardiac contractility, which results in increased stroke volume (CO = HR x SV)
- Vasoconstriction of both arterioles and veins to maintain blood pressure and increase volume return to the heart
  - Arteriole constriction maintains systemic vascular resistance to maintain blood pressure
    - This response shunts blood away from liver, spleen, kidneys, and periphery
  - Venous constriction results in increased return of blood to the heart

## Slide 12

- Response to Shock
- Other mechanisms in place to help restore decreased cardiac output:
  - Intestines: absorption of fluid from intestinal spaces
  - Kidneys: sympathetic vasoconstriction of blood flow to the kidneys, shunting blood away from the kidneys
  - Pituitary gland: 10% reduction in intravascular volume causes hypothalamus to stimulate posterior pituitary gland to release ADH, causing:
    - Reabsorption of water by the kidneys
    - Increased thirst
    - Decreased urine production, resulting in oliguria

## Slide 14

- Cardiogenic Shock
- Divided into 4 broad categories:
  - Myocardial damage
    - Myocardial infarction
    - Myocardial contusion
    - Cardiomyopathies
  - Sustained arrhythmias
  - Valvular or septal defects
  - Cardiac surgery

## Slide 15

- Epidemiology
- Myocardial infarction is the most common cause
- 6-8% of patients with AMI will develop cardiogenic shock
- ≥ 40% loss of myocardium causes cardiogenic shock
  - Either one massive AMI or a series of AMIs
  - Usually develops within 8 hours of a massive AMI

## Slide 16

- Risk Factors
- Age > 65 years
- Past medical history of CAD, heart failure, or diabetes
- AMI involving the left main coronary artery and its branches, resulting in left ventricular dysfunction
- Mechanical complications
  - Chordae tendineae rupture
  - Rupture of the ventricular septal or free wall

## Slide 17

- Etiology
- Most commonly, patients have multiple vessel disease involving the left anterior portion of the heart
  - Occlusion of the left main coronary artery and its branches
- Right ventricular dysfunction occurs about 5% of the time secondary to an inferior wall AMI
  - Right coronary artery

## Slide 18

- Cardiac Pump Failure
- Occurs when heart fails to pump blood sufficiently to meet body’s demand
- Poor cardiac output is due to decreased contractility, increased preload, and increased afterload
- Eventually, coronary artery perfusion is impaired because of increased preload and afterload, and cardiac function decreases because of poor myocardial oxygen supply
  - Results in decreased cardiac output, leading to hypotension, hypoperfusion, and tissue hypoxia

## Slide 19

- Symptoms
- Chest pain
- Dyspnea
- Diaphoresis
- Vomiting
- Arm pain
- Jaw pain
- Altered mental status
- Weakness

## Slide 20

- Signs
- Blood pressure

  - In shock phase, systolic blood pressure drops to <90 mmHg or       ≥ 40 mmHg drop in systolic blood pressure
- Heart rate
  - Tachycardia is normal compensatory response
  - Exceptions include:
    - Bradycardia can occur due to nodal dysfunction from right sided or inferior AMI
    - Normal heart rate can occur if normal compensatory response is blunted by beta blockers or calcium channel blockers

## Slide 21

  - Displaced PMI suggests preexisting cardiomyopathy or CHF
- Vascular
  - Jugular venous distention > 8 cm
  - Peripheral edema suggests preexisting heart failure (pitting edema)

## Slide 22

- Diagnostic Tests
- Laboratory studies

  - Serum lactate to evaluate for systemic ischemia or sepsis
  - CBC to evaluate for infection or anemia

## Slide 32

- Emergency Management
- Emergency care is a temporizing measure while arranging for revascularization/reperfusion in the cardiac catheterization lab for AMI or surgical intervention for mechanical catastrophe
- Airway management and circulatory stabilization must occur simultaneously, and these will often precede complete history, exam, diagnostic studies, and pharmacologic agents

## Slide 33

- Airway Management
- Supplemental oxygen
  - Nasal cannula
  - Non-rebreather mask
  - Bag-valve mask (if patient is obtunded or comatose, prior to intubation)
- Advanced airway device
  - Endotracheal intubation
  - Most patients in cardiogenic shock will ultimately require intubation and placement on a mechanical ventilator

## Slide 34

- IV Fluids
- IV fluids boluses should be held in cardiogenic shock secondary to left ventricular dysfunction or with any evidence of respiratory distress from pulmonary congestion
  - Establish IV access (saline lock) but hold large boluses of fluids

## Slide 35

- IV Fluids
- Cautious 250 cc boluses can be given to treat mild hypotension if there is no evidence of LV dysfunction (respiratory distress, rales, JVD, or pulmonary congestion on chest x-ray)
- Patient should be assessed before and after every 250 cc bolus of fluid
- (more on fluids later)

## Slide 36

- Pharmaceutical Management
- Treatment of cardiogenic shock requires striking a precarious balance
  - Improve cardiac output
  - Reduce the workload and oxygen needs of the myocardium
  - Increase coronary perfusion
- Blood thinners: prevent coagulopathy
  - Aspirin 324 mg orally
  - IV heparin

## Slide 37

- Pharmaceutical Management
- Sympathomimetic inotropic and vasopressor agents: mainstay of first-line therapy
  - Dobutamine
  - Dopamine
  - Norepinephrine (Levophed)
  - Epinephrine
- Avoid negative inotropes: beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers
  - Block tachycardic response and worsen clinical situation

## Slide 38

- Vasopressors
- Vasopressor agents have variable effects on the alpha adrenergic (α1), beta adrenergic (β1 and β2), and dopaminergic receptors
- Vasopressors improve perfusion pressure in the large vessels
- If multiple vasopressors are used, they should be simplified as soon as the best therapeutic agent is identified
- Administered ideally via central line but initial doses can be delivered through peripheral lines

## Slide 39

- Vasopressors
- Alpha adrenergic
  - α-1 adrenergic receptors in vascular walls induce significant vasoconstriction
- Beta adrenergic
  - β-1 adrenergic receptors in heart increase inotropy and chronotropy with minimal vasoconstriction
  - β-2 adrenergic receptors in blood vessels induce vasodilation

## Slide 40

- Vasopressors
- Dopaminergic
  - Dopaminergic receptors in renal, splanchnic (mesenteric), coronary, and cerebral vascular beds induce vasodilation at lower doses of dopamine (< 10 mcg/kg per minute)
  - Second subtype of dopaminergic receptors induce vasoconstriction by causing norepinephrine release at higher doses of dopamine (> 10 mcg/kg per minute)

## Slide 41

- Vasopressors
| Drug | Action | Cardiac Stimulation | Vasoconstriction |
| Dobutamine | Primarily β1  α1 & β2 (dose dependent) | ++++ | + |
| Dopamine | α1 β1 & β2 Dopaminergic | ++ at 5 mcg/kg/min (intermediate dose) | ++ at 15 mcg/kg/min (high dose) |
| Norepinephrine (Levophed) | Primarily α1 Some β1 | ++ | ++++ |

## Slide 42

- Dobutamine
- Synthetic sympathomimetic agent
  - β1 agonist = positive inotrope
  - β2 agonist = some peripheral vasodilation
- Design leads to increase in myocardial contractility and vasodilation with minimal change in heart rate
  - Increases cardiac output and decreases systemic vascular resistance

## Slide 43

- Dobutamine
- Used for cardiogenic shock in the absence of profound hypotension
  - Should only be used when the SBP is ≥ 90 mmHg (avoid when the SBP is < 90 mmHg)
- Onset of action: 1-2 minutes
- May be used in combination with dopamine

## Slide 44

- Dopamine
- Stimulates dopaminergic receptors, α-1, and β-1
- Inotropic (increased contraction) and chronotropic (increased rate) activity
- Dopaminergic effect at lower doses (< 10 mcg/kg/min) stimulates renal and mesenteric vasodilation
- At higher doses (> 10 mcg/kg/min) stimulates vasoconstriction

## Slide 45

- Dopamine
- Indicated in hypotension state when SBP < 90 mmHg
- Onset of action: 5 minutes
- Can be used as a single agent or in combination with dobutamine
- Can cause dose-limiting dysrhythmias

## Slide 46

- Dopamine
- Lower dosing (< 3 mcg/kg/min)
  - Arterial vasodilation
  - Increases renal blood flow and urine output
- Intermediate dosing (3-10 mcg/kg/min)
  - Mixed dopaminergic and β-1 effects
  - Increases SA node stimulation secondary to β-1 stimulation
  - Improves myocardial contractility and cardiac output
- Higher dosing (> 10 mcg/kg/min)
  - Main effect on α-1 adrenergic receptors, provoking peripheral vasoconstriction
  - Causes vasoconstriction of the kidneys, resulting in renal necrosis and failure

## Slide 47

- Norepinephrine (Levophed)
- Preferred agent in cardiogenic shock
- Stimulates primarily α-1 and β-1 adrenergic receptors
- Inotropic (increased contraction) and chronotropic (increased rate) activity
- Increases arterial and venous vascular tone
- Increases heart rate, ventricular contraction, and peripheral vascular resistance
- Shunts blood away from peripheral circulation

## Slide 48

- Norepinephrine (Levophed)
- Indicated in hypotension states when SBP < 90 mmHg
- Onset of action: 1-2 minutes
- Used as a single potent vasopressor

## Slide 49

- Epinephrine
- Stimulates α-1, β-1, and β-2 adrenergic receptors
- β-1 inotropic (increased contraction) and chronotropic (increased rate) activity – leads to increased cardiac output
- β-2 vasodilation, may ↓ systemic vascular resistance (SVR) at lower doses
- α-1 vasoconstriction, becomes more prominent at higher doses, which increases SVR
- Net effect of SVR is dose dependent

## Slide 50

- Epinephrine
- Used as an alternative to norepinephrine
- Onset of action: 1-2 minutes
- However, associated with increased systemic acidosis, tachycardia, and dysrhythmias compared to norepinephrine

- Estimation of preload and right arterial pressure
- Most accurately measured with central venous catheter in internal jugular vein at top of superior vena cava
- Normal CVP is 8 to 12 mmHg

## Slide 57

- Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP)
- Average pressure in patient’s arteries during one cardiac cycle
- Considered a better indicator of perfusion to vital organs than systolic blood pressure
- Most accurately measured with a continuous pressure monitor via arterial line
- Normal MAP is 65 to 90 mmHg

## Slide 58

- 10-inch-long balloon placed into the descending aorta
- Pumps in synchrony with the heart
- Enhances coronary and systemic perfusion
- Decreases afterload and myocardial O2 demand
- Balloon is timed to inflate during ventricular diastole and deflate just before ventricular systole

## Slide 61

- Goals of Resuscitation
- Urine output: > 0.5 mL/kg/hr (35 mL/hour for average 70 kg adult)
- Central venous pressure (CVP): ≥ 8 mmHg
- Mean arterial pressure (MAP): ≥ 65 mmHg
- Systolic blood pressure (SBP): ≥ 90 mmHg
- SpO2: ≥ 92%

## Slide 62

- SHOCK, PRESSORS & FLUID MANAGEMENT  /  CLINICAL SYNTHESIS
- Treat the physiology—not the blood pressure alone
- Assess shock phenotype   →   Choose an intervention   →   Reassess perfusion AND congestion
- 1  ASSESS
- 2  ACT
- 3  REASSESS
- Is tissue perfusion impaired?
- MAP trend · mentation · capillary refill · urine output · lactate trend
- What mechanism fits?
- Volume loss · pump failure · vasodilation · obstruction
- Is fluid tolerance limited?
- JVP · crackles / O₂ requirement · focused echo or POCUS
- Nonhemorrhagic volume loss
- → Reassessed isotonic crystalloid
- Active hemorrhage
- → Bleeding control + blood products
- Pump failure with congestion
- → Treat cause; support output/pressure
- Vasodilatory shock
- → Treat cause; fluids / pressor as indicated
- Obstructive shock
- → Urgently relieve obstruction
- Intervention depends on cause and clinical context.
- Perfusion improving?
- Mentation · capillary refill · MAP · urine output · lactate trend
- Congestion emerging?
- Respiratory effort / O₂ need · JVP · lung findings · POCUS
- If response is poor:
- Recheck phenotype, fluid responsiveness, source control and need to escalate.

## Slide 64

- Basics of Fluid Management
- Purpose of IV fluid resuscitation is to assist the body in maintaining organ perfusion and oxygenation by providing hemodynamic support to prevent systemic collapse
- Reestablishment of tissue perfusion often re-equilibrates the fluid electrolyte and acid-base imbalance

## Slide 65

- Body Fluid Distribution
- 60% of an average normal adult body weight is water
- There are 2 main compartments where the body fluid is found:
  - Intracellular
  - Extracellular
    - Interstitial
    - Intravascular (Plasma)
    - Transcellular – minor component of ECF. Specialized fluids (ex: CSF, synovial fluid, peritoneal fluid, ocular)

## Slide 66

- Body Fluid Distribution

## Slide 67

- Intracellular Space
- The fluid inside the plasma membrane of the body’s cells
- Average person has 28 liters of intracellular fluid (about 40% of body weight of average adult)

## Slide 68

- Interstitial Space
- Filled with interstitial fluid (water)
- Average person has 10 liters of interstitial fluid (about 14% of body weight of average adult)
- Fluid is a solution that bathes and surrounds the cells
- Provides environment that allows movement of ions, proteins, gases, and wastes across cell barrier

## Slide 69

- Interstitial Space
- Fluid is generated by the systolic force of the heart pushing water out of the capillaries
- Interstitial fluid is recollected into the intravascular space through the lymphatic system
- When excessive fluid accumulates in the interstitial space, edema develops

## Slide 70

- Intravascular Space
- Fluid is made up of plasma, platelets, and RBCs
  - RBCs, platelets, and plasma proteins cannot pass through the walls of the capillaries
- Average person has 5 liters of blood in the intravascular space
- Intravascular volume regulated by osmotic pressure gradients, hydrostatic pressure gradients, and kidneys
  - Hydrostatic pressure is the pushing force
  - Osmotic pressure is the pulling force

## Slide 71

- Transcellular Space
- Compartments which hold fluid found in the body, which are not readily available for use, to maintain hemodynamic support or correct electrolyte imbalances
  - Pleural spaces
  - Peritoneal space
  - Pericardial space
  - Cerebral Spinal Fluid (CSF) space
  - Joint spaces
  - GI tract

## Slide 72

- Third Spacing
- Clinical term describing abnormal redistribution of extracellular fluid into tissues or body cavities
- Important clinical concept:
  - Pt can have excessive fluid in their body while simultaneously having inadequate circulating volume or tissue perfusion!

## Slide 74

- Basics of Fluids and Electrolytes
- Electrolytes: substances that dissociate in solution to form particles or ions
  - Cations: positively charged ions
  - Anions: negatively charged ions
  - Monovalent: ions that carry one charge (K+)
  - Divalent: ions that carry two charges, usually a positive and negative charge (Ca2+;Mg2+)
- Non-electrolyte particles: substances that do not dissociate into ions
  - Glucose
  - Blood urea nitrogen (BUN)

## Slide 75

- Basics of Fluids and Electrolytes
- All body fluids contain equal amounts of cations and anions
- Cations and anions may be exchanged for one another, provided they carry the same charge
  - H+ ion may be exchanged for a K+ ion
  - HCO- ion may be exchanged for a Cl- ion

## Slide 76

- Electrolyte Balance
- Major intracellular electrolytes:
  - Large amounts of potassium
  - Moderate amounts of magnesium and phosphorus
- Major extracellular electrolytes:
  - Large amounts of sodium and chloride
  - Moderate amount of bicarbonate
  - Small amounts calcium

## Slide 78

- Diffusion
- Diffusion: movement of charged or uncharged particles along an osmotic concentration gradient
- In concentrated solution, there are more molecules in motion, creating energy forces that drive particles from areas of high concentration to areas of low concentration
- Despite the significant difference in the concentration of solutes in intracellular and extracellular spaces, the total concentration gradient is the same in both compartments because of the osmotic movement of water

## Slide 79

- Osmosis
- Osmosis: movement of water across a semipermeable membrane
- As with solutes, water diffuses down its concentration gradient, moving from the side of the membrane with greater concentration of water (lesser concentration of solute particles) into a space with lesser concentration of water (greater concentration of solute particles)
- As the water moves across the semipermeable membrane, it generates a pressure called the osmotic pressure

## Slide 80

- Osmolarity and Osmolality
- The osmotic activity of a solution may be expressed in terms of osmolarity or osmolality, the amount of force per volume
- Osmolarity: concentration of osmoles in 1 L of solution
- Osmolality: concentration of osmoles in 1 kg of solvent
- 90-95% of serum osmolarity/osmolality is determined by NaCl and NaHCO3
- 5% of serum osmolarity/osmolality is determined by glucose and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)

## Slide 81

- Osmolal Gap
- Osmolal gap: indication of unmeasured solute in the blood
- osmolal gap  =  measured osmolality  –  calculated osmolality
- normal osmolal gap is < 10 mosmol/kg
- Measured osmolality: measured in lab using osmometer, measures how many osmoles are in plasma of patient
- Calculated osmolality: lab measurement of the serum, calculated using the equation
- serum osmolality  =  2[Na+]  +  (glucose/18)  +  (BUN/2.8)
- normal calculated osmolality is 275-295 mosmol/kg

## Slide 82

- Osmolal Gap
- Normally, measured osmolality = calculated osmolality
  - Hence, normal osmolal gap is usually less than 10 mosmol/kg
- Osmolality of interstitium = osmolality of plasma = osmolality of intracellular fluid
- If the osmolal gap is > 10 mosmol/kg, then there are abnormal solutes in the fluid compartments, which will affect the fluid balance

## Slide 83

- Tonicity
- Tonicity: refers to the osmotic effect that a solution exerts on cell volume in terms of causing a cell to swell or shrink
- Isotonic solutions: cells undergo no change in size
- Hypotonic solutions: cells increase in size
- Hypertonic solutions: cells decrease in size

## Slide 85

- Classification of Intravenous Solutions
- Crystalloids
- Solutions of electrolytes and sterile water
  - Normal saline
  - Lactated ringers
  - Dextrose 5% water
- May be isotonic, hypotonic, or hypertonic when compared to serum plasma
- When infused, 20% stays within the intravascular compartment, while 80% enters the interstitial compartment within 30 minutes
- Colloids
- Human derivatives
  - Fresh frozen plasma
  - Human albumin
  - Whole blood
- Semisynthetic
  - Dextrins
  - Gelatins
  - Hetastarch
- Colloids are primarily retained in the intravascular compartment, resulting in serum plasma expansion

  - 0.9 normal saline solution (0.9NS)
  - Lactated ringers solution (LR)

  - 0.45 normal saline (0.45NS or ½NS)
  - Dextrose 5% in water (D5W)
- Hypertonic solutions
  - Dextrose 5% in normal saline (D5NS)
  - 3% saline solution (3%NS)

## Slide 87

- Sensible and Insensible Losses
- Sensible losses: losses which are easily explained or measured by a clinician
  - Not consuming enough fluids, excessive vomiting, diarrhea, etc.
  - Examples: urine output (~500 mL/day), stool output (~200 mL/day)
- Insensible losses: losses that are not easily measured, or quantified
  - Examples: sweating (~500 mL/day), lungs (~400 mL/day)

## Slide 88

- Hypovolemia
- Hypovolemia may refer to any condition of extracellular volume reduction
  - Vomiting
  - Blood loss
- Severe hypovolemia produces a reduction in tissue perfusion
- Hypovolemic shock is the most common form of shock
- Fluid resuscitation (regardless of the cause) is essential for dealing with intravascular volume depletion

## Slide 89

- Hypovolemia
- When there is volume loss, the body senses a loss in volume and:
  - Stimulates the carotid and aortic baroreceptors, kicking off sympathetic nervous response and causing reduction in vagal stimulation
  - Causes arterial vasoconstriction to shunt flow from areas of less need to areas of more need
  - Increases HR and contractility, causing improved cardiac output
  - Vasoconstricts venous system to augment venous return
  - Releases vasoactive hormones (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine, cortisol) to increase vascular tone
  - Causes renal involvement of RAAS and ADH to cause extravascular volume to be shunted to intravascular volume

## Slide 90

- History of Hypovolemia
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Fever
- Decreased fluid intake
- Chronic disease
- Altered mental status

## Slide 91

- Vitals Signs of Hypovolemia
- Vital signs can be misleading when it comes to hypovolemia
- Classically: tachycardia, hypotension, poor peripheral perfusion (weak pulses and prolonged capillary refill), and decreased mental status are signs of decreased perfusion
- These findings may be delayed in presentation of hypovolemia

## Slide 92

- Physical Exam of Hypovolemia
- Dry mucosa
- Shrunken tongue
- Decreased skin turgor
- Decreased capillary refill
- Lack of tears
- Decreased urine output or wet diapers
- Sunken fontanelles (infants)

## Slide 93

- Diagnostic Studies of Hypovolemia
- Laboratory diagnostics are not always reliable indicators of fluid status
- BUN, creatinine, hematocrit, and urine specific gravity are commonly used but not always sensitive to fluid volume

## Slide 94

- Clinical Assessment of Hypervolemia
- Volume overload is a clinical diagnosis
  - Edema (central or peripheral)
  - Respiratory distress (pulmonary edema)
  - Jugular venous distension
- Risk factors for volume overload include
  - Renal disease
  - Liver disease
  - Heart failure

## Slide 95

- Goals of IV Fluid Replacement
- Goals of replacement therapy:
  - Stop fluid loss
  - Restore volume lost to sustain critical organ perfusion
  - Maintain oxygen carrying capacity for adequate cellular oxygen delivery
  - Correct derangements in electrolytes/coagulation
- In short, assist the body in compensatory efforts, preventing hemodynamic compromise and ultimately systemic collapse

## Slide 96

- Evaluating the Fluid Deficit
- There is no formula that can be used to accurately estimate the total fluid deficit
- Clinical parameters can be used to assess the possible presence of volume depletion, including the blood pressure, heart rate, jugular venous pressure, and urine output
- These parameters should be followed to assess the effectiveness of volume replacement

## Slide 97

- Evaluating the Fluid Deficit
- In the acute (particularly the trauma) setting, fluid and blood products serve to expand volume and act as a bridge to determining source, then control and correct loss
- IV fluid replacement is not an indefinite means of intervention
- IV fluids are a temporizing measure, particularly as it pertains to volume loss from hemorrhage or any unresponsive condition

## Slide 98

- Intravenous Access
- First, assume airway and breathing are accomplished and stable
- Usually, establishing IV access occurs simultaneously with the assistance of the nursing staff
- Options for intravenous access include:
  - Peripheral line
  - Central line
  - Intra-osseous line

## Slide 99

- Crystalloids
- Isotonic crystalloids are the preferred initial agent in fluid resuscitation
- Due to the low oncotic pressure, crystalloid solutions experience a substantial shift from the intravascular space into the interstitial spaces
- 3:1 rule – theoretic concept for fluid resuscitation
  - For every amount of blood lost, 3 times this amount of isotonic crystalloid is required to effectively restore intravascular volume, because at best only 30% will remain in the intravascular space
- Actual determination of volume loss is made clinically

## Slide 100

- Colloids
- No evidence of improved outcomes when considering crystalloid vs. colloid
- In the presence of hemorrhagic disease and sepsis as sources of volume loss, the vessels become so permeable that even the proteins, once prohibited from traversing across the vessel, can flow freely into the interstitial space
- Colloids are costly

## Slide 101

- Blood Transfusions
- No hard or fast rule regarding blood transfusions
- Most common transfused blood product is PRBCs
- 0.9NS is the only IV fluid that can be administered in conjunction with blood products
- Following 2 L of rapid transfusion of a crystalloid solution, blood transfusion should be considered to avoid hemodilution
  - Permissive hypotension in the treatment of hypovolemia due to hemorrhage from trauma

## Slide 102

- Blood Transfusions
- Prior to blood transfusion (if possible):
  - Discuss risks and benefits and obtain consent from patients
  - Order a type and screen when anticipating the need for blood products
  - Order a type and cross when urgent need for blood products

## Slide 103

- Blood Transfusions
- If hemodynamic instability from a gross blood loss, then immediate transfusion may be necessary
  - Emergency release blood (O negative)
  - 1:1:1 ratio (PRBCs : FFP : Platelets) – initial starting point for massive transfusion protocol (MTP)
  - Massive transfusion (> 10 units/24 hours)
- Remain mindful of the complications that can occur related to blood transfusions
  - Acute febrile hemolytic reaction
  - TRALI
  - Anaphylaxis

## Slide 104

- Rate of Fluid Resuscitation
- For mild or moderate hypovolemia, rapid fluid resuscitation is not necessary
- To avoid worsening of the volume deficit, the rate of fluid administration must be greater than the rate of continued fluid losses
- Usually, 1 liter isotonic saline bolus to correct existing fluid deficit followed by 100 cc/hour until clinical parameters improve

## Slide 105

- Rate of Fluid Resuscitation
- Severe volume depletion or hypovolemic shock requires rapid resuscitation
- Provide at least 1 to 2 liters of isotonic saline boluses wide open in an attempt to restore tissue perfusion
- Monitor effects of fluid resuscitation
  - Blood pressure
  - Heart rate
  - JVP
  - Urine output
  - Mental status

## Slide 106

- Indications for Isotonic Solutions0.9 Normal Saline (0.9NS)
- Most commonly used intravenous solution
- Isotonic solution that matches blood serum osmolarity
- Can be given simultaneous to blood products
- Indications:
  - Hypovolemia			—Dehydration
  - Blood loss			—DKA
  - Hyponatremia
- Precautions:
  - Heart failure from excessive use
  - Acidemia with prolonged use (0.9 NS induced hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis)

## Slide 108

- Indications for Isotonic SolutionsLactated Ringers (LR)
- Electrolyte composition similar to serum plasma
- Commonly used with surgical patients
- Lactate acts as a buffer to prevent metabolic acidosis
- Indications:
  - Acute blood loss (operating room, trauma)

- Precautions:
  - Renal disease
  - Liver disease
  - pH > 7.5

## Slide 109

## Slide 110

- Indications for Hypotonic SolutionsDextrose 5% Water (D5W)
- Indications:
  - Used for free-water replacement, not for volume resuscitation
  - Maintenance fluid only (100 cc/hour)
  - Hypoglycemia (oral hypoglycemic overdose)
  - Malnourished
- Precautions:
  - Hyperglycemia from excessive use

## Slide 111

- Indications for Hypertonic Solution3% Normal Saline (3%NS)
- Indications:
  - Severe hyponatremia with symptoms (e.g., seizures, encephalopathy)
  - Intracranial hemorrhage (to decrease intracranial pressure)
- Precautions:
  - Rapid net correction of sodium can result in osmotic demyelination syndrome (formally known as central pontine myelinolysis)
    - Destruction of the myelin sheath down axons into the brain stem, causing death

## Slide 112

- SHOCK, PRESSORS & FLUID MANAGEMENT  /  CLINICAL SYNTHESIS
- Treat the physiology—not the blood pressure alone
- Assess shock phenotype   →   Choose an intervention   →   Reassess perfusion AND congestion
- 1  ASSESS
- 2  ACT
- 3  REASSESS
- Is tissue perfusion impaired?
- MAP trend · mentation · capillary refill · urine output · lactate trend
- What mechanism fits?
- Volume loss · pump failure · vasodilation · obstruction
- Is fluid tolerance limited?
- JVP · crackles / O₂ requirement · focused echo or POCUS
- Nonhemorrhagic volume loss
- → Reassessed isotonic crystalloid
- Active hemorrhage
- → Bleeding control + blood products
- Pump failure with congestion
- → Treat cause; support output/pressure
- Vasodilatory shock
- → Treat cause; fluids / pressor as indicated
- Obstructive shock
- → Urgently relieve obstruction
- Intervention depends on cause and clinical context.
- Perfusion improving?
- Mentation · capillary refill · MAP · urine output · lactate trend
- Congestion emerging?
- Respiratory effort / O₂ need · JVP · lung findings · POCUS
- If response is poor:
- Recheck phenotype, fluid responsiveness, source control and need to escalate.
