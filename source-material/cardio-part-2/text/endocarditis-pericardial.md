## Slide 6

- Infective endocarditis (IE)
- A bacterial or fungal infection of the valves or endocardium including the great vessels.
- Risk factors for native valve infectious endocarditis (NVE):
  - Rheumatic
  - Congenital or degenerative valve disease
    - Including MVP with mitral regurgitation
  - Congenital heart defects: VSD, Tetralogy of Fallot, coarct, PDA
  - IVDA
  - Poor dentition or frequent dental infection
  - HACIE: Healthcare-associated IE, up to 15-30%
    - Intravascular devices, hyperalimentation catheters, pacemakers, shunts

> Notes: Tetralogy of Fallot: Stenosis of the pulmonary artery, ventricular septal defect(VSD), origin of the aorta overriding the right and left ventricles and concentric RV hypertrophy 
> PDA: patent Ductus arteriosus

- IE occurs on native valve when bacteria or fungi gain access to the valve during a transient bacteremia

## Slide 8

- IE Pathophysiology
- For IE to develop you must have:
  - 1. Damaged or change to normal endothelial surfaces,
  - 2. Bacteremia, and
  - 3. Adherence to the surface
- Undamaged endothelium is resistant to infection by most bacteria and thrombus formation.
- Therefore endothelial injury is initial step
- This is followed by platelet and fibrin adherence
  - Serves as a scaffold
- This becomes secondarily infected by transient bacteria

## Slide 9

- IE: Pathophysiology and the Development of Vegetation
- The typical lesion of IE is a vegetation: a mass of fibrin and cellular elements of the blood, that becomes a haven for bacteria to attach.
- Vegetations most commonly found at areas of congenital malformation or areas of valve damage, on the left side of the heart.

## Slide 10

- IE: Development of Vegetation - common locations
- Vegetations are most commonly found on the lower pressure side when blood travels through a narrow orifice.
  - Atrial side of mitral valve; corde tendineae of the ventricle of the aortic valve
- IE is most likely with small, tight lesions.
  - A high-pressure differential between the two chambers connected by the defect predisposes to IE
    - Small VSD more likely this will occur over a larger VSD

## Slide 11

- IE: Pathophysiology
- The bacterial infection of the endocardium causes a inflammatory reaction that can cause erosions and further damage.
  - Without tx vegetation enlarges leading to regurgitation due to destruction of the leaflet or scarring and retraction of the leaflet
  - Erosions may lead to perforation

## Slide 12

- IE Pathophysiology
- Underlying disease present in IE
- Congenital: 10-20%
- Atherosclerotic/pre-existing heart disease: 30-40%
  - Rheumatic, MVP, aortic, aortic stenosis
- PVE: 15-30%
- IVDU: 10-20%
- Cardiac devices: ~5%
- Valves most commonly affected by IE
- Mitral: ~41%
- Aortic: ~37%
- Mitral and Aortic: 5-15%
- Tricuspid: ~15-20%
- Pulmonary: 1%

> Notes: PVE: Prosthetic valve

## Slide 13

- IE Epidemiology
- The average age of a patient with endocarditis in the US has become older.
- Due to:
  - Decreasing rates of rheumatic heart disease
  - Increases in instrumentation, invasive procedures and lines predisposing the patient to bacteremia
  - People living longer with immunosuppression
  - Significant number of IE are nosocomial now

## Slide 14

- IE: Native Valve Organisms

- Staphylococcus aureus…most common
- Streptococcus viridans ~ 17%
- Enterococci

## Slide 15

- IE: Special populations – Prosthetic Valve
- Prosthetic valve replacement:
  - Highest risk in the first year, but remains elevated through the life Early PVE:
  - If IE occurs with valve replacement likely related to contamination from surgery
  - hematogenous seeding from extra-cardiac site
    - Such as UTI at time of surgery causing seeding
  - Commonly staph and strep, gram neg bacilli and Candida
- Late PVE: >12 months
  - Bacteria is similar to native valve infections
  - Usually a result of oral or skin site that seeds the valve
- IE can cause prosthetic valve dehiscence and perivalvular leak

## Slide 16

- IE: Special populations - IVDA
- IVDA: majority do not have known valve dz
- IE is more likely to affect the tricuspid valve in these patients. Why?
- Same organisms as native valve IE are likely
- S. Aureus  is the most common bug in IVDA ~70% of cases
- Strep and enterococci are second leading cause.
- Pseudomonas, serratia, Candida, unusual pathogens, and polymicrobial infections – cause hygiene not great.

## Slide 17

- Natural History of IE
- IE has a wide spectrum of illness:
- Increasingly, rapid valvular destruction and CHF when highly virulent organisms invade, such as S. aureus, Strep pneumoniae, Strep pyogenes and Neisseria gonorrhea.
- Indolent course with few symptoms despite valve destruction with bacteria such as Strep viridans, Enterococcus faecalis and some GNRs, is becoming more rare

## Slide 29

- Duke criteria:
- Microbiology criteria (+ in either blood culture or lab test)
    - Positive blood cultures
      - Positive blood culture for typical (common) organisms from 2+ or more cultures OR
      - Microorganism that occasionally or rarely causes IE from 3+ (non-typical)
    - Positive Laboratory tests
      - + PCR or other nucleic acid-based technique for Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella species, or Tropheryma whipplei from blood OR
      - Coxiella burnetti antiphase I IgG antibody titer >1:800, or isolated from a single blood culture OR
      - Indirect immunofluorescence assays (IFA) for detection of IgM and IgG antibodies to Bartonella henselae or Bartonella quintana with IgG titer >1:800
- Major Diagnostic Criteria
- 2023

## Slide 30

- Dukes Criteria
- Imaging:
  - Echo and/or cardiac CT showing:
    - vegetation, valvular/leaflet perforation, valvular/leaflet aneurysm, abscess, pseudoaneurysm, or intracardiac fistula OR
    - Significant new valvular regurgitation on echo compared to previous. Worsening pre-exiting regurgitation is NOT sufficient. OR
    - New partial dehiscence of prosthetic valve
  - PET/CT imaging
    - Abnormal  metabolic activity involving a native or prosthetic valve, ascending aortic graft, intracardiac device leads or other prosthetic material
  - Surgical major criteria
    - Evidence of IE documented by direct inspection during heart surgery neither major imaging or histologic or micro-confirmed
- Major Diagnostic Criteria
- 2023

## Slide 41

- Prophylactic Antibiotic guidelines
- Based more on theoretical rather than data from clinical trials
  - No human study has definitively demonstrated that prophylactic  ABX prevent endocarditis after invasive procedures.
- However, if patient is having a high risk procedure AND has high risk cardiac condition the suggestion is to prophylactically treat for endocarditis.

## Slide 42

- Prevention: Patients at Highest Risk
- AHA IE recommends prophylaxis for:
  - All prosthetic heart valves including TAVR, mechanical and bioprosthetic valves, all prosthetic valve repairs
  - Pts with ventricular assist device
  - Anyone with a h/o IE
  - Valve disease in cardiac transplants
  - Congenital hear conditions…see next slide

## Slide 43

- Prophylaxis for endocarditis: Congenital Heart Conditions
  - Unrepaired or incompletely repaired cyanotic congenital heart disease, including those with palliative shunts and conduits
  - Completely repaired congenital heart defect with prosthetic material or device, during the first six months after the procedure
  - Any repaired congenital heart defect with residual defect at the site or adjacent to site of a prosthetic patch or a prosthetic device

## Slide 44

- Prophylactic Antibiotic guidelines: Procedure
- Procedures for which IE prophylaxis is recommended:
- Dental:
  - Any manipulation of the gingiva or oral mucosa perforation: cleaning, extractions, abscesses.
  - These are the most high risk
- Respiratory:
  - Tonsillectomy & adenoidectomy, bronchoscopy with biopsy of the tract
- Cardiac procedures with prosthetic material
- GI or GU: no routine prophylaxis
- Skin:
  - Surgical procedure for the management of infected skin, skin structure, or musculoskeletal tissue
    - Example: abscess
  - Skin flora should be covered

## Slide 45

  - Rheumatic heart disease
  - MVP
  - Bicuspid aortic valve
  - HOCM
  - ASD
  - h/o pacers, stents, ICD or CABG procedure
- AHA IE prophylaxis recommendations:
  - Drug of choice: Amoxil 2 gm po 1 hr. before dental procedure

## Slide 47

- The Pericardium
- A protective two layer sac around the heart.
- Parietal pericardium (outer layer) is tough fibrous layer, and the inner visceral pericardium is a delicate overlay of the myocardial surface.
- Normally, 5-15 ml of pericardial fluid, secreted by the parietal pericardium
  - Provides lubrication between heart and surrounding viscera

## Slide 48

- Pericardial Disease
- Acute Pericarditis:
  - Inflammation of the sac surrounding the heart
- Pericardial effusion:
  - A collection of inflammatory fluid that forms between the visceral and parietal pericardial layers.
- Chronic pericarditis:
  - Diffuse and symmetric permanent scarring of the pericardium secondary to inflammation

## Slide 49

- Acute Pericarditis: Etiologies
- Acute inflammation of the pericardium from a number of etiologies that include:
- Viral: Most common
  - Likely etiology of idiopathic
  - Coxsackievirus, echoviruses, influenza, EBV, HIB
- Tuberculosis: rare in the US but common in undeveloped nations
  - Other bacterial causes are rare

## Slide 50

- Acute Pericarditis: Etiologies
- Uremia:
  - Mechanism is unknown
  - Occurs in stable dialysis patients and those with untreated renal failure
- Neoplastic:
  - Spread of primary CA to pericardium
  - Lung, breast, renal cell, Hodgkin's and NHL
- Inflammatory
  - Dressler’s: usually a few days to weeks after transmural MI, an inflammatory response to infarct
  - Connective tissue disorder: RA, SLE, drug reaction can provoke pericarditis
- Trauma

## Slide 51

- Chest pain: sharp, substernal chest pain that begins abruptly and is pleuritic
- Exacerbating factor:
  - Supine
- Relieving factor:

- Dyspnea
- Fever

- Acute Pericarditis: Treatment
- Depends on etiology
- Treat underlying cause
- If idiopathic: (both are used)

## Slide 56

- If pericarditis resulting in pericardial effusions is significant or recurrent, a pericardial window may be performed

## Slide 58

- Pericardial Effusions
- Signs:
- Usually none but can have:
  - A rub if large
  - Muffled heart sounds
  - Inability to palpate the PMI
- Etiologies: can be the result of any disease causing acute pericarditis.
- Most commonly:
  - Idiopathic

  - Neoplastic

## Slide 59

- Cardiac Tamponade
- Occurs when a pericardial effusion grows too large or accumulates too rapidly resulting in impaired pumping of the heart
  - Filling of the right will initially be impaired followed by the left heart
- In response to the decrease in cardiac output the body will do what?
- This response may compensate for a short time, but eventually pt will develop s&s of tamponade

## Slide 60

- Cardiac Tamponade: S & S
- S&S will depend on the rate of effusion accumulation, hydration, and severity of cardiac tamponade
- Acute cardiac tamponade:
  - More sudden in onset and may be associated with chest pain, tachypnea, dyspnea, JVD and hypotension. Will have symptoms related to decreased cardiac output
- Subacute Cardiac tamponade:
  - Less dramatic.  Pt may be asymptomatic early but once pressure on heart reaches critical levels they will complains of SOB, chest discomfort or fullness, peripheral edema, fatigability

## Slide 61

- Cardiac Tamponade: S & S
- Tachycardia
- Classic triad: Hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds
- Narrow pulse pressure
- Elevated jugular venous pressure
- Pulsus paradoxus: fall in SBP > 10mmHg during inspiration

- Echocardiogram will be the test of choice

- EKG:

## Slide 68

- Constrictive Pericarditis
- The pericardium becomes thickened and fibrotic, and occasionally calcified.
  - This restricts diastolic filing of the heart
  - What does this cause?
- Etiologies are same for acute pericarditis
  - Idiopathic or viral is most common in developed countries
  - Post radiation
  - Post cardiac surgery
  - Connective tissue disorder
  - TB(MC developing countries), uremia and other potential causes

## Slide 69

- Constrictive Pericarditis
- Signs and symptoms are consistent with slowly advancing heart failure:
  - DOE
  - Fluid overload type picture
  - Decrease exercise tolerance
  - Chest pain
  - Elevated JVP
- Pericardial “Knock”: an early diastolic sound at LSB, as the apex recoils from systole

## Slide 71

- Constrictive Pericarditis: Diagnosis
- CXR:  thickened and calcified pericardium and/or Pleural effusions
- EKG:  non-specific ST-T wave changes and QRS voltage
  - Atrial dysrhythmias are common
- Echo: thickened and immobile pericardium with septal motion abnormalities
  - This usually makes the diagnosis. (essential diagnostic test)
  - Not moving the way it should and increased pericardial thickness
- CT/MRI: gives additional anatomic info if surgery considered. Can show extent of pericardial thickening/calcification/scarring
- If patients echo is non-diagnostic or poor quality:
  - Cardiac cath is needed to make the diagnosis and evaluate cardiac function

## Slide 72

- Constrictive Pericarditis: Treatment
- Constrictive pericarditis is a progressive disease
- Early disease is treated similar to acute pericarditis with NSAIDS and colchicine for 2-3 months
- Tachycardia is a protective mechanism
  - Beta blockers and calcium channel blockers should be avoided
- Medical therapy such as diuretics and salt restriction can be used if patients are not a surgical candidate in late disease
- Pericardiectomy for patients with moderate to severe disease.
