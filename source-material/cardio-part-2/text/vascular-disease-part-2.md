## Slide 5

- Venous Insufficiency: Patho
- Normal venous return: adequate muscle contraction and competent venous valves.
- Insufficiency occurs by one of the below mechanisms:
  - Venous value incompetence: Blood refluxes and pools in lower extremity…results in increased venous pressure.
    - Persistent venous HTN causes further damage of the bicuspid valves and dilated tortuous veins.
  - Inadequate muscle pump function
  - Venous outflow obstruction
  - Or a condition that results in increased venous pressure (Obesity, heart failure, venous compression)

## Slide 15

- Venous Insufficiency: Diagnosis
- Clinical Exam/Hx
- Venous Duplex US (B-mode + Doppler)
  - B-mode evaluates venous anatomy and compressibility
  - Doppler: shows flow direction and reflux
    - Show retrograde flow of >0.5 sec in duration
    - evaluate the nature and extent of venous reflux
  - Evaluates location and extend of superficial and deep insufficiency
  - Excludes or includes other diagnoses too

## Slide 16

- Venous Insufficiency: Primary Care Tx
- Conservative tx:
- Lifestyle modifications-avoid prolonged standing/sitting, wt. loss when indicated
- Leg elevation-when able
- Exercise: walking and calf muscle exercises to improve venous return.
- Discuss proper skincare
  - Prevention of dryness and developing fissures
  - Don’t itch/scratch to prevent development of ulcers.
- Compression therapy/stockings
  - Reduces edema and improves venous sx
  - Also indicated for ulcer management
  - Assess for PAD if indicated

## Slide 20

- Superficial Phlebitis vs. Thrombophlebitis
- Superficial phlebitis- is inflammation of vein without thrombus
- Superficial thrombophlebitis- inflammation and confirmed thrombosis within a superficial vein.
  - Not considered a DVT, but may extend into deep venous system
    - SVT near saphenofemoral junction carries increased risk of DVT and PE
- Signs and symptoms include:
  - Localized pain, tenderness, redness, swelling along the course of a superficial vein
  - Vein feels like a palpable cord

## Slide 23

- Thrombophlebitis migrans (Trousseau Syndrome)
- Migrating thrombophlebitis due to thrombosis of superficial veins in multiple areas of the body at the same time.
- Associated with hypercoagulability
  - Glioma
  - Adenocarcinoma of pancreas, lung
- Patients have multiple superficial areas of thrombophlebitis
- Can sometimes be an early sign of cancer
- Known as Trousseau’s Syndrome or Migratory thrombophlebitis

## Slide 24

- Superficial phlebitis / Thrombophlebitis: Diagnosis
- Clinically you will suspect this based on H&P
  - However, you should rule out the presence of coexistent DVT
- Obtain a venous duplex US
  - Confirms SVT and rules out DVT
  - Also determines the proximity of the SVT to the deep venous system

## Slide 25

- Superficial phlebitis / Thrombophlebitis: Treatment
- Treatment depends on location, extent, and risk of thrombus extension.
- Conservative tx:
  - NSAIDS- for pain/inflammation
  - Warm compresses
  - Removal of IV catheter if possible
- Antibiotics
  - Not indicated for uncomplicated superficial thrombophlebitis
  - If infectious thrombophlebitis cover with empiric antibiotics
- Anticoagulants generally not indicated for most SVT
  - Unless near saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction

## Slide 43

- Pulmonary Embolism (PE): Definition
- Obstruction of the Pulmonary Artery or of its branches.
  - Thrombus
    - Usually from a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) that breaks loose and travels ultimately settling in the lungs.
    - Lower extremity DVT above the knee is likely to travel vs below the knee stay
  - Other causes:
    - Tumor
    - Fat
    - Air
- Purpose of this lecture will focus on thrombus as this is the most common

## Slide 44

- PE: Pathophysiology
- DVT breaks off section causing embolus
- Embolus travels through right heart to pulmonary artery ending in lung
- This blocks blood flow to lung resulting in inadequate O2 exchange leading to hypoxia
- As heart struggles to push against increased resistance patient can develop increased right heart strain
- This decreases preload to left heart   heart failure, death
- Results in decreased cerebral perfusion, shock and death

## Slide 49

- Epidemiology
- Venous thromboembolism (VTE) affects up to 900K annually (CDC estimates)
- Sudden death in about 25% of PEs.
- VTE slightly higher in men, although risk varies with age and hormonal factors.
- Risk increases with age, particularly after 60.
- Special populations risk is significantly higher:
  - Hospitalized pts
  - Pts undergoing major surgery
  - Pts with active malignancy
  - Pregnant and postpartum pts

- PE Workup
- Obtain a complete history and PE.
  - Ask specific questions such as travel, surgery, family history of PE, surgery  or injury, hormone etc.…
- Once history obtained you can use two clinical prediction tools to determine if a D-dimer or more advnaced imaging should be done (assuming they are hemodynamically stable):
  - PERC: May exlude PE w/o further testing in very-low risk pts
  - Wells: estimates pretest probability and guild D-dimer vs imaging.

## Slide 53

- D-Dimer
- Bi-product of fibrin degradation
- If D-dimer is +, it's not specific to a PE, but will warrant advanced imaging to rule out a PE
- If D-dimer is negative, because it is so sensitive, you can rule out a PE.
  - No further testing warranted
- This number can be adjusted by patient's age
- Not typically done in pregnancy as it is usually elevated

## Slide 54

- PERC Criteria
- PERC is used only in patients with low clinical suspicion for PE. If all eight criteria are negative, PE can be excluded without additional testing. If any criterion is positive, further assessment is needed.
  - Age ≥50 years old
  - Pulse ≥100 bpm
  - SaO2 < 95%
  - Hemoptysis
  - Estrogen use
  - Surgery /trauma/ hospitalization in the last 4 weeks
  - Prior venous thromboembolism
  - Unilateral leg swelling
- If I have even one on this list that is positive what’s the next step? D-Dimer or CTA PE protocol?

## Slide 55

- Wells Clinical Prediction rule for PE
| Criteria | Score |
| Clinical signs and symptoms of DVT | 3 |
| PE is #1 diagnosis or equally likely | 3 |
| HR > 100 | 1.5 |
| Immobilization at least 3 days, or Surgery in the last 4 weeks | 1.5 |
| Previous diagnosed PE or DVT | 1.5 |
| Hemoptysis | 1 |
| Active malignancy (Tx ongoing, w/I past 6 months, or pallative) | 1 |

## Slide 56

- Wells risk score interpretations
- > 6 points:  high risk
  - Proceed to advanced imaging such as a CTA or VQ scan
  - D-dimer NOT recommended
- 2 to 6 points:  moderate risk
  - Obtain a high-sensitive D-dimer
  - Negative D-Dimer → PE can generally be excluded.
  - Positive D-Dimer → CTPA or V/Q scan as appropriate
- <2 points:  low risk
  - Consider PERC only if clinical suspicion is sufficiently low.
    - PERC negative → No additional testing.
    - PERC positive → Obtain D-dimer
  - Positive D-Dimer → Diagnostic imaging
- Document decision making section

> Notes: Don’t memorize this scoring

## Slide 60

- D-Dimer in Pregnancy: Evaluation for PE
- D-Dimer can be used to exclude PE in pregnancy with low clinical suspicion.
  - A negative high-sensitivity D-Dimer, combined with low clinical probability, can safely exclude PE.
  - Three month VTE rate following a neg D-Dimer: 0.32%
- Limitations during pregnancy:
  - D-Dimer levels increase with gestational age.
  - Fewer than 5% of suspected PE in third trimester have a negative D-Dimer using traditional cutoff.
  - No universally accepted pregnancy-adjusted cutoff exists.
- Clinical application:
  - A positive D-dimer does not diagnose PE; further evaluation is required.
  - Pregnancy-adapted YEARS criteria with D-Dimer may be used.

## Slide 78

- Classification of PE
- High risk (Massive) PE
  - Hemodynamically unstable
    - Sustained SBP <90 mmHg or a drop in systolic of ≥ 40mmHg from baseline
    - Often have right ventricular dysfunction
    - Massive refers to hemodynamic compromise not the size of the PE
- Intermediate risk (Submassive) PE
  - Hemodynamically stable
  - Nml vitals or near nml
  - Right ventricle dysfunction and/or elevated cardiac markers(Troponin or BNP)
- Low-risk PE
  - No evidence of right ventricle strain or elevation of biomarkers
  - Hemodynamically stable

## Slide 87

- TPA - Absolute contraindications
- Intracranial hemorrhage
- Ischemic stroke within 3 months
- Suspect aortic dissection
- Active bleeding
- Recent head/face injury with radiology evidence bony fx or brains injury
- Recent surgery on brain or involving spinal canal
- Known malignant neoplasm of brain/spinal cord

> Notes: Relative contraindications include
> age >75 years;
> current use of anticoagulation;
> pregnancy;
> noncompressible vascular punctures;
> traumatic or prolonged cardiopulmonary resuscitation (>10 minutes);
> recent internal bleeding (within 2 to 4 weeks);
> history of chronic, severe, and poorly controlled hypertension;
> severe uncontrolled hypertension on presentation (systolic blood pressure >180 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure >110 mm Hg);
> dementia;
> remote (>3 months) ischemic stroke; and
> major surgery within 3 weeks.

## Slide 90

- Treatment-IVC filter
- Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) filter
  - Purpose: Traps blood clots traveling from the lower extremities towards the lung.
  - Does not treat VTE

- Other indications:
  - Recurrent PE despite use of anticoagulant
- Not routinely recommended in pts who can receive anticoagulation.

## Slide 92

- PE Tx: Low Risk
- Low risk PE:
  - They have symptoms, but nml vitals and no heart strain
    - A Pulmonary embolisms severity index (PESI) can help determine low-risk patients who can be treated as out-patient
- Initiate anticoagulation:
  - DOAC preferred for most eligible pts
  - Minimum tx is 3 months.
- Disposition:
  - Output tx or early discharge may be appropriate
  - Use validated risk assessment tool (PESI) to help determine eligibility

## Slide 93

- PE Tx: Intermediate risk
- Clinical presentation:
  - Hemodynamically stable- no persistent hypotension or shock
  - RV dysfunction and/or elevated cardiac biomarkers (troponin or BNP)
- Tx:
  - LMWH for most pts
  - Consider unfractionated heparin (UFH) when rapid adjustment or urgent procedure anticipated
  - Admit telemetry vs ICU vs step down
  - Advanced interventions:
  - Not routinely done
  - Consider urgent consult if pt develops impending cardiopulmonary failure
- Discharge:
  - Continue anticoagulation for at least 3 months, with individualized according to recurrence and bleeding risk. See up coming slide.

## Slide 94

- High-Risk PE Tx
- Clinical Presentation:
  - Persistent hypotension, obstructive shock, or cardiac arrest
  - Often significant RV dysfunction
- Immediate management:
  - Urgent ICU level care
  - O2 and hemodynamic support; vasopressors are needed
- Reperfusion therapy:
  - Systemic thrombolysis (alteplase) is recommended as a primary options
  - Other considerations:
    - Catheter directed thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy
    - Surgical embolectomy considered when thrombolysis contraindicated
- While advanced therapies are being arranged immediate anticoagulation must be initiated: LMWH
  - Held during systemic TPA

## Slide 95

- Long Term VTE Anticoagulant Selection
- General population:
  - Factor Xa inhibitors (DOACS) preferred
- Patients with active cancer:
  - DOACS
  - LMWH
- Pregnancy:
  - LMW Heparin preferred
- Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)
  - Warfarin (Coumadin) preferred

## Slide 96

- Venous Thromboembolism: Long Term Tx
- Duration of long-term anti-coagulation is dependent on clinical scenario:
  - First proximal DVT or PE due to provoked cause
    - Ex: Surgery, trauma, temp immobilization, long flight to Asia.
    - Anticoagulation 3 months
  - First DVT/PE unprovoked:
    - No identifiable precipitating factor
    - At least 3 months then reassess risk/benefit.
    - Extended anticoagulation often recommended (6-12 months)
  - Recurrent DVT/PE:
    - Indefinite anticoagulation
  - Persistent Risk Factors:
    - Ex: Active cancer or APS
    - Extended treatment often indicated

## Slide 97

- Venous Thromboembolism: Long Term Anticoagulation
- Things that are considered during reassessment:
  - Unprovoked DVT/PE
    - Extended or indefinite anticoagulation is often recommended if bleeding risk is acceptable
  - Factors considered when determining duration
    - Risk of Recurrence
      - Provoked vs Unprovoked VTE
      - H/O previous VTE
      - Persistent risk factors (Active CA, APS)
    - Risk of major bleeding
    - Pt preferences and ability to maintain tx
  - Important considerations:
    - Routine D-dimer, repeat US, and thrombophilia testing are generally not needed solely to determine tx duration
- This decision must be made with the pt and is individualized based on risk factors, provoked/unprovoked and risk for recurrence and bleeding.
