## Slide 10

- Mechanisms of Arrhythmias
- Arrhythmias occur when the heart’s normal electrical signals are disrupted. They can result from problems with how impulses are formed, conducted, or recycled in the heart.
- Disorders of Impulse Formation - The heart’s pacemaker (the SA node) fails to generate or time signals correctly.
  - Sinus node arrest: SA node temporarily stops firing.
  - Premature beats: Extra beats start from the atria or ventricles before the next normal beat.
  - Ectopic focus: A different area takes over as the pacemaker.
- Disorders of Impulse Conduction - Electrical signals start normally but get blocked or delayed as they move through the heart.
  - SA block: Impulse doesn’t leave the SA node properly.
  - AV block: Impulse is slowed or stopped before reaching the ventricles.
- Reentry - A single impulse repeatedly travels in a loop, reactivating tissue that should already be resting.
  - Example: AV nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT) or Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.
- Triggered Activity - After a heartbeat, some cells fire again before they should, causing extra beats.

## Slide 12

- Sinus Arrhythmia
  - Sinus Arrhythmia Overview
    - Definition: Variation of normal sinus rhythm with an irregular rate
    - Diagnostic Criterion: Variation in P-P interval by 0.12 seconds
  - Types of Sinus Arrhythmia
    - 1.  Phasic: Rate changes with the respiratory cycle (increases on inspiration, decreases on expiration)
    - 2. Nonphasic: Not related to the respiratory cycle
    - 3. Ventriculophasic: Seen in 3rd-degree AV block and after a compensatory pause following a PVC; P-P interval including a QRS is shorter than one excluding a QRS

## Slide 13

  - Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia
    - Nature: Common, benign, and usually normal phenomenon
  - Mechanism
    - Autonomic Input: Changes during respiration
    - Inspiration: Inhibits vagal tone, increasing sinus rate
    - Expiration: Vagal tone rises, and sinus rate declines
  - Associations
    - Seen in: Young, healthy individuals
    - Associated with: Obesity, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension
  - Clinical Significance
    - Mostly Benign: Does not require additional cardiac evaluation

## Slide 15

  - What is a PAC?
    - A PAC is an early heartbeat that starts in the atria instead of the SA node.
    - Think of it as the atria “jumping the gun” before the next normal beat
  - Where it comes from?
    - Ectopic pacemaker: A group of atrial cells (not the SA node) fires early.
    - These impulses can come from anywhere in the atria.
  - How It Looks on an EKG?
    - The P wave appears earlier than expected.
    - The P wave shape looks different from normal (since it’s coming from a new spot).
    - The QRS that follows is usually normal because the ventricles still depolarize normally.
  - What Happens After the PAC?
    - If the AV node is ready, the PAC conducts normally → followed by a short pause.
    - If the AV node is still refractory, the PAC doesn’t conduct → missing QRS (a “nonconducted PAC”).
  - When They Occur
    - PACs can happen randomly (sporadic) or in regular patterns (every 2nd or 3rd beat).
    - Common triggers: stress, caffeine, alcohol, fatigue.
    - Usually benign, but frequent PACs can sometimes precede atrial fibrillation.

    - Definition: Rhythm where the SA node rate is less than 60 beats per minute
  - Occurrence in Healthy Individuals

## Slide 36

- First-Degree AV Block
  - First-Degree AV Block
    - Definition: Delayed conduction from the atrium to the ventricle
    - PR Interval: Prolonged >200 ms
  - Conduction Delay Location
    - AV Node: Most common site
    - His-Purkinje System: Possible location

## Slide 37

  - First-Degree AV Block in Normal Hearts
    - Common in: Athletes with lower resting heart rate and increased vagal tone
  - Other Common Causes
    - Structural Diseases: AV node issues or age-related degeneration
    - Myocarditis
    - Acute MI: Most commonly inferior wall MI
    - Medications: Digoxin, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers
  - Additional Causes
    - Inflammatory Conditions: Lyme disease
    - Dilated Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 40

  - Wenckebach’s Block
    - Progressive Prolongation: AV conduction and PR interval gradually lengthen
    - Complete Blockage: Eventually, an atrial impulse is blocked, and no QRS complex appears
  - Conduction Ratio
    - Fixed or Varied: Number of completed QRS complexes to dropped QRS complexes can be fixed or varied
  - Typical Location
    - Level: Usually occurs at the AV node
- Second-Degree AV Block: Mobitz type I

## Slide 41

  - Mechanism of the Block
    - Prolongation: Each depolarization prolongs the AV node's refractory period
    - Slowed Conduction: Subsequent atrial impulses encounter a longer refractory period, slowing conduction
  - Progressive Blockage
    - Absolute Refractory Period: An atrial impulse eventually arrives during this period, blocking conduction
    - Pause: Allows AV node to recover
  - Grouped Beating Pattern
    - Cycle Repeats: After recovery, the cycle of conduction and blockage repeats, creating a "grouped beating" pattern
- Second-Degree AV Block: Mobitz type I
- Pathophysiology

  - Overview of Block
    - Nature: Often transient, most patients are asymptomatic
  - Impact on Cardiac Output

  - Patient Presentation
    - Bradycardia: May be present, but some may have a normal rate
    - Appearance: Patients may appear pale or diaphoretic due to reduced cardiac output
  - Symptoms in Heart Failure Patients
    - Exacerbation: Heart failure worsened by heart block
    - Signs: Crackles, JVD, peripheral edema

## Slide 52

- Third-Degree AV Block
  - Complete Heart Block
    - Definition: Absence of AV nodal conduction; complete dissociation between atria and ventricles
  - Escape Pacemaker
      - QRS Complex: Represents ventricular pacing
      - Rate: Slower than atrial rate (P wave)
  - Junctional Escape
    - Location: Block at or above AV node
    - QRS Complex: Narrow
    - Rate: 40-60 bpm
  - Ventricular Escape
    - Location: Block below AV node
    - QRS Complex: Wide
    - Rate: 20-40 bpm

## Slide 53

      - Cardiomyopathy
      - Myocarditis

## Slide 75

  - Reentry Mechanism
    - Definition: Most common cause of narrow QRS complex tachycardia
    - Process: A propagating impulse fails to die out and re-excites the heart after the refractory period
  - Causes of Reentry
    - Anatomic Obstacle: Fibrosis creating a circular pathway
    - Functional Abnormalities: Electrophysiologic issues
  - Types of Enhanced Automaticity
    - Sinus Tachycardia: Enhanced normal automaticity
    - Ectopic Atrial/Junctional Tachycardia: Abnormal automaticity
  - Triggered Activity
    - Underlying Arrhythmias: Seen in conditions like digoxin toxicity and arrhythmias due to acute ischemia
- Mechanisms of Supraventricular Tachycardias

## Slide 76

- Sinus Tachycardia
  - Definition
    - Heart Rate: Faster than 100 bpm (often 100-160 bpm)
    - Cause: Rapid impulse formation from the SA node
  - Normal Physiologic Response
    - Triggers: Exercise, increased catecholamine release
    - Onset/Termination: Gradual, unlike PSVT due to reentry
  - Tachycardia can also be seen in these Conditions:
    - Fever
    - Hypovolemia
    - Hypotension
    - Sepsis
    - Anemia
    - Hypoxia
    - Pulmonary embolism
    - Acute MI
    - Anxiety
    - Hyperthyroidism
- Triggers: Stimulants, withdrawal from substances (beta blockers, alcohol, opioids, digoxin, benzos)

## Slide 77

  - Sinus Tachycardia does not directly cause symptoms, however some patients may report Palpitations
  - Impact on Cardiac Patients
    - Symptoms:
      - Dyspnea
