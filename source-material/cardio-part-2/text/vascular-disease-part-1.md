## Slide 4

- PAD
- Atherosclerosis of the lower extremities
  - Can involve upper extremities too
  - Subintimal accumulation of lipids and fibrous material that can:
    - Narrow vessel lumen (stenosis)
    - Plaque can rupture and cause embolism (occlusion)
- Higher incidence in MEN, and African American
  - Increases with age, can affect women as well
- Patients may describe this as “poor circulation”

  - Diabetes mellitus
  - Hyperlipidemia
  - Hypertension
  - Age
  - FH
- Same risk factors for cardiovascular disease
- Presence of PAD confers high 10-year Cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk

## Slide 6

- PAD-Intermittent Claudication
- Defined as a pain (ache or cramp), numbness, or a sense of weakness in leg muscles.
  - It occurs during exercise and is relieved by rest
    - The supply of blood may be adequate to meet the demands of inactive muscle, therefore no pain at rest
    - Due to atherosclerosis, a mismatch develops between the supply of blood and increased demand induced by activity.
    - Site of claudication symptoms will be distal to location of lesion

## Slide 20

- PAD: Diagnosis with ABIs
- Based on clinical suspicion and Ankle-Brachial Index can be done at the bedside
  - Calculation of the ankle-brachial index (ABI) is simple and inexpensive method to confirm the clinical suspicion of lower extremity arterial occlusive disease.
  - The resting systolic blood pressure at the ankle is compared with the systolic brachial pressure and the ratio of the two pressures defines the ankle-brachial (or ankle-arm) index.
  - This index provides a measure of the severity of disease.

## Slide 21

- ABI
- Calculation of the ankle-brachial index (ABI) at the bedside is usually performed with a Continuous-wave Doppler probe

## Slide 22

- Obtaining an ABI
- The patients must rest for 15 to 30 minutes prior to measuring the ankle pressure.
- To obtain the ABI, place a blood pressure cuff just above the ankle. While listening to either the dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial artery signal with a continuous wave Doppler, insufflate the cuff to a pressure above which the audible Doppler signal disappears. Slowly release the pressure in the cuff just until the pedal signal returns and record this systolic pressure. Repeat the measurement process in the contralateral lower extremity.
- Measure the systolic brachial artery pressure bilaterally in a similar fashion with the blood pressure cuff placed around the upper arm and using the continuous wave Doppler.
- The ABI for each lower extremity is calculated by dividing the ankle pressure in each lower extremity by the brachial artery systolic pressures.

## Slide 23

- Classification of PAD based on ABI
| ABI Value | Interpretation | Clinical Recommendation |
| >1.4 | Calcification/Vessel Hardening (Non-compressible arteries) | Refer to Vascular |
| 1.0-1.4 | Normal | None |
| 0.91 - 0.99 | Acceptable/Borderline | None |
| ≤0.90 | Abnormal - PAD | Treat lifestyle risk factors, medical therapy, Refer to Vascular |
- ABI confirms the presence of PAD but does not independently determine clinical severity of the need for revascularizing

## Slide 28

- So how do we use all these studies to diagnose PAD?
- History and physical exam + resting ABIs
    - If ABI is ≤0.90  this confirms PAD
      - Consider referral to vascular
        - Advanced imaging (duplex US, CTA, MRA or catheter angiography) is generally reserved for patients being considered for revascularization
        - Suspect limb-threatening ischemia requires emergent referral
- Claudication or exertional leg symptoms with normal or borderline ABI (0.91-1.40) obtain Exercise treadmill testing
- If ABI is inconclusive ( >1.4) or diagnosis is uncertain toe-brachial index (TBI) can be ordered.
    - If diagnosis remains uncertain additional vascular testing may be considered.

## Slide 29

- When to choose alternative imaging
- Arterial Duplex US can be used:
  - If ABI uncertain
  - Exercise testing is positive, more advanced imaging can be considered
  - ABI confirms PAD and arterial localization is needed
  - Evaluating suspected restenosis or bypass graft stenosis
  - When iodinated contrast should be avoided
  - If serial vascular surveillance is needed
- CTA with contrast when:
  - Detailed arterial mapping is needed for revascularization
    - Vascular surgeon needs lesion length, distribution and detailed mapping of anatomy
  - Multilevel or aortoiliac disease is suspected
  - Duplex ultrasound is technically limited or inconclusive

## Slide 32

- Clinical Categories of PAD
- Asymptomatic PAD:
  - Objective evidence of PAD without recognized leg symptoms
  - Focus: Cardiovascular risk reduction
- 2. Chronic Symptomatic PAD
  - Claudication or other chronic exertional leg symptoms
  - Focus: Risk reduction and symptom relief
- Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia
  - Ischemia rest pain, nonhealing wounds, or gangrene, typically lasting more than 2 weeks.
  - Focus: Urgent vascular evaluation and limb preservation
- 4. Acute Limb Ischemia
- Sudden reduction in limb perfusion threatening limb viability
- Focus: Immediate emergency vascular evaluation

## Slide 33

- PAD: Primary Care Management
- Goal: Reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and major adverse limb events (MALE), improve symptoms and functional status, and preserve limb function.
- Cornerstone of therapy is treatment of risk factors for atherosclerosis, lifestyle modification, and antiplatelet therapy.
  - HTN and DM control
  - LDL to goal using high intensity statin therapy
  - Smoking cessation
  - Healthy diet
  - Weight loss if obese
  - Structured exercise therapy improves symptoms

  - Option for symptomatic and asymptomatic PAD
  - Decreases risk of cardiovascular event

  - Reduces risk of cardiovascular event and limb events

  - Consider bleeding risk before

  - Improves walking distance and claudication
  - Maybe initiated alongside structured exercise therapy

## Slide 50

- Gangrene
- Tissue that has inadequate blood supply and is undergoing necrosis
- Types:
- Dry gangrene
  - Risk factors are PAD
  - Other disease processes independent of PAD cause this as well
- Wet gangrene
  - Tissue necrosis complicated by bacterial infection
  - May develop in previously ischemic tissue
- Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis)
  - Rapidly progressive, toxin-mediated infection of skeletal muscle

## Slide 51

- Dry Gangrene
- Sterile ischemic necrosis
  - Results from prolonged, severe reduction in arterial blood flow.  (impaired blood flow)
  - Tissue death occurs WITHOUT superimposed infection
- Etiology:
  - PAD
  - Vasculitis
  - Cold related injuries (frostbite)

## Slide 52

- Dry GangreneClinical Presentation
- Dry, black eschar, which most commonly begins distally at the extremities
- There is a clear demarcation between healthy tissue and necrotic tissue
  - Autoamputation will eventually occur if not surgically removed

## Slide 59

- Gas Gangrene (Clostridial Myonecrosis)
- Pathophysiology:
  - Rapidly progressive, toxin-mediated necrosis of skeletal muscle
  - Most commonly caused by Clostridium perfringens
  - Bacterial toxins cause muscle destruction, vascular injury, and systemic toxicity
  - May progress to shock and multiorgan failure
- Risk Factors:
  - Deep traumatic wounds or contaminated injuries
  - Recent surgery, Particularly GI/GU
  - Injection drug use
  - Spontaneous infection

## Slide 60

- Gas Gangrene: Signs & Symptoms
- Early Presentation:
  - Severe pain out of proportion to examination
  - Rapid progressive swelling and pain
  - Skin may initially appear normal or pale
  - Tachycardia and systemic toxicity may develop early
- Progression/Late Presentation:
  - Bronze, purple, or dusky skin discoloration
  - Bullae, sometimes hemorrhagic
  - Crepitus from gas
  - Thin, serosanguinous drainage(“dish water” appearance) with possible foul odor
  - Hypotension, shock, multiorgan dysfunction

## Slide 62

- Wet and Gas Gangrene: Diagnosis
- Clinical diagnosis…urgent surgical consolation
  - Severe pain, rapid progression, tissue necrosis
- Lab evaluation:
  - CBC, CMP, lactate, CRP/ESR
  - Blood cultures and deep tissue gram stain/culture when feasible
  - Normal labs due not exclude
- Imaging:
  - X-ray: may demonstrate soft-tissue gas
  - CT: may show gas and extend of tissue involvement
  - CTA: done if arterial perfusion assessment is indicated
- Surgical exploration must not be delayed.

## Slide 63

- Clostridium myonecrosis : Tx
- Immediate surgical exploration and debridement
- Immediate IV ABX
  - Empiric coverage initially: Vanc + Zosyn + Clindamycin initially
    - MRSA, broad gram-pos, gram-neg, and anaerobic coverage
    - (Clindamycin: inhibits bacterial protein synthesis and helps suppress clostridial toxin production
  - Once confirmed: IV pen G + clindamycin
- Supportive management:
  - IV fluid resuscitation
  - Tetanus prophylaxis if indicated

## Slide 64

- Gas Gangrene “ Clinical Pearls”
- Clostridial infection should be considered in patients presenting with low-grade fever, tachycardia out of proportion to fever, and pain out of proportion to physical exam findings with recent surgery or trauma
- Shock and multi-organ failure may be rapidly progressive.  Mortality is 80-90% if untreated, 10-25% when treated appropriately
- Clindamycin may improve survival by inhibiting toxin production

## Slide 69

- Aneurysm
- Definition: Dilation of the arterial wall to ≥1.5 times its normal diameter
- Classifications:
  - True aneurysms: Dilation involving all layers of the vessel
    - Wall is intact
  - Pseudoaneurysms (false aneurysm): disruption of the arterial wall with blood contained by surrounding tissues.
    - Develops usually at a site of vessel catheterization, trauma or anastomoses
  - Mycotic aneurysm: dilation that develops because of infection in the vessel wall causing a weakness

## Slide 79

- Aortic Aneurysm: Epidemiology
- Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm occurs in 3-8% affecting predominantly men:
  - White men develop AAA more than double the rate of black men
  - Incidence rises sharply after 60 y/o
  - Death from rupture is about 5-10K a year
- Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm-about 5-10 cases per 100K

## Slide 80

- Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms: Mortality
- Risk of rupture increases with aneurysm diameter:
  - Small AAA (3.0–4.9 cm): generally low annual rupture risk
  - Large AAA (≥5.5 cm): substantially increased rupture risk
  - Risk also influenced by female sex, rapid expansion, and symptoms
- Growth can be variable but approximately 1-4 mm per year
- Other complications:
  - Formation of mural thrombi can also develop leading to emboli
  - Compression of adjacent structures…not as common

## Slide 83

- AAA: Clinical presentation
- As aneurysms expand, they can become symptomatic

    - A mural (wall) thrombus may form and embolize resulting in lower extremity ischemia or mesenteric ischemia

  - Not abnormal in normal THIN individuals to feel Aorta but NOT below the umbilicus

- Vasculitis
- Inflammation of blood vessels (arteries, veins)
  - Arterial inflammation  Arteritis
  - Venous inflammation  Phlebitis
- Pathophysiology:
- Inflammatory cells infiltrate and damage vessel wall, compromising vascular integrity.
  - Leads to:
    - Vessel narrowing or occlusion
    - Downstream ischemia
    - Vessel wall weakening – leads to aneurysms formation
    - Vessel rupture – hemorrhage

## Slide 118

  - Immune-mediated inflammation w/o underlying systemic dx
  - Examples: ANCA-associated vasculitis, giant cell artertisi, Takayasu arteritis
  - Buerger disease-idiopathic
- Secondary vasculitis:
  - Associated with systemic autoimmune disorders (SLE, RA)
  - Underlying infection (Hep B/C, HIV or bacterial)
  - Medications (allopurinol, minocycline)
  - Malignancy
