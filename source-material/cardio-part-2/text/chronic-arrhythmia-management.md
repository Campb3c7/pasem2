## Slide 2

- NSVT in the Hospital
- “Say, aren’t you the PA for Dr. Never-answers-his-phone?  His patient in room 3423 just had a 12-beat run of VT”.
- Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia is extremely common when we are monitoring so many patients on telemetry.
- NSVT is a potential marker for the development of a sustained ventricular dysrhythmia and should always be investigated.

## Slide 3

- NSVT in the Hospital
- First, look at the telemetry strip- artifact may look like NSVT.  Compare it to the patient’s 12-lead.
- Patients are usually asymptomatic, but the clinician will want to ask about CP, dyspnea, palpitations and syncope/pre-syncope symptoms.
- The patient’s physical exam is generally normal, with normal sinus rhythm on the telemetry monitor by the time you arrive to assess them.

## Slide 4

- NSVT in the Hospital
- Reversible causes of NSVT are evaluated.  How?
  - Electrolyte derangements: potassium and magnesium are the most common
  - Anemia
  - Myocardial ischemia: obtain an immediate 12 lead EKG and a HS troponin done now and in 6 hours in all patients.
  - Hypoxia
  - Hypotension
  - Heart failure decompensation
  - Adverse drug effect

## Slide 5

- NSVT in the Hospital
- For a single episode of NSVT, the 12 lead EKG, troponins and evaluation of potentially reversible causes is all that is needed.
- In a patient with recurrent episodes of NSVT or one who is highly symptomatic, additional work-up is appropriate.
  - Start with a 2D echo- generally the study of choice to evaluate cardiac fxn and structural heart disease.
  - Stress testing may be done if onset of ventricular tachycardia seems to correspond to exercise.

## Slide 6

- Other Arrhythmia Issues- Sinus Tach
- Sinus tachycardia is a physiologic response to stress, that may be seen in many illnesses.
- Patients may complain of palpitations, but the majority of symptoms are usually related to the underlying illness that caused sinus tachycardia.
- When persistent sinus tachycardia is noted with an apparent underlying cause, treatment is aimed at correction of that cause.

## Slide 7

- Inappropriate Sinus Tachycardia
- Also called chronic non-paroxysmal sinus tach, is a diagnosis of exclusion in persons without structural heart disease or other cause for sinus tachycardia.
- These patients frequently have an exaggerated HR response to exercise, which is out of proportion to the physiologic needs of the body.

## Slide 8

- Inappropriate Sinus Tachycardia
- Most of the patients with inappropriate sinus tachycardia are young and female.
- The pathophysiologic mechanism is thought to be hyperactivity of the SA node, depressed vagal response and beta-adregenergic hypersensitivity.
- Is this really dangerous?

## Slide 9

- Inappropriate Sinus Tachycardia
- The first step in the evaluation of the patient is to confirm that sinus tachycardia- not atrial tach- is present with a 12 lead EKG.
- Sinus tachycardia usually occurs in response to patient stress or illness, whereas atrial tachycardia is paroxysmal and often unrelated to events.
- Atrial tachycardia usually occurs with a very high heart rate, often > 140 bpm.

## Slide 10

- Inappropriate Sinus Tachycardia
- If no reversible cause can be found for the sinus tachycardia, then it is reasonable to consult cardiology and obtain a 2D echo.
- Inappropriate sinus tachycardia is generally treated with low dose metoprolol.  Ivabridine, an antiarrhythmic, is more efficacious (although off label in the US) for inappropriate sinus tachycardia.

## Slide 11

- POTS
- Postural tachycardia syndrome- patients have an exaggerated HR response to standing, but an intact autonomic reflex in the baroreceptors.
- This means that the patient’s heart races when they stand up suddenly, but they vasoconstrict appropriately to the postural change.
- So, rarely does a patient with POTS have syncope- but often have they have orthostatic hypotension and pre-syncope symptoms.

## Slide 12

- Postural Tachycardia Syndrome-POTS
- POTS is most common in adolescent and young adult women.
- There are multiple proposed mechanisms for POTS- genetics, hypovolemia, cardiac deconditioning, distal extremity denervation, and decreased venous return- but there is no definitive pathophysiology that is common to all patients.

## Slide 13

- POTS
- Patients diagnosed the postural tachycardia syndrome have variable symptoms in response to position changes:
  - CNS: dizziness, weakness, lightheadedness, blurred vision and fatigue with standing
  - Orthostatic: palpitations, tremors, anxiety
  - GI: nausea, abdominal cramps, constipation/ diarrhea.
- In general, they don’t feel well everyday.

- POTS

  - Maintain good oral rehydration

## Slide 16

- Atrial Dysrhythmias
- The major atrial dysrhythmias include atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter and multifocal atrial tachycardia.
- Many times, atrial flutter and MAT management is similar to that of atrial fibrillation.
- The risk factors, epidemiology and symptoms are also very similar.

## Slide 17

- Atrial Flutter
- Atrial Flutter Characteristics
  - Rapid, regular narrow complex tachycardia
  - Common AV Block Ratios:
    - 2:1 block: Ventricular rate of 150 bpm
    - 3:1 block: Ventricular rate of 100 bpm
- Atrial depolarizations have:
    - A single morphology with downward deflection in a ”sawtooth" pattern.
    - Best Seen: In inferior leads and V1

## Slide 24

- EKG Features of Atrial Fibrillation
- Rhythm: "Irregularly irregular”
- P Waves are absent, and the rhythm has a flat or chaotic isoelectric baseline.
- QRS Complexes: Narrow, unless the patient has a bundle branch block or preexcitation syndrome.
- The heart rate is variable.  RVR will be a rate of greater than 100, we treat to control the rate when above 110 bpm.

- Dilated cardiomyopathy
- Arteriosclerotic heart disease (CAD)
- Genetics
- Pulmonary disease (COPD)
- Severe anemia
- Hypertension
- Diabetes
- Chronic kidney disease
- Obesity

## Slide 27

- Epidemiology of AF
- AF affects 4.48% of the US population and rates are increasing!  It is the most common arrhythmia
- AF prevalence increases with age: 12% of people older than 80 years.
- AF is more prevalent in women than men, but men are more likely to develop atrial fibrillation.
- Atrial fib is also more common in persons of European descent than in Blacks.

## Slide 28

- Pathophysiology of AF
- The mechanism by which other CV risk factors cause an increased risk of AF is poorly understood.
  - Catecholamine excess, insulin resistance, atrial ischemia/inflammation, hemodynamic stress
- Many theories of AF initiation/ propagation exist, and the research is not yet clear.
- What we do know is that AF requires a trigger and a permissive atrial substrate- atrial tissue that will “let it happen”.

## Slide 32

- Pathophysiology of AF
- In atrial fibrillation, the AV node is frequently bombarded with up to 400 impulses/minute and therefore conducts electrical impulses irregularly and rapidly to the ventricles.
- Because conduction follows the normal conduction system pathways, the QRS duration remains normal.
    - Except in cases of aberrant conduction when the ventricular system is diseased, like in a BBB.

- Thus, atrial fibrillation is a self promoting process.

## Slide 35

- Classification of AF
- Atrial fibrillation that is unrelated to a correctable cause can be classified as:
- Paroxysmal atrial fibrillation: AF that is self-terminating or intermittent lasting less than 7 days, often lasting less than 24 hours.  This is  abbreviated pAF.
- Persistent AF: if AF fails to self terminate within 7 days.  Persistent AF requires pharmacologic or electrical cardioversion.

> Notes: A patient who met the criteria for persistent atrial fibrillation but becomes paroxysmal with medication will still be classified as persistent since the outcomes are similar to persistent.

## Slide 36

- Classification of AF
- Long-standing persistent AF fails to terminate on its own by 1 year.
- Permanent AF: atrial fibrillation that lasts > 1 year and is accepted- no further treatments are attempted to restore or maintain normal sinus rhythm.
- Lone AF: atrial fibrillation of any type in young individuals (under 60 years of age) in the absence of any structural heart disease or risk factors.

## Slide 37

- Classification of AF- Lone AF
- Lone AF usually occurs in young male patients and may be associated with a specific trigger.
- Greater than one third of persons with lone AF have a family history of atrial fibrillation, which suggests an inherited component.
  - These patients may have more muscle sleeves and a greater number of arrhythmogenic foci in the pulmonary veins.
  - There may be other physiologic predisposing factors which have yet to be discovered.

## Slide 41

- Making the Diagnosis
- The diagnosis may be suspected on clinical grounds but is made by seeing atrial fibrillation or flutter on the 12-lead EKG.

  - LVH
  - Evidence of prior MI
  - Conduction system disease
  - Establishes a baseline QTc interval

## Slide 54

- Inpatient Treatment of New Onset AF
- When treating a patient with new onset AF who doesn’t convert spontaneously, two important questions must be answered during the hospital stay:
- How will we manage the atrial fibrillation? Rhythm control vs rate control strategy.
- What is the embolization risk and what will be done to minimize it?

## Slide 55

- Long Term Management of New Onset AF
- The rate control strategy uses medications to control the person’s resting heart rate to an optimal 80 bpm.
- This strategy is generally recommended for frail persons 80 and older, since rate controlling  meds have fewer drug-drug interactions and side effects than antiarrhythmics.
- Medications used are those that block the AV node: BB or non-DHP CCB are first line for this.

## Slide 56

- Rate Control of AF
- Patients with acutely decompensated systolic heart failure, who cannot tolerate these first line agents, may be treated with amiodarone or digoxin initially.
  - Caution because amiodarone promotes cardioversion.
- [picture]
- In addition, before using a BB or CCB, a clinician MUST exclude the presence of a pre-excitation syndrome in AF!  If we block down the AV node, we will promote tachyarrhythmias via an accessory pathway.

## Slide 57

- Treatment of New Onset AF
- The rhythm control strategy attempts to convert AF to NSR and uses antiarrhythmic medications to keep the patient in NSR.
- Initially, cardioversion is used in an attempt to re-establish normal sinus rhythm.
- Ideally, a patient is loaded with an antiarrhythmic medication and taken to the cardiac lab for cardioversion under controlled circumstances.

## Slide 58

- Cardioversion Procedure
- Electrolytes (especially K) and O2 sats are normalized, and the pt is npo for at least 6 hours.
- If the patient is on an antiarrhythmic, this is at therapeutic levels.
- Conscious sedation is used while vitals, O2 sat and capnography are monitored.
- An electrical shock using 120-200j is delivered from a biphasic defibrillator. (preferred)

## Slide 59

- Cardioversion Procedure
- How does this happen for a patient?
  - The patient is admitted to IM and a cardiology consult is placed.  The patient is kept npo at midnight.
    - Rate control and AC if appropriate.
  - The cardiology service evaluates the patient to determine if they are an appropriate candidate to undergo a cardioversion.

## Slide 60

- Cardioversion Procedure
- If the cardiologist and the patient agree to move forward, the patient is taken to the cardiac lab under controlled conditions for a TEE and if no LA thrombus is seen, a cardioversion.
- [picture]
- The patient is then maintained on antiarrhythmic medications for rhythm control, such as sotalol, amiodarone or dronedarone.

## Slide 61

- To Shock or Not To Shock:New Onset AF
- Cardioversion is highly recommended in most younger patients with new onset AF of short duration- success rate is 90%
- In most patients, ventricular function improves almost immediately after cardioversion, but atrial function takes longer to recover.
- Anticoagulation is continued after cardioversion, and the patient follows up with cardiology as an outpatient.

## Slide 62

- Rhythm vs Rate Control
- Although antiarrhythmics have multiple DDI and side effects, at this point, most persons with new onset atrial fibrillation undergo treatments to try to re-establish NSR.
- There is done to avoid the cardiomyopathies caused by the remodeling of atrial fibrillation.
- Only in very frail, elderly persons is a rate control strategy pursued for new-onset atrial fibrillation.

## Slide 63

- Why Do We Restore NSR
- AF is an independent predictor of CV mortality.
- Restoration of NSR improves cardiac hemodynamics and exercise tolerance- patients can exercise.
- Maintaining the atrial kick improves the signs and symptoms of HF and overall quality of life.
- Persistent AF contributes to pathologic atrial and ventricular remodeling, so restoration of NSR can slow or, in some cases, reverse atrial dilatation and LV dysfunction.

## Slide 65

- Rhythm Control: Antiarrhythmics
- Class IC antiarrhythmics: propafenone and flecanide.
- These drugs are indicated for patients with AF and supraventricular tachycardia in persons without structural heart disease.
  - Generally, not used in patients with concomitant LV dysfunction and/or CAD.
- Sometimes used as a “pill in the pocket”- the patient takes the med for a few days when they have atrial fibrillation symptoms.

## Slide 66

- Rhythm Control: Anti-Arrhythmics
- Class III antiarrhythmic agents: this class includes amiodarone, sotalol and dofetilide.
- These drugs are used for maintenance of sinus rhythm following cardioversion for atrial fibrillation and for other atrial dysrhythmias.
- All have associated QT interval prolongation and the potential for torsade de pointes- monitor the QTc, potassium and magnesium!

## Slide 67

- Rhythm Control: Anti-ArrhythmicsAmiodarone (Pacerone, Cordarone)
- Amiodarone is considered safer in persistent AF compared to other antiarrhythmic drugs.
- In patients with cardiac disease such as CAD or systolic or diastolic heart failure, amiodarone becomes the drug of choice because of its has less pro-arrhythmic effects compared with other antiarrhythmic drugs.

## Slide 68

- Rhythm Control: Amiodarone
- Amiodarone was found to be more effective than other antiarrhythmics in the treatment of persistent atrial fibrillation.
- Intolerable ADRs are common with amiodarone
  - Thyroid abnormalities: hypothyroid and hyperthyroid
  - Lung fibrosis
  - Liver damage/ cirrhosis
  - Neuropathy, gait ataxia
  - Skin discoloration and corneal deposits

## Slide 69

- Rhythm Control: Sotalol (Betapace)
- The proarrhythmic effect of sotalol is increased in patients with HF, so Sotalol is generally contraindicated in HF patients or persons with a prolonged QT interval.
- Hypokalemia should be corrected and potassium monitored during administration of sotalol because it may exacerbate QT prolongation.
- Sotalol can be used in persons with coronary artery disease.

## Slide 70

- Rhythm Control: Dofetilide (Tikosyn)
- The FDA mandates inpatient cardiac monitoring for dofetilide initiation-so patients need to be hospitalized for 3 days while starting or resuming dofetilide, if more than 2 doses are missed.
  - Even when reloaded with the same dose they had previously tolerated, a significant number of persons were unable to tolerate the same dose due to QT prolongation.
- Dofetilide must be given at the same time twice daily and has many drug drug interactions.

## Slide 71

- Rhythm Control: Dofetilide (Tikosyn)
- Dofetilide is indicated for maintenance of sinus rhythm, and for conversion of AF to sinus rhythm (~50%) in patients with persistent atrial fib.
- Dofetilide has no effect on cardiac output, cardiac index, stroke volume index, or systemic vascular resistance in patients with V tach, mild to moderate HF, angina, and either normal or reduced LVEF- making dofetilide a fairly safe antiarrhythmic in persons with HFrEF and CAD.

## Slide 72

- Rhythm Control: Dronedarone (Multaq):
- Dronedarone (Multaq) is a miscellaneous agent, having properties of all 4 anti-arrhythmic classes.
- Dronedarone is for patients with paroxysmal AF or after conversion of persistent AF in order to maintain sinus rhythm.
- Dronedarone can be used in persons who have risk factors for CAD.

## Slide 73

- Rhythm Control: Dronedarone (Multaq):
- Contraindications to dronedarone are:
  - Permanent atrial fibrillation: studies found increased mortality in persons with permanent atrial fibrillation
  - Heart failure: persons taking dronedarone had a 2-fold increase in the risk of death, stroke and hospitalization for HF.

## Slide 74

- Common DDI’s of Antiarrhythmics
- There are several commonly used drugs that have significant interactions with the antiarrhythmics:
  - Other antiarrhythmics: these drugs must be washed out then a new one loaded
  - Haldol, Seroquel and other antipsychotics
  - Zofran and for some phenergan
  - Macrolide antibiotics: azithromycin, clarithro
  - Quinolone antibiotics: cipro, levaquin
  - Azole antifungals
  - HAART for HIV

## Slide 75

- Long-Term Treatment of AF
- A patient who is successfully cardioverted and remains in NSR must still be prescribed AC or ASA if indicated, because they may relapse at any time.
- Cardioversion may be used multiple times over several years for a patient with a history of atrial fibrillation who relapses.
- Cardiology will frequently adjust the antiarrhythmic regimen before repeating the cardioversion.

## Slide 76

- Other Options for Rhythm Control
- If a patient has had multiple cardioversions which have failed or they continue to relapse despite compliance with multiple antiarrhythmics, then they may be candidates for additional treatments to maintain sinus rhythm.
- These include:
  - Catheter ablation: done when patients are failing cardioversion after multiple attempts.
  - Maze procedure: done when the patient is having cardiac surgery for another reason.

## Slide 88

- Difficult Atrial Fibrillation
- Sometimes a patient is not a candidate for one of the rhythm control strategies and either has episodes of tachycardia and bradycardia- likely due to underlying sick-sinus syndrome or has a low blood pressure at baseline.

## Slide 102

- AF and Stroke Risk
- 20 % of all ischemic strokes in the US are believed to be caused by atrial fibrillation, >70,000 per year.
- Patients with atrial fibrillation have 5 times the risk of stroke compared to persons in NSR.
- Strokes attributable to atrial fibrillation are more likely to be larger and are more likely to undergo hemorrhagic conversion than strokes that are not attributable to AF.

## Slide 103

- AF and Stroke
- The rate of embolic stroke is 5-6% per year in patients with AF and is > 10% per year if concomitant rheumatic valvular disease exists.
- Stroke risk is higher in women compared to men with atrial fibrillation.
- The rate of stroke is significantly increased with increasing age.
  - 7.3% in pts 60-69, but 30.8% in patients 80=89 years

## Slide 104

- AF and Stroke
- The rate of stroke is no different in patients with paroxysmal or permanent atrial fibrillation
- Therefore, any patient with persistent, paroxysmal or permanent AF should be on stroke prophylaxis, unless there is a direct contraindication to this prophylactic medication.
- Each person is considered on an individual basis by calculating their CHADS2-VASc score.
