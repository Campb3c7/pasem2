## Slide 2

- Acute Coronary Syndromes
- Despite optimal medical treatment, a patient with CAD may have an acute coronary syndrome event at any time.
- Patients also frequently stop or are non-compliant with medical therapy- increasing their risk of an ACS event.

## Slide 3

- ACS Definitions
- [picture]
- Myocardial Ischemia
  - Inadequate oxygen supply relative to myocardial demand
  - May produce ST depression, T-wave inversion, or other ischemic changes.
- Myocardial Injury
  - Myocardial damage associated with elevated cardiac troponin.
- Myocardial Infarction
  - Irreversible injury to the myocardium
  - Requires evidence of acute myocardial injury AND acute myocardial ischemia
  - Suggested by EKG changes: ST elevation,  Q waves

## Slide 17

- Initial Therapeutics for Suspected ACS
- Initiation of immediate therapy for patients with chest pain due to suspected ACS, often done by EMS or before any work-up is performed.
- Aspirin 325 mg x1 is given-chewed/ swallowed to all possible and definite ACS patients.
- Nitroglycerin SL or IV may be used in patients having active chest pain with a few exceptions-
  - RV infarct, severe AS or use of PDE-5 inhibitors within 24hrs for erectile dysfxn or pulmonary hypertension.

## Slide 18

- Initial Therapeutics for Suspected ACS
- Patients arrive in the ER and are assessed.
- Patients who continue to have chest pain after treatment with 1-2 sublingual nitro are begun on a nitroglycerin drip.
  - Contraindicated if hypotensive, or RV infarction
- Patients who continue to have chest pain despite nitrates or patients with STEMI are started on beta blockers.
  - CI if HF, risk of cardiogenic shock, bradyarrhythmia

## Slide 19

- Drugs to Avoid for Suspected ACS
- Morphine: try to avoid unless the patient is in severe pain, morphine may interfere with antiplatelet meds.
- Oxygen: only use in patients with sats < 90% due to lack of clinical benefit, there is also the potential risk of hyperoxemia induced coronary artery vasospasm.

## Slide 20

- ACS Diagnostics
- 12 lead EKG - The first EKG may be non-diagnostic. Serial EKGs should be obtained (perform a repeat 20-30 minutes or sooner)
- The usual labs - CBC, chemistry panel, PTT, PT/INR
- BNP is often done to assess for heart failure.
- CXR – evaluates for heart failure or other cause of potential chest pain.

## Slide 21

- ACS Diagnostics
- HS Troponin I
  - Specific to cardiac muscle but remember that a high sensitivity assay will detect troponin in more than half of asymptomatic individuals.
  - Obtain a troponin at the time of arrival and again in 1-2 hours.
  - May be normal or minimally elevated if a patient presents quickly - but what is the delta (change) that occurs between the 1st and 2nd levels?
  - Troponin is chronically elevated in late-stage CKD, ESRD and any heart stress may cause leak.

## Slide 23

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
- Steps of therapy:
  - Anti-anginal therapy
    - Nitroglycerin (SL, IV): 0.4mg SL may be given q5 min x3 or a nitroglycerin gtt may be started at 5-10 mcg/min and titrated to relief of symptoms or drop in BP (keep systolic > 100 mmHg)
    - Beta-blockers: an oral cardioselective BB is begun within 24 hours as long as the patient does not have decompensated HF or hypotension/bradycardia.  IV BB use is limited.

## Slide 24

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
  - Dual Anti-Platelet Therapy (DAPT)
    - Uncoated aspirin 325 mg –chewed or crushed as the initial dose in the ER, then EC ASA 81 mg daily
    - Thienopyridine or P2Y12 inhibitor- for patients going to immediate LHC, decided with/by cardiology.
      - Prasugrel (Effient)
      - Ticagrelor (Brilinta)
      - Clopidogrel (Plavix)

## Slide 25

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
  - Anti-thrombotic therapy:
    - Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)- given as an IV drip titrated to anti-Xa level or PTT if cath planned.
    - LMWH -Enoxaparin (Lovenox) given SQ 1 mg/kg twice daily if no cath is planned.
- These drugs inhibit clot extension by preventing further thrombin activation of platelets and coagulation factors.

## Slide 26

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
  - Risk Stratify
  - Patients with NSTEMI or UA and one or more of the following features are unstable and should proceed to emergent cardiac catheterization.
    - Cardiogenic shock
    - Severe LV dysfunction or florid heart failure
    - Recurrent/persistent rest angina despite intensive medical therapy with nitro and BB
    - New or worsening mitral regurgitation or new VSD
    - Sustained ventricular arrhythmias

## Slide 27

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
- Risk Stratify
  - In stable patients, a TIMI score is calculated to determine if the patient has high risk features, and these pts should proceed to LHC within 24-48 hours.
  - A TIMI score of 3+ is at increased risk:
    - Age >=65 (1 point)
    - 3 or more CAD risk factors (1 point)
    - Known CAD with > 50% stenosis (1 point)
    - Aspirin use in the past 7 days (1 point)
    - Severe angina in the preceding 24 hours (1 point)
    - Elevated cardiac markers (1 point)
    - ST deviation greater than 0.5 mm (1 point)

## Slide 29

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
- After risk stratification, determine the invasive management strategy:
  - 1. Immediate Invasive – Very high-risk patients
    - Immediate coronary angiography (emergent cath).
    - Examples: cardiogenic shock, hemodynamic instability, refractory chest pain despite medical therapy, or life-threatening arrhythmias.

## Slide 30

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
- 2. Routine invasive – intermediate and high-risk patients
  - Plan coronary angiography during the hospitalization, even if symptoms improve with medical therapy.
  - Timing depends on risk:
    - Early: generally within 24 hours for selected high-risk patients.
    - Other patients: later during hospitalization, based on clinical risk and stability.
  - PCI or CABG may be performed depending on coronary anatomy.

## Slide 31

- Management of Unstable Angina/NSTEMI
- 3. Selective invasive – Selected low-risk patients
  - Begin with medical management and further risk assessment.
  - Perform stress testing or coronary CT angiography when appropriate.
  - Proceed to coronary angiography if testing demonstrates significant ischemia or symptoms recur.

## Slide 36

- Management of STEMI
- STEMI is a time-critical emergency that requires rapid restoration of coronary blood flow (reperfusion).
- Most STEMIs result from acute coronary atherothrombosis, typically due to plaque rupture or erosion with thrombus formation. This often causes severe or complete coronary artery occlusion.

## Slide 37

- Management of STEMI
- Time is muscle! Prolonged ischemia causes progressive, irreversible myocardial injury. Necrosis generally begins in the subendocardium and spreads outward over time.
- The goal is to restore coronary perfusion as quickly as possible to minimize infarct size and preserve left ventricular function.

- Management of STEMI
- STEMI is hopefully identified prior to arrival to the hospital by the paramedic, who communicates with the ER attending and may be able to transmit the EKG from the field.

## Slide 40

- Management of STEMI
- The goal of therapy is to OPEN THE CORONARY ARTERY ASAP to RESTORE PERFUSION!!!
  - DOOR to BALLOON time goal is 90 minutes.
  - DOOR to NEEDLE time goal is 30 minutes, to administer thrombolytics if a PCI cannot be performed.
- Primary PCI is preferred when it can be performed within the recommended time frame. PCI generally provides better outcomes, including lower risk of reinfarction and intracranial hemorrhage.

## Slide 41

- STEMI: Reperfusion Therapy
- 1. Primary PCI (preferred strategy)
- Direct presentation to PCI-capable hospital: goal of first medical contact-to-device time ≤90 minutes.
- Transfer from non-PCI hospital: goal of first medical contact-to-device time ≤120 minutes.

## Slide 42

- STEMI: Reperfusion Therapy
- 2. Fibrinolytic therapy (when timely PCI is unavailable)
- Consider if PCI cannot be performed within 120 minutes.
- Patient must be eligible, generally within 12 hours of symptom onset.
- Goal: door-to-needle time ≤30 minutes.
- Transfer to a PCI-capable hospital after administration.

## Slide 43

- Management of STEMI
- An inter-hospital transfer is appropriate if you can get the patient to a PCI capable hospital’s cath lab within 120 minutes.
- The use of thrombolytics is reserved for patients who cannot get to PCI within 2 hours and can be used up to 12 hours after symptom onset.
- Emergent CABG has a substantial mortality risk and reserved for patients who fail PCI or lytics.

## Slide 44

- STEMI: Reperfusion Timing Targets
- The goal is to restore coronary blood flow as quickly as possible.
- Door-to-ECG
- ≤ 10 minutes
- Obtain a 12-lead ECG in patients with suspected ACS.
- Rapid recognition helps activate the STEMI pathway.
- Primary PCI
- ≤ 90 minutes
- First medical contact to device activation for direct presentation to a PCI-capable hospital.
- If transfer is required, target ≤ 120 minutes from first medical contact.
- Fibrinolytic therapy
- ≤ 30 minutes
- Door-to-needle target when timely primary PCI cannot be achieved.
- For eligible STEMI patients, generally within 12 hours of symptom onset; assess contraindications.
- After fibrinolysis, transfer the patient to a PCI-capable hospital for a pharmacoinvasive strategy.
- Starting points differ: ECG = hospital arrival | PCI = first medical contact | Fibrinolysis = hospital arrival

## Slide 45

- Management of STEMI
- Initial intervention: Treat the culprit lesion
- Perform urgent coronary angiography to identify the infarct-related (culprit) artery.
- Perform primary PCI to restore blood flow through the culprit artery as quickly as possible.

## Slide 46

- Management of STEMI
- What if additional obstructive lesions are present?
- In hemodynamically stable patients, complete revascularization is generally recommended when significant nonculprit lesions are present.
- Additional lesions may be treated:
  - During the initial PCI in selected patients, OR
  - During a staged PCI later during hospitalization or within 45 days.
- The decision depends on coronary anatomy, lesion complexity, renal function, and clinical stability.

## Slide 47

- Management of STEMI
- Important exception: Cardiogenic shock
- Prioritize PCI of the culprit artery.
- Do NOT routinely perform PCI of nonculprit arteries during the initial procedure because of increased risk of adverse outcomes.

## Slide 48

## Slide 51

- MINOCA: Myocardial Infarction with Non-Obstructive Coronary Arteries
- Key characteristics:
- Accounts for approximately 5–15% of acute MI presentations, depending on the population studied.
- More common in women and relatively younger patients.
- Defined by no coronary stenosis ≥50% in a major epicardial artery.
- Can present as either STEMI or NSTEMI.
- Initially a working diagnosis requiring further evaluation.

## Slide 52

- MINOCA: Myocardial Infarction with Non-Obstructive Coronary Arteries
- True ischemic MINOCA:
- Coronary vasospasm
- SCAD without obstructive disease
- Coronary embolism or thrombosis
- Coronary microvascular dysfunction
- Plaque rupture or erosion with transient thrombosis or no significant residual obstruction

## Slide 53

- MINOCA: Myocardial Infarction with Non-Obstructive Coronary Arteries
- MINOCA mimics:
- Myocarditis
- Takotsubo (stress-induced cardiomyopathy)
- Pulmonary embolism
- Other causes of myocardial injury

## Slide 55

- Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy (Stress-Induced Cardiomyopathy)
- Causes transient regional left ventricular systolic dysfunction.
- Can mimic ACS with chest pain, EKG changes, and elevated troponin.
- Often associated with emotional or physical stress, although a trigger is not always identified.

## Slide 56

- Vasospastic AnginaAlso called variant angina or Prinzmetal angina
- Patients develop atypical chest pain usually occurring at rest, most commonly from midnight to morning.
- Patients are typically young smokers with few cardiovascular risk factors.  History of cocaine or meth use is an important question to ask.
- EKG recordings show a transient ST segment elevation which resolves after the pain subsides.

## Slide 59

- Stimulant-Associated Cardiac Ischemia
- Cocaine and methamphetamine increase sympathetic activity, causing tachycardia, HTN, and increased myocardial oxygen demand.
- Can cause coronary vasospasm, coronary thrombosis, and acute MI.
- Ask about recent cocaine or methamphetamine use in patients presenting with chest pain.
- Acute management:
- Benzodiazepines to reduce sympathetic stimulation.
- Nitrates for coronary vasospasm.
- Calcium channel blockers may be used when appropriate.

## Slide 60

- Right Ventricular Infarction
- Usually caused by occlusion of the proximal right coronary artery (RCA) or one of its branches.
- Commonly occurs in association with an inferior MI.
- Classic clinical triad:
  - Hypotension – decreased cardiac output.
  - JVD – increased right-sided filling pressures.
  - Clear lungs – typically no pulmonary edema in isolated RV infarction.
- RV dysfunction → decreased blood flow to lungs → decreased LV preload → decreased cardiac output → hypotension.

## Slide 61

- Right Ventricular Infarction
- Remember that the systemic blood pressure is dependent upon preload (blood being delivered to left atria/ventricle).
- If right side fails, preload falls and hypotension results.
- A right ventricular infarction is diagnosed by finding ST-segment elevation in right precordial leads on a right sided 12-lead.
  - OBTAIN a RIGHT SIDED EKG on all patients with an INFERIOR MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION

## Slide 62

- Right Ventricular Infarction
- Standard 12-lead ECG:
- Uses six precordial (chest) leads, V1–V6.
- Primarily evaluates the anterior, septal, and lateral portions of the left ventricle, along with other cardiac electrical views.
- May identify an inferior MI but can miss right ventricular involvement.
- Right-sided ECG:
- Additional ECG leads are placed on the right side of the chest.
- The most important lead is V4R, which directly evaluates the right ventricular free wall.
- Helps identify right ventricular infarction, particularly when an inferior STEMI is present.

## Slide 65

- Right Ventricular Infarction
- In a patient who has a right ventricular infarction the management has some key differences.
- Avoid vasodilators (nitrolglycerin) and diuretics, as these drugs will worsen hypotension.
- To treat hypotension, we will need to expand preload, so give volume- normal saline boluses IV.

## Slide 66

- Recognize RV Infarction
- Inferior STEMI + hypotension + JVD + clear lungs = suspect RV infarction.
  - Obtain a right-sided ECG, particularly lead V4R.
  - Avoid nitrates and diuretics because they decrease preload.
  - Consider cautious IV fluid administration if hypotensive and preload deficient, with frequent reassessment.
  - Urgent reperfusion (usually PCI) is essential.

## Slide 67

- CAD in the Elderly
- 1. Higher prevalence and mortality
  - Approximately 60–65% of MIs occur in patients ≥65 years.
  - Older adults account for the majority of ACS-related deaths.
- 2. Atypical clinical presentation
- More likely to present with NSTEMI than STEMI.
- May present without classic chest pain.
- Possible symptoms include:
  - Dyspnea
  - Syncope
  - Unexplained weakness or fatigue
  - Acute confusion (delirium)
- Atypical presentations may delay recognition and treatment.
- 3. Increased risk of complications
- Greater risk of acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock.
- Increased risk of arrhythmias, bleeding, and acute kidney injury.
- Higher overall mortality following MI.

## Slide 69

- AMI Complications: Functional
- Left ventricular failure: Pulmonary congestion and edema; treat with diuretics when congested and guideline-directed HF therapy (BB, ACEi)
- Right ventricular failure: Hypotension with elevated JVP and clear lungs; cautious IV fluids if preload-dependent; avoid nitrates and diuretics when hypotensive
- Cardiogenic shock: Severe pump failure and tissue hypoperfusion; urgent revascularization is essential

## Slide 70

- AMI Complications: Mechanical
- LV free-wall rupture: Hemopericardium → cardiac tamponade → PEA or sudden death
- Ventricular septal rupture: New harsh holosystolic murmur; left-to-right shunt; oxygen saturation step-up
- Papillary muscle rupture: Acute severe mitral regurgitation → pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock

## Slide 71

- AMI Complications: Electrical
- BRADYARRHYTHMIAS / HEART BLOCK
- Sinus bradycardia and AV block may occur, particularly with inferior MI
- Atropine may be used for symptomatic bradycardia
- Temporary pacing for refractory symptomatic or hemodynamically significant bradycardia
- Permanent pacemaker only for persistent, clinically significant conduction disease after an appropriate waiting period
- TACHYARRHYTHMIAS
- VT/VF: Potentially life-threatening; defibrillation for pulseless VT/VF; synchronized cardioversion for unstable VT with a pulse
- SVT: Treat according to rhythm and hemodynamic stability
- CONDUCTION ABNORMALITIES
- New bundle branch or fascicular block may indicate extensive myocardial injury
- Isolated new bundle branch block does not automatically require a pacemaker

## Slide 77

- Cardiac Rehabilitation
- Cardiac rehab is a medically supervised program to improve CV health in a patient who has had an MI, PTCA with stent, CABG, valve surgery, or HFrEF.
- Participating in cardiac rehab has well-proven benefits including reducing cardiac and all-cause mortality, reducing morbidity and improving quality of life for cardiac patients.
- Despite this, fewer than 30% of eligible patients participate in cardiac rehab in the US.

## Slide 78

- Cardiac Rehabilitation
- Cardiac rehab has three equally important parts:
  - Exercise counseling and training
  - Counseling to reduce stress
  - Education on heart healthy living
- Patients are normally asked to attend 36 sessions over a three-month period, where they partake in supervised exercise sessions and undergo nutritional counseling and receive advice on lipids, diabetes, blood pressure, smoking cessation, and receive psychological support.

## Slide 79

- Barriers to Cardiac Rehabilitation and Solutions
- Cost/coverage: Coverage and out-of-pocket costs vary by insurance plan.
- Access: Transportation, distance, scheduling, and availability can limit participation.
- Patient factors: Lack of awareness, competing responsibilities, and difficulty making lifestyle changes can reduce participation.

## Slide 80

- ACS: Core Secondary Prevention
- Lifestyle modification and patient education
  - Heart-healthy diet, physical activity, medication adherence
- Antiplatelet therapy
  - Aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor (DAPT), generally for 12 months after ACS if bleeding risk is not high
- High-intensity statin therapy
  - Add nonstatin therapy when needed to achieve further LDL-C reduction
- Beta-blocker therapy
  - Particularly important with reduced LVEF, heart failure, angina, or arrhythmias
- ACE inhibitor or ARB
  - Especially with LVEF ≤40%, hypertension, diabetes, or anterior STEMI

## Slide 81

- ACS: Additional Secondary Prevention Measures
- Lipid management: High-intensity statin; add ezetimibe or other nonstatin therapy as needed.
- Cardiac rehabilitation: Referral for supervised exercise and comprehensive risk-factor modification.
- Smoking cessation: Counseling and pharmacologic support when appropriate.
- Weight management and nutrition: Encourage a heart-healthy diet and healthy weight.
- Blood pressure control: Treat hypertension to an individualized goal.
- Diabetes management: Individualize A1c goal; consider GLP-1 receptor agonist or SGLT2 inhibitor with cardiovascular benefit in patients with type 2 diabetes and ASCVD.
- Treat heart failure when present: Guideline-directed medical therapy; diuretics for congestion.
- Anticoagulation when indicated: For atrial fibrillation or LV thrombus—not routinely after every MI.
