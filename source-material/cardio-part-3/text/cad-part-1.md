## Slide 7

- ASCVD Risk Factors
- The five most common modifiable risk factors (HLD, DM, HTN, obesity, and smoking) are estimated to be responsible for more than 50% of cardiovascular mortality.
- Cigarette smoking increases the risk of ASCVD 2-3x.  That risk decreases 50% within 2 years of quitting.
- Even modest exercise lowers the risk of ASCVD substantially.

## Slide 8

- ASCVD Risk Reduction
- Stop tobacco use
  - Assess tobacco exposure, offer counseling, and provide evidence-based cessation treatment.
- Control blood pressure and lipids
  - Identify elevated levels early and use lifestyle measures and medications when indicated.
- Promote physical activity and a heart-healthy diet
  - Encourage sustainable changes rather than an all-or-nothing approach.
- Manage diabetes and other contributing conditions
  - Individualize treatment according to overall cardiovascular risk and comorbidities.

## Slide 9

- Atherosclerosis Pathophysiology
- Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory process involving lipid accumulation and plaque formation within the arterial wall.
- Lipoproteins enter and accumulate in the intima.
- Inflammatory cells are recruited to the vessel wall.
- Lipid-laden cells form an early lesion called a fatty streak.
- Over time, some lesions progress into more complex atherosclerotic plaques.

## Slide 19

- Atherosclerosis Pathophysiology
- As an unruptured plaque ages, it’s inflammation levels gradually decrease, and it becomes progressively more stable with better collagen content in the cap.
- [picture]
- Stable plaques tend to have a dense fibrous cap, minimal lipid accumulation and little inflammation whereas “vulnerable” unstable plaques have thin caps, large lipid cores and dense inflammatory infiltrates.

## Slide 20

- Clinical Consequences
- Plaques that are fairly new with thin fibrous caps are in the process of replacing the collagen fibers in their cap, while active inflammation is present in the lipid pool below the cap.
- Therefore, young plaques are asymptomatic because they don’t block much blood flow, but when they suddenly rupture, they cause acute coronary syndromes, UA/ NSTEMI and STEMI, due to abrupt termination or limitation of blood flow by clot (thrombus) formation.

## Slide 21

- Clinical Consequences
- Older plaques with thick fibrous caps are less likely to rupture but cause narrowing of arterial lumen (stenosis) and limitation of blood flow:
    - Exertional Angina
    - Claudication

## Slide 25

- Positive and Negative Remodeling
- Plaque formation may remodel the coronary artery in one of two ways:
- Positive remodeling occurs when the arterial wall bulges out at the site of the plaque. This is most common and less likely to cause angina initially, though many may change to negative remodeling with plaque progression.
- Negative remodeling occurs when there is no arterial wall dilation, and the plaque narrows the lumen of the blood vessel as it grows.

## Slide 28

- The “Needy” Heart
- The heart has high and continuous metabolic demands.
- Although it makes up only 0.3% of body weight, it accounts for 7% of resting oxygen consumption.
- Coronary blood flow brings oxygen to myocytes and removes waste products such as carbon dioxide, lactic acid, and hydrogen ions.

## Slide 29

- Coronary Blood Flow
- Normal coronary blood flow:
- Blood flow to the myocardium occurs predominantly during diastole.
- During systole, myocardial contraction compresses intramyocardial vessels and reduces blood flow.

## Slide 30

- Coronary Blood Flow
- During exercise:
- Heart rate and contractility increase.
- Myocardial oxygen demand increases.
- Diastole shortens as heart rate rises, reducing the time available for coronary perfusion per beat.

## Slide 31

- Coronary Blood Flow
- How the heart meets demand:
- Normal coronary arteries dilate to increase blood flow.
- With flow-limiting CAD, blood flow may not increase enough to meet demand, resulting in myocardial ischemia.

## Slide 32

- Cardiac Ischemia: Coronary Stenosis
- Coronary atherosclerosis can narrow the coronary arteries and limit blood flow to the myocardium.
- Reduced blood flow limits the heart's ability to increase oxygen delivery when myocardial oxygen demand rises.
- When myocardial oxygen supply cannot meet oxygen demand, myocardial ischemia develops and may cause anginal symptoms.

## Slide 33

- Atherosclerotic coronary disease can cause myocardial ischemia if the fibrous cap over the lipid plaque suddenly ruptures.
- A relatively small plaque, that is not causing significant stenosis at all, can suddenly fissure, especially if the vessel is exposed to significant stress.

## Slide 35

- Cardiac Ischemia
- Cardiac tissue can become ischemic for a variety of reasons- not just ASCVD causing inability of the artery to vasodilate in the setting of increased demand or plaque rupture.
  - Vasospasm- causes Printzmetal’s angina.  Vasospasm may be caused by “twitchy” arterioles or by illicit sympathomimetic drugs creating intense vasoconstriction.
  - Congenital lesions- uncommon
  - Emboli- septic vegetations from IE

## Slide 36

- Cardiac Ischemia
- When myocardial oxygen supply is inadequate, the regional myocardial hypoxia causes anaerobic cellular respiration and production of lactic acid.
- The localized acidosis causes abnormalities in the myocardial relaxation, leading to reduced compliance and contractility and causing regional wall motion abnormalities which are visible on echocardiography.
- Changes of ischemia become visible on the EKG

## Slide 37

- Cardiac Ischemia: Effects on Myocardial Function
- Myocardial stunning
- Occurs after a transient episode of ischemia followed by restoration of blood flow (reperfusion).
- Blood flow has been restored, but myocardial contractility remains temporarily impaired.
- The myocardium is viable; there is no irreversible myocardial injury.
- Recovery may take hours to days, sometimes longer.

## Slide 38

- Cardiac Ischemia: Effects on Myocardial Function
- Myocardial hibernation
- Describes chronically dysfunctional but viable myocardium associated with chronic or repetitive reductions in coronary blood flow.
- The myocardium downregulates its contractile function as an adaptation to reduced oxygen availability.
- Function may improve after coronary blood flow is restored, such as following revascularization.

## Slide 39

- Cardiac Ischemia: Effects on Myocardial Function
- Myocardial infarction
- Prolonged, severe ischemia causes irreversible cardiomyocyte injury and necrosis
- Results in permanent loss of functioning myocardium in the affected region

- Symptoms of CAD
- Coronary artery disease can cause gradual and intermittent symptoms or can be acutely symptomatic.
- When coronary atherosclerosis causes gradual and intermittent symptoms, this is due to progressively less blood perfusing the myocardium as the plaque grows.

## Slide 52

- Exercise Stress Testing
- Exercise Stress Test (Treadmill Stress Test):
- Appropriate for those who are able to exercise adequately and have a resting EKG that is interpretable for ischemia
- Patient is connected to an electrocardiogram (EKG) and a blood pressure monitor. Patient walks on a treadmill while speed and incline gradually increase every 3 minutes – The Bruce Protocol

## Slide 53

- Exercise Stress Testing
- The Duke Treadmill Score - estimates prognosis and stratifies risk following an exercise treadmill ECG.
- Three variables used:
  - Exercise duration (minutes)
  - Maximum ST-segment deviation (mm)
  - Angina index (0 = none; 1 = nonlimiting; 2 = exercise-limiting)
| Score | Risk |
| ≥ +5 | Low |
| −10 to +4 | Intermediate |
| ≤ −11 | High |

## Slide 54

- Exercise Stress Testing
- Clinical implications:
- Low risk: Generally favorable prognosis; additional testing often unnecessary.
- Intermediate risk: Consider additional evaluation with stress imaging or CCTA.
- High risk: Prompt clinical evaluation; consider invasive coronary angiography depending on clinical findings.

## Slide 57

- Pharmacologic Stress Testing
- Pharmacologic agents create cardiac stress in two ways:
- 1. Coronary vasodilators
- Adenosine
- Regadenoson (Lexiscan) – most common
- Dipyridamole (Persantine)
- Dilate coronary arterioles, increasing blood flow in healthy coronary territories more than in territories supplied by significantly stenosed arteries.
- 2. Increased myocardial oxygen demand
- Dobutamine (stress echo)
- Increases heart rate and contractility, raising myocardial oxygen demand to simulate the effects of exercise.

- Who gets which stress test?
- Rule #1: Acute MI/ ongoing cardiac ischemia is a contraindication to stress testing of any type.

- Rule #2: An exercise stress test is generally preferred, however if the patient cannot exercise adequately due to joint disease or deconditioning/ obesity, it will not be able to be done.

## Slide 59

## Slide 61

- Radionucleotide Myocardial Perfusion Imaging (MPI)
- Purpose
- Evaluates myocardial blood flow at rest and during stress.
- Identifies areas of reduced perfusion that may indicate myocardial ischemia.
- Helps assess the extent and severity of perfusion abnormalities.
- When is MPI particularly useful?
- Baseline ECG abnormalities that make exercise ECG interpretation unreliable (e.g., LBBB).
- Patients unable to exercise adequately.
- Patients requiring additional assessment of myocardial perfusion.

## Slide 62

- How Stress Testing With MPI Is Done
- How is the test performed?
- Patient receives exercise or pharmacologic stress.
- A radioactive tracer (radiopharmaceutical) is injected intravenously during stress.
- A gamma camera obtains images of myocardial tracer distribution.
- Rest and stress images are compared to identify perfusion defects.

## Slide 63

- How Stress Testing With MPI Is Done
- Reversible ischemia: areas of darkness in the exercise images (no tracer) but perfused with tracer in the resting images.

## Slide 64

- Stress Testing with MPI
- Pregnancy is the only contraindication to the use of MPI because of the radioactivity in the tracer.
- The stress test is read by a specially trained cardiologist who will report on each section separately, and often will calculate an EF.
- A stress test is positive if there is evidence of myocardial ischemia on ECG or imaging

## Slide 65

- Other Forms of Cardiac Stress Testing
- Stress Echocardiography
- A baseline resting echo is obtained first.  Then, stress is induced with exercise (treadmill or bicycle) or with medications (dobutamine).
- Echocardiography is used to evaluate for wall motion abnormalities induced by ischemia
- Stress echo using dobutamine can also evaluate the viability of hibernating myocardium.

## Slide 67

- The patient has a known history of coronary artery disease and develops worsening symptoms or new symptoms.

## Slide 68

- Cardiac Stress Testing: Inappropriate Pts

## Slide 69

- Cardiac Stress Testing: Inappropriate Pts

## Slide 70

- CCTA (Coronary CT Angiography)
- What it does
- Uses IV contrast to visualize coronary arteries, plaque, and luminal narrowing.
- When it is useful
- Stable chest pain with no previously established CAD, particularly intermediate clinical likelihood.
- Can clarify inconclusive or abnormal stress test results.
- Limitations
- Radiation exposure and iodinated contrast.
- Image quality may be reduced by heavy coronary calcification or irregular heart rhythms.
- May identify incidental findings.

## Slide 73

- For individuals with known moderate or severe coronary calcification, we prefer stress testing over CCTA because calcium can obscure the lumen of coronary arteries, making quantification of stenosis difficult.

## Slide 74

- CAC Scoring
- Noncontrast CT measures calcified plaque burden in the coronary arteries.
- Reported as an Agatston score.
- A score of 0 means no detectable calcified plaque, but does not exclude noncalcified plaque or CAD.
- Used selectively to refine primary-prevention decisions when the need for lipid-lowering treatment is uncertain.
- A higher score indicates greater calcified plaque burden and generally higher ASCVD risk.

## Slide 76

- Coronary Angiography
- What is it?
- Coronary angiography uses contrast dye and fluoroscopy to visualize coronary arteries and identify stenosis or occlusion.
- Invasive coronary angiography is the reference standard for defining coronary anatomy.
- Allows for intervention (e.g., PCI with stent placement) when indicated.

## Slide 77

- 1. Vascular access
- Radial (wrist) or femoral (groin) artery is accessed.
- A small introducer sheath is inserted.
- 2. Catheter advancement
- A catheter is advanced through the artery into the aorta under fluoroscopic guidance.
- For coronary angiography, the catheter is positioned at the openings (ostia) of the coronary arteries.
- 3. Coronary angiography
- Iodinated contrast is injected into the coronary arteries.
- Fluoroscopy captures images showing coronary anatomy and areas of narrowing or blockage.
- Coronary Angiography

## Slide 84

- Coronary angiography can be diagnostic and therapeutic.
- If significant coronary artery narrowing is identified, the cardiologist may perform percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) during the same procedure.
- PCI restores coronary blood flow using balloon angioplasty and, commonly, placement of a coronary stent.
- How PCI works:
- Balloon catheter advanced across the narrowed segment.
- Balloon inflated to compress plaque against the arterial wall and expand the vessel.
- Stent expanded to help maintain vessel patency and restore blood flow.
