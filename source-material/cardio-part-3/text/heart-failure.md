## Slide 14

- Cardiac Pathophysiology
- Cardiac Output:
- The amount of blood the heart can eject each minute
- This is adjusted based on the body’s needs in each moment
- Sleeping: cardiac output ↓
- Exercising: cardiac output ↑
- The ability to ↑ cardiac output when needed is called cardiac reserve

## Slide 15

- Preload: End diastolic pressure on the ventricle walls caused by the EDV
- Stroke Volume: volume of blood ejected by the ventricle per heartbeat

## Slide 22

- 1. Sympathetic Nervous System Activity
- In response to low cardiac output, the SNS releases norepinephrine and epinephrine to increase the following:​
- Cardiac contractility​
- Heart Rate
- Therefore increasing Cardiac Output
- [picture]

## Slide 23

- 1. Sympathetic Nervous System Activity
- Most Immediate compensatory mechanism
- Downsides:
- Increased sympathetic activity → β adrenergic receptor stimulation → tachycardia, vasoconstriction, and cardiac arrythmias
- Tachycardia: ↑ myocardial oxygen demand → ischemia, myocyte damage, ↓ inotropy
  - Overtime worsens Heart Failure
- Vasoconstriction: ↑ systemic vascular resistance (afterload)
  - Overtime worsens Heart Failure (decrease cardiac output) and decreases flow to the kidneys

## Slide 24

- 2. Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone Mechanism
- Pressure-sensitive receptors in the kidney, particularly in the afferent arterioles, recognize drop in blood flow
- This activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS)​
  - Angiotensin II and Aldosterone
- Overall blood volume then increases

## Slide 25

- 2. Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone Mechanism
- To Summarize:
- Lowered Cardiac Output of HF causes ↓ renal blood flow
- This leads to Sodium and Water Retention & Vasoconstriction
  - Initially this can be good: ↑ fluid means ↑ preload, thereby improving stroke volume (Frank-Starling)

## Slide 26

- 2. Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone Mechanism
- Downsides:
- Sodium and Water Retention
  - ↑ muscle stretching that occurs with Frank-Starling Mechanism also causes an ↑ in ventricular wall tension and resultant ↑ in myocardial oxygen consumption
  - This can cause ischemia and contribute to further ↓ of inotropy
  - Once this occurs, the ↑ in Preload is no longer causing compensation, rather worsening HF
- Vasoconstriction
  - Increased Afterload means lower cardiac output

## Slide 27

- 2. Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone Mechanism
- Downsides:
- Angiotensin II additional effects
  - Angiotensin II, also binds to cardiac angiotensin receptors causing myocyte hypertrophy, apoptosis, and interstitial fibrosis​
    - This occurs through myocardial fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposits​
  - The abnormal hypertrophy and fibrosis increase the passive stiffness of the ventricles , interfering with ventricular filling and resulting in ventricular remodeling

## Slide 28

- 3. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)
- Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) also known as arginine vasopressin is synthesized in the hypothalamus and is released due to elevated concentrations of both:​
  - norepinephrine ​
  - -and- ​
  - angiotensin II​

## Slide 29

- 3. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)
- ADH stimulates 2 types of receptors​
- V1 receptors​
  - Located in the cardiac myocytes and vascular smooth muscle​
  - Stimulation of these receptors stimulate increase cardiac contraction and vasoconstriction​
- V2 receptors​
  - Located in the renal collecting ducts​
  - Stimulate free water reabsorption​

## Slide 30

- 3. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)
- Downsides:
- Increased cardiac contraction and vasoconstriction​
  - Prolonged stimulation results in cardiac hypertrophy and LV remodeling​
- Free water reabsorption​
  - Prolonged stimulation results in vascular congestion and hyponatremia due to free water retention​

## Slide 31

- 4. Natriuretic Peptides
- The heart produces a group of natriuretic hormones/peptides that have potent diuretic, natriuretic, and vascular smooth muscle effects
- 2 of the 4  NPs most associated with HF: ANP & BNP
- ANP: Atrial natriuretic peptide, secreted by atria
- BNP: Brain natriuretic peptide, secreted by ventricles
- The one that fights against the downsides of the other compensatory mechanisms.

## Slide 32

- 4. Natriuretic Peptides
- In response to increased chamber stretch and pressure, NPs promote rapid transient natriuresis and diuresis through an increase in GFR and inhibition of tubular reabsorption of Sodium and Water
- NPs can also inhibit the SNS, RAAS, endothelin inflammatory cytokines, and vasopressin: stimulate vasodilation and reduce pulmonary artery pressures
- The RAAS and adrenergic system eventually overcomes the ability of natriuretic peptides

## Slide 33

- 4. Natriuretic Peptides
- In early HF: NT-proBNP can be detected as a byproduct of BNP
- BNP/proBNP elevation on lab work has been shown to correlate with Heart Failure Severity
- Many HF meds (diuretics, ACE inhibitors) lower BNP, therefore Chronic HF patients may have a normal BNP
- Eventually in many patients with heart failure, the natriuretic peptides become ineffective despite high circulating levels​ (can't out compete the other compensation mechanisms)

## Slide 34

- 5. Remodeling
- Many different comorbidities, risk factors, and compensatory mechanisms promote remodeling of the heart muscle structure.
- This remodeling promotes altered biomechanics, flow of the electrical system (arrhythmias), and increases risk of ischemia to the muscle tissue over time.

## Slide 56

- HFrEF
- Once a patient's systolic dysfunction as worsened enough to produce reduced ejection fraction on echo they are considered HFrEF.
- They are prone to a more "eccentric" or elongated
- appearance of their remodeling.
- Remember this remodeling makes them prone
- to arrythmias.

## Slide 57

- HFpEF
- Many clinicians and investigators have used the term “diastolic heart failure” for HFpEF in the past because many patients with HFpEF do have abnormal diastolic function ​
- However the term “diastolic heart failure” is not totally accurate  because there is evidence that some patients with HFpEF have some degree of “focal” systolic dysfunction despite a normal ejection fraction on echocardiogram​
- HFpEF is a better term

## Slide 58

- HFpEF
- Left Ventricular Remodeling​
- In patients with diastolic dysfunction, LV geometric changes result in concentric remodeling​
- ↑ wall thickness
- Diffuse interstitial fibrosis of the LV myocardium ​
- Impaired active relaxation of the myocardium​
- [picture]

## Slide 59

- HFpEF
- Chronotropic incompetence​
- Ineffective at increasing heart rate​
  - A key difference between HFrEF and HFpEF​
- Patients with advanced HFpEF have a very stiff LV and are unable to augment stroke volume with exercise:​
  - Restrictive cardiomyopathy​
  - Severe LV hypertrophy​
  - Severe coronary artery disease​
- The inability to increase heart rate with exercise causes exercise intolerance

## Slide 64

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure- PND
- Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND) occurs after the patient has been in the supine position for some time​
- The patient wakes up suddenly with a sensation of choking and air hunger and assumes an upright posture to relieve the symptoms​
- PND usually precedes orthopnea​
- PND is caused by a mobilization of interstitial fluid from infra-diaphragmatic locations during recumbency with a resultant increase in pulmonary venous pressure

## Slide 65

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure-Orthopnea
- Orthopnea (dyspnea occurring within a few minutes after the patient assumes a supine position) has the same cause as PND, but represents a more severe cardiac dysfunction​
- Elevating the head of the bed, sitting up, or standing can improve the symptoms​
- Severely affected patients usually sleep sitting upright​
- Another manifestation of pulmonary congestion cough (especially at night)​

## Slide 66

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure
- Clinical Manifestations:
- Thirst ​
  - Associated with activation of the ADH (arginine–vasopressin) system​
- Nocturia ​
  - Due to enhanced urine formation in the recumbent position when renal vasoconstriction decreases and venous return to the heart increases​
- Oliguria ​
  - Due to renal hypoperfusion from reduced cardiac output​
- Cerebral symptoms​
  - Anxiety, confusion, insomnia, memory impairment​

## Slide 68

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure
- Physical Exam Findings:
- Pleural Effusions​
- With progressive fluid accumulation, pleural effusions (hydrothorax) occur and will increase the severity of dyspnea by reducing pulmonary vital capacity​
- Dullness on percussion is the characteristic sign of a pleural effusion​
- Shifting dullness can be found when the patient moves from the sitting to the lateral decubitus position​
- Breath sounds over the area of pleural effusion are diminished or absent​
- Pleural effusion usually is slowly reabsorbed slowly as diuresis begins

## Slide 73

## Slide 75

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure
- Physical Exam Findings:
- Cardiac Cachexia​
- Although most patients with HF appear well nourished, those with more advanced disease (especially in the setting of chronic splanchnic congestion) frequently appear malnourished​
- ​This appearance arises from:​
  - Poor caloric intake (due to early satiety and nausea)​
  - Impaired absorption of fat​
  - Increased metabolism due to elevated levels of norepinephrine​
- The combination of reduced intake and increased energy expenditure leads to a reduction of tissue mass AKA: cardiac cachexia (weight loss from skeletal muscle atrophy)

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure
- Diagnostics:
- ECG should be routinely obtained and examined for evidence of: ​
- Underlying structural alterations:​
  - Left ventricular hypertrophy​
  - Prior myocardial infarctions​

## Slide 83

- Evaluation of Patients with Heart Failure
- Diagnostics:
- Chest radiography (CXR)​
- Careful examination of the cardiac silhouette, as well as the pulmonary vasculature, remains part of routine evaluation in all patients with HF: ​
- Cardiomegaly (cardiothoracic ratio > 50%) can be found in almost all patients with chronic HF, but may be absent in those with acute HF due to acute myocardial infarction or acute myocarditis​
- Interstitial Pulmonary Edema: bronchovascular markings that develop when the left-sided filling pressures increase above 25 mmHg​
  - Kerley lines- spindle-shaped linear opacities  ​
- Pleural effusions can also be seen ​
  - Often there may be a delay of 48 hours before improvement and  clearing of pulmonary findings can be seen on chest x-ray following diuresis

- Exercise Stress Testing
- Cardiopulmonary exercise stress testing (CPET)​
- CPET has been shown to add substantial precision in the initial evaluation of patients with HF and in their prognosis over time, especially when advanced therapies are considered​
- ​Measurements of the peak oxygen consumption (peak VO2) and the slope of the ratio of ventilation (VE) to carbon dioxide production (VCO2) (VE/VCO2) are powerful predictors of prognosis​
- Peak VO2 less than 50% predicted are considered indications for heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation​
- Since exercise intolerance is a key symptom in HFpEF, exercise testing is extremely valuable in these patients ​

- Indicated in patients with chronic HTN, CAD and features of heart failure

## Slide 191

- Decompensated Heart Failure-Loop Diuretics​
- Treatment with loop diuretics in acute HF provides symptomatic relief of congestive symptoms ​
- Most ED pts require IV dosing, because bowel wall edema may prevent proper GI absorption ​
- Dosing is guided by symptoms and prior usage ​
- Patients with chronic renal failure will require higher dosing  ​

- furosemide (Lasix) is the MC used loop diuretic in the emergency setting​
- CAUTION:  When administering loop diuretics alone without vasodilators (NTG), for patients with heart failure and HTN, there is an increased risk of worsening renal dysfunction and increased mortality ​

## Slide 192

- For patients who are furosemide (Lasix) naïve:​
- Recommended starting dose of Lasix IV in the emergency setting is 40 mg IV​
- For patients who are already taking furosemide (Lasix) on a daily basis:​
- Recommended IV starting dose is 1 to 2.5x the normal total oral daily dose​
- Example, if the patient is on furosemide 40 milligrams PO daily, then an initial ED dose is 40 to 100 milligrams IV bolus​
- In general, the maximum ER dose for patient’s with normal renal function is 80 mg IV, due to concerns of over diuresis
