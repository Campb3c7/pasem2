## Slide 5

- Treatment Goals in Coronary Artery  Disease
- Short-term:
- Reduce or prevent the symptoms of angina that limit a patient’s exercise capability and impair their quality of life
- Restore balance between supply and demand
- Long-term:
- Prevent CAD events such as myocardial infarction, arrhythmias and heart failure
- Prevent morbidity and mortality

## Slide 6

- Treatment in a Nutshell
- Reduce future cardiovascular risk through lifestyle changes and evidence-based preventive medications.
- Relieve angina and improve function with antianginal medications, selected according to symptoms, heart rate, blood pressure, and comorbidities.
  - Beta-blockers
  - Calcium channel blockers
  - Nitrates
  - Ranolazine

## Slide 7

- Risk Factor Modification: Smoking, Hypertension, Lipids
- Tobacco: Assess use, advise cessation, and offer behavioral support and medication when appropriate. Encourage avoidance of secondhand smoke.
- Blood pressure: Treat hypertension; a goal below 130/80 mm Hg is appropriate for many patients with CAD, individualized to the patient.
- Lipids: Use guideline-directed lipid-lowering therapy—typically high-intensity statin therapy for established ASCVD—and reassess LDL-C response to determine whether additional therapy is indicated.

## Slide 8

- Risk Factor Modification: Exercise, Weight, Diabetes, Alcohol
- Physical activity: Aim for at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity activity, as tolerated; tailor activity to symptoms and clinical status.
- Weight: Encourage sustainable weight management and assess weight-related health risks; BMI is one measure, not a universal treatment target.
- Diabetes: Individualize glycemic goals. For many nonpregnant adults, an A1C goal around 7% is reasonable, but goals depend on comorbidities, hypoglycemia risk, and patient factors.
- Alcohol: Do not recommend alcohol for cardiovascular benefit. If patients drink, discuss risks and moderation based on their health and medications.

## Slide 10

- Drug Therapy for Stable Angina
- There are three classes of anti-ischemic drugs commonly used in the management of chronic stable angina:
    - Beta blockers
    - Nitrates
    - Calcium channel blockers
- Choose therapy based on heart rate, blood pressure, ventricular function, comorbidities, and response.
- A beta-blocker or calcium channel blocker is commonly used initially; additional therapy may be needed if symptoms persist.

## Slide 11

- Nitrates
- Mechanism of Action:
- Nitrates are converted to nitric oxide, which increases vascular smooth-muscle relaxation.
- Predominant effect: venodilation → decreased venous return and preload.
- Reduced preload lowers ventricular wall stress and myocardial oxygen demand.
- Nitrates also dilate coronary arteries and can relieve coronary vasospasm.

## Slide 14

- IV Nitroglycerin
- Can be used for persistent ischemic discomfort despite sublingual therapy, once the IV is established.
- The drip can be titrated up or down and the effect is transient. This is useful in patients with marginal blood pressure
- Also, the treatment of choice for hypertensive emergency associated with decompensated HF/pulmonary edema

## Slide 15

- Sublingual Nitroglycerin
- Taken on a PRN basis: for quick relief when CP occurs. All patients with CAD are instructed to carry NTG tablets with them.
- Directions for use:
  - Stop activity and sit down.
  - Take one sublingual nitroglycerin dose at the onset of chest pain.
  - If pain is not relieved within 5 minutes, call 911. Do not wait to finish all three doses.
  - While awaiting EMS, additional doses may be taken every 5 minutes, up to three total, if blood pressure permits and the patient has been instructed to do so.

## Slide 16

- Sublingual Nitroglycerin
- Special Considerations:
  - Headache is a common adverse effect of nitroglycerin. Results from vasodilation, including dilation of cerebral blood vessels.
  - SL nitro causes rapid venous and to a lesser extent, arterial vasodilation.
    - Sit down before placing the medication under the tongue and allowing it to dissolve
  - Do NOT swallow the tablet! - let it dissolve slowly

## Slide 17

- Long-Acting Nitrates
- Long-acting nitrates are the second-line medication when a patient has frequent anginal symptoms that are not controlled with a beta blocker.
- Isosorbide mononitrate (Imdur)
  - Extended release is given once daily
  - Immediate release is given at 8am and 3pm to allow for a drug free interval overnight.
- Isosorbide dinitrate (Isordil): Given 2-3 times daily in a similar fashion.

## Slide 18

- Long-Acting Nitrates
- Extended-release nitrates are used for prevention of anginal symptoms, not acute relief.
- Dosing is scheduled to provide coverage during periods when the patient is most likely to experience angina.
- A daily nitrate-free interval (typically 10–12 hours) is needed to minimize nitrate tolerance.

## Slide 19

- Side Effects of Nitrates
- 1. Tolerance (Tachyphylaxis)
- Decreased responsiveness to nitrates with continuous or prolonged exposure.
- Can develop within 24–48 hours of continuous nitrate therapy.
- A daily nitrate-free interval helps maintain effectiveness.
- 2. Headache
- Common adverse effect due to vasodilation.
- May occur shortly after administration.
- Does not indicate whether the medication is therapeutically effective.

## Slide 20

- Side Effects of Nitrates
- 3. Flushing
- Results from vasodilation.
- 4. Orthostatic hypotension / Dizziness
- Due primarily to venous pooling and decreased venous return.
- Sit down before taking short-acting sublingual nitrates.
- Use caution in patients with low blood pressure or volume depletion.

## Slide 21

- Nitrate Contraindications
- Drug-Drug Interactions: PDE-5 inhibitors which are prescribed for erectile dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension.
    - Sildenafil (Viagra or Revatio)
    - Vardenafil (Levitra)
    - Tadalafil (Cialis, Acarida)
  - If a nitrate is used within 24 hours of a PDE-5 inhibitor, severe hypotension may develop.

## Slide 22

- Nitrate Contraindications and Cautions
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with LV outflow tract obstruction: Nitrates reduce preload and may worsen obstruction and symptoms.
- Severe aortic stenosis: Use cautiously because vasodilation and reduced preload may cause hypotension.
- Volume depletion or low blood pressure: Nitrates may worsen hypotension.

## Slide 23

- Nitrates
- Monitoring:
  - Monitor blood pressure, heart rate, dizziness, and anginal symptoms.
  - Track use of short-acting nitroglycerin and the frequency or pattern of chest discomfort.
  - Short-acting nitroglycerin is for acute episodes or predictable exertion.
  - New, worsening, prolonged, or rest symptoms require urgent evaluation.

## Slide 24

- Nitrates
- Be aware that nitrates can be used off-label to relieve esophageal spasm- a differential when a patient presents with chest pain.
- So, substernal pain that resolves with nitro can be of esophageal origin!

## Slide 25

- Beta-Blockers
- β1 receptors — Located primarily in the heart and juxtaglomerular cells of the kidneys.
- Stimulation increases heart rate, contractility, AV nodal conduction, and renin release
- Blockade reduces these effects
- β2 receptors — Present in bronchial and vascular smooth muscle, among other tissues.
- Stimulation promotes bronchodilation and vasodilation
- Blockade may cause bronchoconstriction

## Slide 27

- Beta-Blockers
- First-line therapy in the control of symptoms in patients with chronic stable angina.
- Beta-blockers block beta-adrenergic receptors, reducing the effects of epinephrine and norepinephrine on the heart.
- They reduce myocardial oxygen demand primarily by decreasing HR, BP and contractility
- Lower heart rate also increases diastolic filling time, which may support coronary perfusion.

## Slide 28

- Beta-Blockers
- β1-selective (cardioselective): Preferentially block β1 receptors; examples include metoprolol, atenolol, and bisoprolol.
- Nonselective: Block both β1 and β2 receptors; examples include propranolol, nadolol, and timolol.
- β-blockers with additional α1 blockade: carvedilol, labetalol

## Slide 29

- Beta-blockers
- Oxygen Supply
- Oxygen Demand
- May support coronary perfusion bylengthening diastolic filling time
- Main antianginal effect:↓ Heart rate
- ↓ Contractility
- ↓ Blood pressure/wall stress

## Slide 30

- Beta-Blockers
- Avoid in vasospastic angina
- Beta-blockers—particularly nonselective agents—may worsen coronary vasospasm due to unopposed alpha-receptor activity
- Calcium channel blockers are preferred first-line therapy for vasospastic angina; nitrates may also be used

## Slide 31

- Beta-Blockers: Adverse Effects
- General: Fatigue, dizziness, reduced exercise tolerance, cold extremities, sexual dysfunction.
- Cardiovascular: Bradycardia, hypotension, AV block; may worsen acute decompensated HF.
- Respiratory: Bronchospasm (especially nonselective agents).
- Metabolic: May mask adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycemia (e.g., tachycardia), especially in patients using insulin or sulfonylureas.
- Withdrawal: Abrupt discontinuation may cause rebound tachycardia, angina, or MI; taper gradually.

## Slide 32

- Beta-Blockers: Contraindications
- Severe bradycardia or symptomatic hypotension
- Second- or third-degree AV block without a    functioning pacemaker
- Acute decompensated heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or low-output state
- Active bronchospasm

## Slide 33

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) are a first-line option alongside beta blockers for chronic stable angina.
- They relieve symptoms by reducing myocardial oxygen demand, increasing coronary blood flow, or both.
- Calcium Channel Blockers are categorized as dihydropyridine (DHP) or non-dihydropyridine (Non-DHP)

## Slide 34

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- DHP CCB: Block calcium entry in vascular smooth muscle, leading to arterial vasodilation and reduced BP/afterload
  - Amlodipine
  - Nifedipine
- Non-DHP CCB: Block calcium entry in the cardiac tissue, which helps slow heart rate, decrease contractility and control arrhythmias
  - Verapamil
  - Diltiazem

## Slide 35

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- CCB should be considered if there are contraindications or adverse reactions to beta blockers/nitrates or if symptoms are not well controlled with a combination of these agents
- DHP CCB, such as amlodipine, is recommended when used in combination with a beta-blocker
- Combining a beta blocker with a non-DHP CCB can cause excessive bradycardia, AV block, or worsening LV dysfunction
- For vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina, CCBs are preferred; beta blockers may worsen coronary spasm.

## Slide 36

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- Adverse Effects:
- DHPs (amlodipine, nifedipine ER):
  - Edema
  - Headache
  - Flushing
  - Reflex tachycardia, more likely with short-acting agents (Nifedipine)

## Slide 37

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- Adverse Effects:
- Non-DHPs (verapamil, diltiazem):
  - Bradycardia and AV block
  - Hypotension, dizziness, and fatigue
  - Worsening heart failure in patients with reduced LV systolic function
  - Constipation, especially with verapamil

## Slide 38

- Ranolazine (Ranexa)
- MOA: inhibits the late phase of inward sodium channels in cardiac myocytes during repolarization reducing intracellular sodium concentration and thereby reducing calcium influx
  - Prolongs the ventricular action potential
  - Reduces ventricular wall tension, improves O2 delivery
  - Decreases myocardial oxygen demand
  - Ranolazine does NOT affect the HR and BP!

## Slide 39

- Ranolazine (Ranexa)
- Ranolazine is an add-on antianginal for patients who remain symptomatic despite a beta blocker, calcium channel blocker, or long-acting nitrate.
- Use and dosing:
  - Start 500 mg extended-release twice daily; increase to 1,000 mg twice daily if needed and tolerated.
  - With diltiazem or verapamil, limit ranolazine to 500 mg twice daily because these drugs increase its concentration.

## Slide 40

- Ranolazine (Ranexa)
- Adverse Effects:
  - Constipation
  - Headache
  - Nausea
  - Dizziness
  - QT prolongation
- Extensive hepatic metabolism. Contraindicated in patients with liver cirrhosis/clinically significant hepatic impairment.
- Monitor renal function in patients with moderate-to-severe renal impairment.

## Slide 41

- Ranolazine (Ranexa)
- Drug-Drug Interactions:
  - Digoxin: Increases digoxin levels; monitor and adjust dose as needed.
  - Simvastatin: Maximum dose 20 mg/day.
  - Diltiazem / Verapamil: Limit ranolazine to 500 mg twice daily.
  - Strong CYP3A inhibitors: Avoid (e.g., ketoconazole, itraconazole, clarithromycin).
  - Grapefruit juice / Fluconazole: Limit ranolazine to 500 mg twice daily.

## Slide 45

- Treatment Goals
- Relieve ischemic discomfort and reduce myocardial oxygen demand.
- Restore coronary blood flow when indicated and limit myocardial injury.
- Prevent further thrombus formation and recurrent ischemic events.
- Reduce complications and mortality.

## Slide 46

- Drug Therapy – Acute Coronary Syndrome
- Core ACS therapies: aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor, and an anticoagulant; reperfusion when indicated.
- Symptom-directed therapies: nitroglycerin, beta-blocker, and oxygen when clinically indicated.
- Selected situations: opioids for persistent pain despite anti-ischemic treatment; fibrinolysis for eligible STEMI when timely PCI is unavailable.
- Avoid routine use: non-aspirin NSAIDs; prophylactic antiarrhythmics.

## Slide 47

- Nitroglycerin
- Mechanism: predominantly venodilation → ↓ preload and ventricular wall stress → ↓ myocardial oxygen demand.
- SL nitro at first, often given by EMS, then IV drip if pain persists
- Avoid with hypotension, suspected right ventricular infarction, or recent PDE-5 inhibitor use.
- Use caution in severe aortic stenosis or obstructive HCM.

## Slide 48

- Oxygen
- Give supplemental oxygen if oxygen saturation <90% to improve myocardial oxygen supply and decrease anginal symptoms
- Patients without hypoxia have not demonstrated any clinical benefit from routine use of supplemental oxygen

## Slide 49

- Beta-blockers
- Administration in ACS can reduce the size of the infarction by improving perfusion
- Decreases recurrent ischemia or re-infarction
- Decreases mortality when continued long term
- Decreases the incidence of lethal ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death

## Slide 50

- Beta-blockers
- Oral beta blocker should be administered within the first 24 hours to patients without contraindications.
- A cardioselective beta blocker is preferred in the setting of an acute MI (metoprolol, atenolol).
- There is no apparent benefit to giving the beta blocker prior to PCI as long as the beta blocker is begun within the first 24 hours after the patient presents.

## Slide 51

- Beta-blockers
- Titrate the dose to a goal HR of 55-70 bpm while maintaining a systolic BP > 90mmHg
- Contraindications to beta blockers in ACS
  - Overt signs of decompensated heart failure
  - Bradycardia <55 bpm
  - Hypotension with a BP less than 90 mmHg systolic, cardiogenic shock
  - Second or third-degree heart block
  - Active asthma, bronchospasm, wheezing
  - Reassess once the patient is clinically stable.

## Slide 52

- Beta-blockers
- Long term: base decision on EF and other indications
- Reduced EF or heart failure: Continue beta-blocker therapy unless contraindicated
  - For chronic therapy when LVEF is below 50%,  guideline-preferred options include metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, or bisoprolol
- Preserved EF, uncomplicated/revascularized MI: Routine lifelong therapy has not shown clear outcome benefit; reassess the need over time.

## Slide 53

- ACEi/ARB
- ACE-inhibitors used post-MI can reduce mortality and recurrent cardiovascular events
- The 2025 ACS guideline recommends an oral ACE inhibitor—or an ARB if an ACE inhibitor is not tolerated—for patients with ACS who have any of these high-risk features:
  - LVEF ≤40%
  - Heart failure
  - Hypertension
  - Diabetes
  - Anterior STEMI
- Modest survival benefit even in lower-risk MI patient

## Slide 54

- ACEi/ARB
- Typically started within the first 24 hours of ACS presentation
- Continued long-term for high-risk patients or those with reduced EF
- Monitor blood pressure, creatinine, and potassium after initiation and dose changes
- Avoid ACEi + ARB combination

## Slide 55

- Analgesia - Opioids
- Consider IV morphine or fentanyl for severe pain that persists despite maximally tolerated anti-ischemic therapy.
- Not routine first-line treatment; do not use simply to mask ongoing ischemia.
- May cause hypotension, respiratory depression, nausea, and sedation.
- Can slow gastric emptying and delay the effect of oral P2Y12 inhibitors.

## Slide 56

- Analgesia - Opioids
- Monitor:
  - Pain relief
  - Vitals: BP, RR
  - CNS/respiratory depression
  - Pruritus may occur from opioid-related  histamine release and does not always indicate allergy
- Reversal agent: Naloxone (Narcan®) 0.4 mg IVP.  Should be on hand or easily accessible

- Analgesia
- Avoid non-aspirin NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen or diclofenac) for suspected or confirmed ischemic chest pain when possible

## Slide 59

- Antiplatelets vs Anticoagulants vs Fibrinolytics
| Medication type | What it does | Simple way to remember | Examples |
| Antiplatelets | Inhibit platelet activation or aggregation | Prevent the platelet plug from growing | Aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel |
| Anticoagulants | Inhibit coagulation factors and fibrin formation | Slow formation of the fibrin mesh | Heparin, enoxaparin, bivalirudin |
| Fibrinolytics | Promote breakdown of fibrin in an existing clot | Dissolve the clot’s mesh | Tenecteplase, alteplase |

## Slide 61

- Anti-Platelet Agents - ASA
- Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid)
- Available in oral tabs of 81, 162, 325 and 500 mg or as a 300mg rectal suppository
- MOA:
- Aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet COX-1.
- This decreases thromboxane A₂ production and reduces platelet aggregation.
- Its antiplatelet effect lasts for the life of the affected platelet.

## Slide 62

- Anti-Platelet Agents - ASA
- At presentation with ACS:
- Give 162–325 mg non–enteric-coated aspirin, chewed, as soon as possible unless contraindicated.
- Give the loading dose even if the patient takes daily aspirin.
- Follow with 81 mg daily.
- Aspirin plus an oral P2Y₁₂ inhibitor is dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT).

## Slide 63

- Anti-Platelet Agents - ASA
- After MI (secondary prevention):
- For most patients with ACS who are not at high bleeding risk, the default is DAPT—aspirin plus a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor—for at least 12 months
- After that, low-dose aspirin is commonly continued long term for secondary prevention

## Slide 65

- Anti-Platelet Agents - Clopidogrel (Plavix)
- Used in ACS and in selected patients with coronary artery disease.
- Also used for secondary prevention after ischemic stroke and for symptomatic peripheral artery disease.
- Loading dose in ACS: 300-600mg
- Maintenance dose: 75 mg daily
- Rare adverse effect: thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)—requires urgent recognition and treatment.

## Slide 66

- Anti-Platelet Agents - Prasugrel (Effient)
- Indications
  - ACS patients undergoing PCI (STEMI or NSTEMI), when appropriate.
- Dosing:
  - Loading dose: 60 mg orally when PCI is planned
  - Maintenance dose: 10 mg orally daily
  - Consider 5 mg daily for patients weighing <60 kg

## Slide 67

- Anti-Platelet Agents - Prasugrel (Effient)
- Adverse Effects:
- Bleeding: Most important adverse effect; may be severe or life-threatening. Monitor for signs of bleeding and easy bruising.
  - Contraindicated in patients with a history of stroke or TIA
  - It is generally avoided in patients aged 75 or older, except in selected high-risk patients.
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP): Rare  but serious; may present with fever, weakness, thrombocytopenia, renal dysfunction, or neurologic changes.

## Slide 68

- Anti-Platelet Agents- Ticagrelor (Brilinta)
- Indications
    - ACS patients managed with PCI or medical therapy
- Dosing:
    - Loading dose: 180 mg orally
    - Maintenance: 90 mg twice daily for the first 12 months after ACS

## Slide 69

- Anti-Platelet Agents- Ticagrelor (Brilinta)
- Adverse effects:
- Bleeding: Most important adverse effect; monitor for signs of bleeding.
- Hyperuricemia: May increase serum uric acid; use caution in patients with a history of gout.
- Dyspnea: Usually mild to moderate and often resolves with continued therapy.
  - Evaluate other causes of dyspnea (e.g., HF, recurrent ischemia) before attributing it to ticagrelor. Consider 	an alternative if symptoms are intolerable.

## Slide 72

- Anticoagulant Therapy
- Anticoagulation helps prevent thrombus propagation and recurrent coronary thrombosis during ACS treatment.
- Options include:
  - Unfractionated heparin (UFH) as an IV drip titrated with PTT or anti-Xa levels
  - Low-molecular weight heparin (LMWH) - therapeutic dose enoxaparin (Lovenox), 1 mg/kg SQ q12 hours

## Slide 73

- Anticoagulant Therapy
- Unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low-molecular weight heparin are used in patients with NSTEMI and STEMI and should be started in the ER as soon as the MI is identified.
  - Enoxaparin (Lovenox) is given only if the patient is not anticipated to go to LHC.
  - Unfractionated heparin drip is preferred in STEMI or NSTEMI with plans for LHC.

## Slide 75

- NSTEMI/Unstable Angina: Invasive or Selective Strategy?
- Invasive strategy: coronary angiography with revascularization when appropriate.
- Selective invasive strategy: medical treatment initially, with angiography if recurrent ischemia, instability, or other indications develop.
- Decide based on overall risk and clinical context—not a single score alone.

## Slide 76

- Provide guideline-directed anti-ischemic and antithrombotic therapy as appropriate.
- Monitor symptoms, ECG, hemodynamics, and evidence of recurrent ischemia.
- Obtain echocardiography when indicated to assess ventricular function and complications.
- Reconsider angiography if symptoms recur or the patient becomes unstable.
- Selective Invasive Strategy: Medical Management

## Slide 77

- Invasive Strategy / STEMI
- Contact cardiology/interventional cardiology promptly when an invasive strategy is indicated.
- Give aspirin and other antithrombotic therapy according to the ACS pathway.
- Use nitrates and beta-blockers only when clinically appropriate and not contraindicated.
- Do not delay urgent angiography or reperfusion to achieve pain relief or complete medication administration.
- STEMI requiring primary PCI: activate the cath lab and proceed as rapidly as possible.

## Slide 78

- NSTEMI: Stable vs. Unstable
- Unstable NSTEMI (e.g., ongoing/refractory ischemia, hemodynamic instability, life-threatening arrhythmia): urgent invasive evaluation.
- Stable NSTEMI: determine timing of angiography from overall ischemic risk and clinical course.
- Use antithrombotic and anti-ischemic therapy as appropriate.
- NPO status should be tied to planned procedures.

## Slide 81

- Fibrinolytics: Alteplase (tPA)
- MOA: Activates plasminogen which accelerates the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin to break down fibrin.
- Alteplase has been proven beneficial in limiting infarct size, improving LV function and reducing mortality, but it is far inferior to PCI- therefore it is only used when a PCI cannot be done.
- Alteplase is only used for a STEMI.

## Slide 83

- Fibrinolytics: Alteplase (tPA)
- Use: A patient presents within 12 hours of CP onset with significant ST elevation MI and an invasive strategy is NOT an option.
- Patients who are given a fibrinolytic should be on UFH/LMWH/fondaparinux for minimum of 48 hours and up to 8 days after the drug.  This helps to prevent small thromboses from causing additional blockages as the clot is lysed.

## Slide 84

- Additional Considerations
- Arrhythmia prevention: Do not use prophylactic lidocaine or other antiarrhythmics to prevent VT/VF.
- Electrolytes: Correct hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia to reduce arrhythmia risk.
- Secondary prevention: Initiate high-intensity statin therapy in ACS patients unless contraindicated, regardless of baseline LDL-C.
