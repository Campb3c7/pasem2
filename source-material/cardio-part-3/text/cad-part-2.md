## Slide 2

- Primary Care and CAD
- Primary Prevention:
- Reduce modifiable risk factors through lifestyle modification and early identification of metabolic abnormalities to prevent the development of CAD
- Secondary Prevention:
- Prevent progression of established atherosclerosis, preventing further cardiac damage, and reducing the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events in patients who already have a diagnosis of CAD.

## Slide 3

- Primary Care and CAD
- Cardiovascular Risk Assessment in Primary Care:
- Assess cardiovascular risk factors as part of routine preventive care.
- Evaluate blood pressure, lipid levels, smoking status, diabetes, kidney function, and lifestyle.
- Estimate cardiovascular risk when appropriate to guide preventive treatment decisions.
- Reassess risk as clinical circumstances change.

## Slide 4

- Primary Care and CAD
- Patients ages 20 to 29 years –
  - Identify and address cardiovascular risk factors early.
  - Assess lifetime risk and family history when appropriate.
  - Consider earlier lipid-lowering treatment in selected young adults, particularly those with LDL-C ≥160 mg/dL or a strong family history of premature ASCVD.

## Slide 5

- Primary Care and CAD
- Patients ages 30 to 79 years –
  - Use PREVENT-ASCVD for eligible patients to estimate 10-year risk.
  - Consider 30-year risk in eligible adults ages 30–59.
  - Use the estimate alongside clinical risk factors to guide prevention.

## Slide 8

- PREVENT-ASCVD vs PCE
- A. Kidney function: eGFR
- The PREVENT base equations incorporate estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), a measure of kidney function.
- Why is this important?
- Reduced kidney function is associated with increased cardiovascular risk.
- CKD and cardiovascular disease frequently coexist.
- GFR helps capture risk associated with impaired kidney function that the PCE does not directly incorporate.

## Slide 9

- PREVENT-ASCVD vs PCE
- B. Metabolic health: BMI and HbA1c
- These measures help capture aspects of metabolic health beyond simply identifying whether a patient has diabetes.
- BMI
- Provides information about body size and obesity-related risk.
- Helps incorporate metabolic health into cardiovascular risk estimation.
- HbA1c
- Available as an optional additional predictor.
- Reflects average blood glucose over approximately the preceding 2–3 months.
- Can provide additional information about glycemic status beyond a simple yes/no diabetes variable.

## Slide 10

- PREVENT-ASCVD vs PCE
- C. Kidney damage: Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)
- UACR assesses urinary albumin excretion relative to urinary creatinine. It helps identify kidney damage, including damage that may occur before substantial reductions in eGFR.
- Albuminuria provides additional information about kidney and cardiovascular health.
- For example, a patient with diabetes and a normal eGFR may still have significant albuminuria. UACR can help identify this additional risk.

## Slide 11

- PREVENT-ASCVD vs PCE
- D. PREVENT also offers an optional Social Deprivation Index based on the patient's ZIP code.
- This measure reflects area-level social and economic conditions that may influence health, such as:
- Access to healthcare.
- Economic resources.
- Educational opportunities.
- Housing and other social conditions.

## Slide 12

- PREVENT-ASCVD
- Patient Eligibility:
- Use PREVENT-ASCVD for eligible adults ages 30–79 without known CVD or subclinical atherosclerosis who fall within the applicable model's parameters.
- Do not use PREVENT as the primary decision tool for patients with:
- Known cardiovascular disease.
- Severe subclinical cardiovascular disease, such as CAC ≥300.
- End-stage kidney disease.
- A life expectancy of less than one year.
- Certain inherited cardiovascular conditions that independently establish elevated risk.

## Slide 15

- Primary Care Approach to CV Risk Modification
- Any approach to CV risk modification begins with patient education.
- For those patients who are healthy, emphasis is placed on continuation of healthy habits.
- For those patients with unhealthy habits, the counseling is more extensive, and the provider should set specific attainable goals for change with the patient.

## Slide 16

- Primary Care Approach to CV Risk Modification: Good Habits
- 1. Follow a heart-healthy diet
- Emphasize fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, and lean protein.
- Replace saturated fats with unsaturated fats (olive oil, nuts, avocados, and fatty fish).
- Choose low-fat or fat-free dairy products.
- 2. Limit foods that increase cardiovascular risk
- Limit saturated fats, trans fats, processed meats, and highly processed foods.
- Reduce sodium intake.
- Limit added sugars and sugar-sweetened beverages.

## Slide 17

- Primary Care Approach to CV Risk Modification: Good Habits
- 3. Maintain adequate hydration
- Choose water over sugar-sweetened beverages.
- 4. Engage in regular physical activity
- At least 150 minutes/week of moderate-intensity OR 75 minutes/week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity.
- Include muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days/week.
- 5. Prioritize adequate sleep
- Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep per night.

## Slide 31

- Lipoprotein A - Lp(a)
- LDL-like particle with an additional apolipoprotein(a) attached to apoB-100.
- Levels are largely genetically determined.
- Measure at least once in all adults.
- Elevated: ≥125 nmol/L (50 mg/dL) — approximately 1.4-fold increased ASCVD risk.
- Very high: ≥250 nmol/L (100 mg/dL) — approximately 2-fold increased estimated ASCVD risk.
- Elevated Lp(a) warrants more intensive management of modifiable risk factors and LDL-C lowering.

## Slide 32

- Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)
- ApoB measures the number of circulating atherogenic lipoprotein particles.
- An elevated ApoB suggests increased atherogenic particle burden.
- ApoB can reveal risk that LDL-C alone may underestimate, particularly in patients with elevated triglycerides, diabetes, or CKM syndrome.
- ApoB ≥120 mg/dL = ASCVD risk-enhancing factor (2026 guideline).

## Slide 33

- ApoB vs. Lp(a)
- Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)
- Measures the number of atherogenic lipoprotein particles.
- Each atherogenic particle carries one ApoB molecule.
- Includes LDL, VLDL, IDL, and Lp(a).
- Think: Particle number
- Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]
- A specific, inherited type of LDL-like particle.
- Has an additional protein called apolipoprotein(a) attached to ApoB.
- Elevated levels increase ASCVD risk.
- Think: Inherited risk particle

## Slide 34

- High-Sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (hs-CRP)
- CRP is a marker of systemic inflammation.
- Elevated hs-CRP is associated with increased ASCVD risk.
- In adults without ASCVD who have borderline PREVENT-ASCVD risk (3% to less than 5%), if hs-CRP is:
  - ≥2 mg/L on two successive occasions
  - With no identifiable underlying cause
- Then high-intensity statin therapy can be useful to reduce ASCVD events

## Slide 35

- Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) Scoring
- Consider CAC scoring in selected adults at borderline or intermediate PREVENT-ASCVD risk when uncertainty about lipid-lowering therapy remains.
- CAC provides evidence of calcified coronary atherosclerosis and can help refine risk.
  - CAC = 0: May support deferring therapy in selected patients, provided important exceptions—such as diabetes, smoking, and a strong family history of premature ASCVD—are absent.
  - CAC 1–99: Indicates coronary atherosclerosis; the treatment decision should incorporate the patient's overall risk and CAC percentile.
  - CAC ≥100 or ≥75th percentile: Supports lipid-lowering therapy.

## Slide 38

- Diabetics and CV Risk Modification
- Adult DM treatment goals:
  - Many nonpregnant adults: An A1c goal below 7% is appropriate.
  - Selected patients: A goal below 6.5% may be appropriate when achievable without significant hypoglycemia or other treatment burden.
  - Patients with substantial comorbidity, frailty, limited life expectancy, or high treatment-related risk: A less stringent goal, such as below 8%, may be appropriate.

## Slide 39

- Diabetics and CV Risk Modification
- In patients with type 2 diabetes and established ASCVD, heart failure, or CKD, use a GLP-1 RA and/or SGLT2 inhibitor with demonstrated cardiovascular or kidney benefit, as appropriate.
- These medications may be indicated regardless of baseline A1c or metformin use.
- Drug selection depends on cardiovascular and kidney conditions, glycemic needs, weight goals, contraindications, tolerability, and patient preferences.

## Slide 41

- Other Aspects of CV Risk Modification
- Diagnose and treat hypertension to goal
- Confirm hypertension using standardized office BP measurements and home or ambulatory monitoring when appropriate.
- General treatment goal: BP below 130/80 mmHg for most adults.
- Consider more intensive SBP lowering (below 120 mmHg) in appropriate patients at increased cardiovascular risk.
- Individualize treatment based on comorbidities, orthostatic symptoms, frailty, and medication tolerance.
- Select antihypertensive therapy based on the patient's clinical profile and compelling indications.

## Slide 42

- Other Aspects of CV Risk Modification
- Recognize and treat obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)
- Recurrent upper-airway obstruction during sleep causes intermittent hypoxemia (repeated drops in oxygen saturation).
- Brief arousals restore airway patency and breathing but fragment normal sleep.
- Each episode triggers sympathetic activation, increasing heart rate and blood pressure.
- Repeated episodes contribute to inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and cardiovascular stress.

## Slide 43

- Other Aspects of CV Risk Modification
- Persons with untreated sleep apnea develop RV hypertrophy and pulmonary hypertension, as the body attempts to desperately increase oxygenation to the tissues by circulating the blood through the lungs more quickly.
- Eventually, these patients will develop right sided heart failure (cor pulmonale).
- Treatment by wearing their CPAP or BiPAP at night every night is essential.

## Slide 44

- Other Aspects of CV Risk Modification
- Screen for unhealthy alcohol use
- Cardiovascular risk increases with greater alcohol consumption; heavy and binge drinking are particularly harmful.
- Recognize excessive drinking
- Binge: ≥4 drinks for women or ≥5 for men on one occasion
- Heavy: ≥8 drinks/week for women or ≥15 drinks/week for men

## Slide 51

- Take Home Messages for CAD Prevention
- Lifestyle changes, lifestyle changes, lifestyle changes!  They rely on a motivated patient and are the hardest to accomplish but lifestyle changes make the greatest impact on ASCVD risk.  Keep chipping away and working with your patient to find goals that they can accomplish.
- The importance of controlling these gateway diseases to goal- hypertension, dyslipidemia and diabetes- in preventing CAD cannot be overstated.

## Slide 52

- Aspirin for Primary Prevention: Use Selectively
- Routine low-dose aspirin (81 mg/day) is not recommended for most adults without established ASCVD.
- Aspirin may be considered in select adults ages 40–70 with elevated cardiovascular risk and low bleeding risk.
- Do not routinely initiate aspirin in adults older than 70 or patients with increased bleeding risk.
- Key takeaway
- The decision requires individualized assessment of cardiovascular benefit versus bleeding risk.

## Slide 60

- Take-Home MessagesPrimary Prevention of CAD
- Start early. Atherosclerosis develops over time, and prevention should begin before short-term risk becomes high.
- Calculate appropriately. Use PREVENT-ASCVD to estimate 10-year risk in eligible adults and consider 30-year risk in eligible younger adults.
- Recognize independent treatment indications. Severe hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, and stage 3–4 CKD can warrant lipid-lowering therapy without relying on calculated risk.
- Personalize the estimate. Consider family history, Lp(a), other risk factors, and patient preferences.

## Slide 61

- Take-Home MessagesPrimary Prevention of CAD
- Reclassify when appropriate. CAC scoring can help resolve uncertainty about treatment.
- Treat to goals. Follow both the recommended percentage LDL-C reduction and applicable LDL-C/non-HDL-C goals.
- Address all major risk factors. Smoking cessation, physical activity, nutrition, blood pressure, and diabetes management remain essential.
