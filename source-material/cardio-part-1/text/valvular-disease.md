- Imaging
- Cardiac IMAGING in Valvular Disease
- Why Echocardiography (aka ‘Echo’)?
  - First-line imaging for suspected valve disease
  - Non-invasive, widely available
  - Provides:
    - Valve anatomy (structure)
    - Valve function (movement, opening/closing)
    - Blood flow and Pressure gradients, utilizing doppler
    - Ventricle response: chamber size, wall thickness, Ejection Fraction (EF)

## Slide 5

- IMAGING: Transthoracic Echo (TTE)
- Ultrasound probe paced on chest wall
- Standard FIRST test for valve disease
- Noninvasive, quick
- Provides real-time view
- Limitations: image quality depends on multiple factors such as body habitus, lung interference, comorbidities, technician skill
- Interpreted by a cardiologist who provides an official report of findings

## Slide 11

- Aortic Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis
- Narrowing of the aortic valve due to thickened or immobile leaflets  obstructs blood flow from LV to aorta
- LV must generate higher pressure to eject blood
  - Resulting in: pressure overload, LV hypertrophy (LVH), and eventually, LV dysfunction
- Men > Women
- Aortic Sclerosis: thickened leaflets without significant stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis: measurable outflow obstruction and reduced valve area

## Slide 12

- Aortic Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis
- Etiologies:
  - Degenerative: calcification of valve over time
    - Primarily 65+ years of age
    - Risk factors: smoking, HTN, DM, high LDL, CRP elevation
    - Leading cause of valvular surgery in US
  - Congenital: malformed leaflets
    - Typically, bicuspid aortic valve but can be unicuspid
    - Primarily younger patients
  - Rheumatic heart disease
    - Most common cause worldwide, but rare in industrialized countries
  - Other less common causes: radiation, connective tissue disease, hyperlipoproteinemia syndromes

## Slide 15

- Aortic Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis: Pathophysiology
- Congenital:
  - Abnormal valves are exposed to more profound abnormalities in blood flow and develop AS earlier than tri-leaflet valves, usually in their 4th-5th decade.
- Rheumatic heart disease (RHD):
  - An autoimmune inflammatory reaction is triggered by prior Streptococcus infection that targets the valvular endothelium leading to inflammation, leaflet thickening, and commissural fusion. Eventual calcification is also seen in rheumatic AS but often less than degenerative

## Slide 16

- Aortic Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis: Pathophysiology
- Consequences:
  - Stenosis occurs slowly and is subclinical until the disease is fairly advanced
  - Progressive calcium deposition and inflammation limit aortic leaflet mobility, leading to a pressure burden on the left ventricle
  - As valve narrows, the LV must contract stronger to eject blood out, causing the LV myocardium to hypertrophy (concentric)
  - Initially, this maintains cardiac output
  - However, it becomes increasingly more difficult to empty the ventricle before next cardiac cycle

## Slide 17

- Aortic Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis: Pathophysiology
- Consequences (continued):
  - This LV hypertrophy creates a large pressure gradient within the LV to maintain stroke volume. LVH also results in increased LV stiffness, causing diastolic dysfunction and increasing myocardial oxygen demand
  - This process continues with worsening outflow tract obstruction as the valve becomes progressively more stenotic and resultant LV thickening
  - Cardiac output becomes fixed; unable to adjust adequately to exertion

## Slide 18

- Aortic Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis: Pathophysiology
- Consequences (continued):
  - Early/Compensated Aortic Stenosis:
    - Despite severe obstruction, EF is usually normal (≥ 50%)
    - LVH allows the ventricle to generate high pressure to maintain stroke volume
    - This is why pts can be asymptomatic for years even with severe narrowing
  - Late/Decompensated AS:
    - Over time, the LV becomes too stiff and too oxygen-hungry
    - First this causes diastolic dysfunction and then leads to systolic dysfunction with a reduced EF (<50%)
    - Once EF falls, prognosis worsens dramatically

- Aortic Regurgitation
- Incompetent aortic valve allows blood to leak back from the aorta into the LV during diastole  LV faces volume overload (being filled from the left atrium + backflow from aorta)
- LV must stretch more to compensate for extra volume
  - At first, this dilation helps maintain forward output
  - Eventually, this causes hypertrophy (eccentric)
  - LV walls become too dilated and overstretched that the myocardium loses its contractile strength - leading to systolic failure with reduced EF
    - Also causes blood to back up into the left atrium, thereby increasing LA pressure

- AR most often develops slowly as we discussed (chronic AR)
- Acute AR:
  - However, there are clinical circumstances where severe AR develops abruptly and there is a sudden backflow into the LV

## Slide 40

- RHD
- Rheumatic Heart Disease
- Let’s pause to review RHD
  - Mechanism: antibodies against strep proteins cross-react with ‘self’ antigens in the heart, joints, vessels --- produces inflammatory response
  - During the acute rheumatic fever phase, all 3 layers of the heart (pancarditis) may be affected
  - Valve leaflets inflame and small sterile vegetations develop (most often mitral, followed by aortic valve)
  - Repeated inflammation heals with fibrosis (chronic scarring)
    - Effect: fusion and contracture of valve leaflets, shortening the chordae

## Slide 41

- RHD
- Rheumatic Heart Disease
- Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD)
  - Chronic valve damage develops years later as recurrent inflammation and scarring deform valve leaflets
    - Mitral > Aortic > Tricuspid > Pulmonic
    - Combination of valves may be affected
  - A child/teenager with Acute Rheumatic Fever may not present with clinically significant RHD until adulthood
  - Major cause of morbidity/mortality world-wide where access to legitimate antibiotics is limited and crowding common

## Slide 48

- Mitral Stenosis
- Mitral Stenosis
- MS leads to difficulty emptying the LA. This leads to an increase in LA pressures
- Higher pressures in the LA cause pulmonary venous congestion, and dilation of the LA. This stretches the conduction system which can lead to atrial dysrhythmias
- The pulmonary arteries respond with vasoconstricting and arterial hypertrophy… leading to remodeling which leads to pulmonary HTN

## Slide 49

- Mitral stenosis
- Mitral Stenosis
- As the pulmonary arteries become overloaded and hypertrophied, right ventricular pressures slowly increase to help force blood through these less compliant vessels
- This eventually leads to severe pulmonary HTN causing RV failure

## Slide 50

- Mitral stenosis
- Mitral Stenosis
- In mild to moderate stenosis, there may not be enough strain to cause symptoms when the patient is at rest
  - However, with exertion or when the heart is stressed (exercise, anemia, pregnancy) the patient may complain of feeling symptomatic more easily
- Severe MS will have symptoms at rest
- Chronic wall tension causes inflammation, and clotting occurs more readily in a chamber that doesn’t empty well

  - Rheumatic fever- most common

  - Valve degeneration: aging
- Female predominance (~70%)

## Slide 65

- Mitral regurgitation
- Mitral Regurgitation
- The mitral valve does not seal properly, thus blood flows back into the LA when the left ventricle contracts (systole)
  - In chronic MR, it occurs slowly - the left atrium initially hypertrophies (slightly) to force an increased volume of blood into the LV, but LA dilation is the predominant coping mechanism for the regurgitant volume
  - This increases pressure on the LA and stretches the atrial conduction system

## Slide 66

- Mitral regurgitation
- Mitral Regurgitation
- LV dilates (eccentric) to accept larger than normal preload
- As MR worsens, it causes pulmonary vascular congestion – leading to development of pulmonary HTN and right-sided heart failure
- LV output will eventually decline as regurgitation worsens
- LV becomes increasingly dilated and LV forward stroke volume decreases

    - Annular dilation
- Symptoms:
  - Asymptomatic for mild
  - Eventually will develop fatigue, dyspnea, palpitations, and other signs of heart failure

- Tricuspid Stenosis
- Fixed tricuspid valve decreases the opening for blood to pass from the right atrium into the right ventricle during diastole
  - Leads to reduced RV filling (preload) and increased right atrial pressure  less cardiac output and more systemic venous congestion
- Etiology:
  - Exceedingly rare and almost always a component of mixed valve pathology due to Rheumatic Heart Disease

## Slide 88

- Tricuspid regurgitation
- Tricuspid Regurgitation
- Rare but more common than Tricuspid Stenosis
- A leaky tricuspid valve does not seal properly during systole
  - When RV contracts to push volume into the Pulmonary Artery, it also moves blood back into Right Atrium through unsealed tricuspid valve
  - This increases the amount of blood that fills the RV during diastole (normal preload + regurgitant volume)
  - Overtime, RA and RV stretch to accommodate the volume overload
    - Leads to right-sided heart failure

## Slide 89

- Tricuspid regurgitation
- Tricuspid Regurgitation
- Etiologies:
  - Primary: endocarditis, carcinoid syndrome, congenital abnormalities
  - Most Common = Secondary/functional: due to RV dilation from pulmonary HTN or RV failure
    - Whatever can cause pulm HTN or RV failure can cause TR
      - Pulmonary dz (PE, COPD), left-sided HF, MS, MR, etc.

## Slide 95

- Surgical treatment
- Surgical Treatment of Valvular Disease
- Medical therapy (medications) helps symptoms
- Only surgery can definitively fix the valve
- 2 main strategies:
  - Valve repair: preserve patient’s native valve
  - Valve replacement: remove native valve and replace with prosthetic valve

## Slide 96

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Repair
- Preferred when feasible, especially mitral valve
- Benefits:
  - Preserves native tissue
  - No need for lifelong anticoagulation
  - Lower risk of infection
- Most utilized for mitral regurgitation

## Slide 97

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement
- Repair may be technically undesirable if valve/surrounding anatomy is too damaged
  - Severe calcification (especially rheumatic disease with fused/rigid leaflets)
  - Extensive leaflet destruction or distortion - cannot be reconstructed properly
  - Traumatic rupture
  - Surgical team feels the regurgitation or stenosis would persist despite repair

## Slide 98

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement - Types
- Mechanical valves
  - Durable (20-30 years)
  - Synthetic
  - Requires lifelong anticoagulation (warfarin)
  - Preferred for younger patients (<60 yo)
- Bioprosthetic (Tissue) valves
  - Shorter durability (10-20 years)
  - Bovine or porcine pericardium
  - No need for anticoagulation
  - Often chosen for older patients (>60 yo) or when anticoagulation is contraindicated

## Slide 99

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement
- Anticoagulation
  - Risk of thromboembolic event highest within the first three months after valve replacement
  - Mechanical valves are more thrombogenic, thus needing lifelong warfarin
    - INR target is generally 2.5 – 3.5 but does depend on valve location and other risk factors
    - Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) like apixaban are contraindicated

## Slide 100

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement
- Anticoagulation (continued)
  - Bioprosthetic Valves – less prone to thrombosis
    - Post-operative, patients are anticoagulated for 3-6 months with either low-dose aspirin or warfarin
    - Long-term, patients usually take a low-dose daily aspirin

## Slide 101

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement
- The presence of foreign material makes pt more susceptible to infection
  - Native tissue valves have an endothelial layer that generally repels colonization
  - Bacteria have a general tendency to colonize damaged/abnormal areas
  - Bacteria can adhere to prosthetic material and form biofilm, which provides better protection from the body’s natural immune defenses and antibiotics

## Slide 102

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement
- Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE)
  - Early (within 12 months of procedure): likely hospital-acquired, typically Staphylococcus aureus
    - ABX are given peri-operatively; they are not continued long-term
    - Follow-up care important, as well as strong patient education about increased risk of PVE and to report any worrisome s/s promptly
  - Late (after 12 months): usually caused by hematogenous spread, commonly Streptococci

## Slide 103

- Surgical treatment
- Valve Replacement
- Prophylaxis for Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE)
  - Pts with prosthetic material (mechanical valve or bioprosthetic) require prophylactic abx for certain procedures
    - Dental cleanings and procedures
    - Respiratory tract procedures
    - Invasive skin procedures
  - Standard regimen: Amoxicillin 2g PO taken 30-60 minutes before the procedure
