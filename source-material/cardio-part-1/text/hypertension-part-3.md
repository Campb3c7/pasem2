## Slide 4

- How Do Clinicians Decide On BP Treatment?
- Common diseases in the US population, like hypertension, are studied and clinical guidelines, by multiple expert groups, developed for diagnosis and treatment.
  - American College of Cardiology (ACC), the American Heart Association (AHA), the American Society of Hypertension (ASH), the Joint National Committee (JNC), the International Society on Hypertension in Blacks (ISHIB).

## Slide 5

- Who is Running This Show?
- Joint National Committee 7th Meeting (JNC7) established guidelines in 2003 that were in place until December 2013 when the long overdue JNC 8 guidelines were published.
  - The JNC 8 guidelines were controversial and characterized by many as “likely to harm patients” and “as clear as mud”.  There were calls for retraction of the guidelines- but they have now become largely ignored.
- Most US clinicians now use the American College of Cardiology/ American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines.

## Slide 6

- Goals of Antihypertensive Treatment
- BP goal <130/80 is desired for those undergoing treatment for HTN
                - The overall goal of reducing blood pressure is to reduce the risk of future adverse outcomes of CVD, thus all potential risk factors must be assessed and potential CV risk calculator used (PREVENT or ASCVD plus).
- CVD risk will double if the BP is greater than 115/75 for every 20/10 increment of BP elevation.

## Slide 7

- Lifestyle Management Recommendations in HTN
- As systolic BP increases with age, the goal should always be considering lifestyle modifications to help prevent HTN in all patients.
- Lifestyle modification is an important approach either alone for those persons with elevated BP or in combination with antihypertensive medication for patients with Stage 1 or 2 HTN.
- If lifestyle modification is insufficient to get to goal, drug treatment must be initiated.

## Slide 8

- Lifestyle Management Recommendations in HTN
- A heart healthy DASH diet:
  - Eating vegetables, fruits, and whole grains
  - Including fat-free or low-fat dairy products, fish, poultry, beans, nuts, and vegetable oils
  - Limiting foods high in saturated fat, such as fatty meats, full-fat dairy, and tropical oils such as coconut, palm kernel, and palm oils
  - Limiting sugar-sweetened beverages and sweets

## Slide 13

- Long Term Management of HTN
- What should we do at a chronic care visit with the hypertensive patient:
- Blood pressure readings
  - If able use automated oscillometric meter to check BP, otherwise check manually, and compare them to previous visits.
  - Evaluate the patient’s home BP log, noting:
    - Average SBP and DBP
    - Highest reading and lowest reading. Ask about any symptoms of low blood pressure.
  - Compare home cuff to in-office manual reading, if not already done at previous visit, to assure accuracy and reliability of the home BP log.

## Slide 14

- Long Term Management of HTN
- Chronic care visit with the HTN patient:
- Assess for HTN associated symptoms:
    - ENT: Headaches, visual disturbances, epistaxis, lightheadedness/dizziness, tinnitus
    - CV: Diaphoresis, chest pain, palpitations, edema, dyspnea or decreased exercise tolerance.
  - Check for compliance with currently prescribed medications and monitor for side effects.

## Slide 15

- Long Term Management of HTN
- Chronic care visit with the HTN patient:
- Perform a physical exam:
    - General assessment, HEENT, cardio and vascular exams, lungs, GI, neuro, lymph, psych
- Review lab results if present, or if due, order regular labs.
    - Frequency depends on chronicity and HTN control.
    - Will likely include chemistry panel, and screening for comorbid conditions (A1c and lipid panel).

## Slide 16

- Long Term Management of HTN

- Assess how lifestyle change is going
    - At minimum address one needed lifestyle management goal per chronic visit.
    - Needs to be measurable, attainable, and should be reassessed at all chronic care visits.
- Treatment plan: Decide if the medication regimen needs to be adjusted or if any additional work-up is needed.

## Slide 17

## Slide 21

- Withholding Drug Treatment of HTN
- Consider withholding antihypertensive treatment and tolerating a higher BP in stage I HTN (130-139/ 80-89) in patients who are over age 75 with:

  - ECF residence
  - Multiple comorbidities (not HTN risks)
  - Orthostatic hypotension
  - Limited life expectancy

## Slide 23

- Treatment of Hypertension
- For patients indicated to begin antihypertensive medication beyond lifestyle changes consider the following:
  - Determine if monotherapy or combination therapy is needed
  - Determine if there is a compelling indication such as associated comorbidities for a particular class of anti-hypertensive.
  - Look at other patient characteristics such as child-bearing, pregnant, and race to determine if there is a strong reason to chose or avoid a drug class.

## Slide 24

- HTN Drug Treatment PrinciplesMono- or Combination Therapy
- In stage 1 hypertension, a single agent is generally sufficient to lower the blood pressure to goal of <130 and <80 mmHg.
- In stage 2 hypertension, strongly consider initiation of two drugs at low dose (except ACE-I or ARB) within a fixed dose combination pill to improve your chances of getting to goal.
  - Combination therapy lowers blood pressure more than monotherapy
  - Increases the likelihood that target blood pressure will be achieved in a reasonable time period
  - Reduced risk of dose dependent side effects
  - Improves adherence

## Slide 25

- Compelling Indications
- Compelling indications: High-risk conditions that are direct sequela of HTN or are commonly associated with HTN.
- In these disease states, a specific class of drugs should be initiated because the class has evidence-based survival benefit.
- Some compelling indications are:
  - Heart Failure			Post MI
  - CAD				*PAD
  - Afib				Stroke
  - Diabetes Mellitus		CKD

## Slide 26

- Drug Treatment Principles
- The following drugs are indicated in the presence of these compelling indications
- Heart failure (Systolic Dysfunction): ACEI or ARB + beta-blocker + aldosterone antagonist + diuretic, try to get at least the first 2-3 on board (avoid non-DHP CCB)
- *Post-myocardial infarction: beta blocker + ACEI or ARB, try to get both on board

## Slide 27

- Drug Treatment Principles
- CAD: Beta blocker, ACEI or ARB, then a DHP-CCB or thiazide
- Afib: Beta blocker or non-DHP CCB, ACEI or ARB, then thiazide diuretic
- Diabetes: ACEI or ARB, then either a DHP-CCB or thiazide
- Chronic kidney disease/ Proteinuria: ACEI or ARB, then either a DHP-CCB or a thiazide
- Recurrent stroke prevention: ACEI or ARB, then either a thiazide or a DHP-CCB

## Slide 28

- Drug Treatment Principles
- Other factors to consider when choosing a drug regimen.
- Pregnancy potential: NO ACEI or ARB; however, thiazides, DHP-CCB or BB are okay
- Age >60 years: Thiazide, ACE-I or ARB preferred
  - Gout, urinary incontinence, SSRI treatment or low serum sodium: avoid thiazide diuretics.

## Slide 29

- Drug Treatment Principles

- Calcium channel blockers and thiazide diuretics work best in AA patients with hypertension.

## Slide 32

- Diuretics
- Diuretics stimulate the production of urine by the kidneys, by decreasing the kidney’s ability to reabsorb water from the filtrate passing through the nephron.
- Increasing urine production lowers blood volume and CO temporarily, leading to increased PVR, however 6-8wk later CO normalizes and PVR reduces.
- Types of diuretics that lower blood pressure include thiazides, loop, and potassium sparing diuretics.

## Slide 35

- Thiazide Diuretics
- These drugs work by increasing renal excretion of sodium and may have some vasodilator effects.
- Clinical outcome benefits (reduction of strokes and major cardiovascular events) have been best established with chlorthalidone, and indapamide. No benefit has been noted with HCTZ
- Efficacy in reduction of BP is more so seen with chlorthalidone (1.5-2x greater) and indapamide vs HCTZ. Efficacy improves with addition of ACEI or ARB
- Diuretics are relatively weak anti-hypertensives and most effective when combined with ACEI or ARBs, may be used with CCBs.

## Slide 36

- Thiazide Diuretics
- These drugs are given in the morning to avoid nocturnal diuresis
- Adverse effects:
  - Electrolyte abnormalities: hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypoMg, hyperCa (usually seen in first 2 weeks of therapy until body adjusts)
  - Gout- hyperuricemia
  - Hyperglycemia- worsening DM control
  - Hypovolemia (overdiuresis)
- SBP and LDL cholesterol levels were lower in patients taking chlorthalidone compared to HCTZ

## Slide 37

- Thiazide Diuretics
- Due to their mild glucose raising effects, clinicians should avoid prescribing thiazides as first line in diabetic patients, although they can be used.
- Patients newly started on a thiazide or loop diuretic or if prescribed a dosage increase should have a chemistry panel checked within 5-7 days of initiation to evaluate for:
  - Hypokalemia
  - Hyponatremia

- Loop diuretics are not usually used for hypertension treatment, instead they are frequently used for symptomatic

## Slide 39

- Loop Diuretics
- Account for most of sodium excretion in the thick ascending limb, are sulfonamide derivatives and relatively cheap except ethacrynic acid.
- Furosemide (Lasix) 20-80 mg PO daily or bid, less bioavailable than other loops, so an IV dose of the same milligram is roughly twice as effective as an oral dose
- Bumetanide (Bumex) 0.5-2 mg daily or bid, highly bioavailable
- Torsemide (Demadex) 10-80 mg daily/bid, highly bioavailable
- Ethacrynic acid (Edecrin)-a non-sulfa loop for those patients with severe sulfa allergy.

## Slide 40

- Loop Diuretics Dosing
- In equipotent doses, the effects of each of the loop diuretics is very similar.
- Lasix 40 mg = Bumex 1 mg = Demadex 20mg.
- All the loop diuretics are available IV, and the degree of diuresis is largely determined by the rate at which the drug reaches its’ site of action in the nephron.

## Slide 41

- Loop Diuretics Dosing
- Higher doses of loop diuretics may be needed in patients with:
- Significantly decreased glomerular filtration rate from CKD or AKI or
- If the patient is accustomed to the medication
- IV formulations (bolus/ drip) of loops are given:
- In acutely decompensated heart failure
- When there is poor GI absorption (edema of the bowel from volume overload)

## Slide 42

- Loop Diuretics Dosing
- Loop diuretics are almost always given with supplemental potassium in order to avoid hypokalemia.
- In a healthy kidney, about 20mEq of potassium is given for every 40 mg of Lasix- although this can vary, and chemistry panel should be used to adjust this dose if needed.
- Loop diuretics are given in AM or afternoon (if bid no later than 5pm) to avoid a nocturnal diuresis

## Slide 43

- Loop Diuretics- Adverse Reactions
- Diuretic effects:
  - Electrolytes: hypokalemia, hyponatremia
  - Overdiuresis/ hypovolemia and contraction alkalosis
  - Gout/ hyperuricemia
- Hypersensitivity reactions: rash and rare acute interstitial nephritis
- Ototoxicity (tinnitus and hearing loss), transient to irreversible, usually occurs with very high dose

## Slide 44

- Potassium Sparing Diuretics
- Triamterene (Dyazide or Maxzide) when used in combination with HCTZ
- Amiloride (Midamor)
- Spironalactone (Aldactone)
- Eplerenone (Inspra)
- Triamterene and amiloride directly block sodium channels in the distal tubule leading to a weak diuretic effect, but conservation of potassium.  Amiloride is better tolerated and less nephrotoxic than triamterene.

## Slide 45

- Potassium Sparing Diuretics
- Like all diuretics, give in the morning to avoid nocturnal diuresis, and if used BID, avoid giving after 5pm
- Adverse effects of the potassium sparing diuretics include:
  - Hyperkalemia: particularly in diabetics and CKD patients, or patients on an ACEI or ARB
  - Crystalluria and cast formation is seen with triamterene- leading to kidney stones and AKI

## Slide 46

- Potassium Sparing Diuretics/ Aldosterone Antagonists
- Two of the potassium sparing diuretics work by a different mechanism, competitively inhibiting the mineralcorticoid (aldosterone) receptor in the distal tubule which decreases the number of Na-K pumps and increases serum potassium, while increasing sodium loss in the urine.
- Eplerenone (Inspra)
- Spironolactone (Aldactone)

## Slide 47

- Aldosterone Antagonists
- For that reason, they carry a compelling indication for HFrEF and are the initial diuretic of choice in a patient with ascites from chronic liver disease.
- Although they are beneficial for their weak diuretic effect- they are more commonly valued for their ability to block the effects of aldosterone on the kidney, vasculature and heart.

## Slide 48

- Aldosterone Antagonists: Adverse Effects
- Hyperkalemia especially if given in combination with ACEI, ARB or potassium supplements, use with caution and monitor chem panel, especially in GFR < 50, diabetics and pts with proteinuria.
- Endocrine side effects: spironolactone is less specific to the mineralocorticoid receptor, and binds to estrogen and progesterone receptors, resulting in gynecomastia, irregular menstrual periods and impotence.
  - Eplerenone (Inspra) more selective thus reducing potential for endocrine SEs.

## Slide 50

- Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) Inhibitors
- ACE inhibitors work by blocking conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II
  - End result- inhibiting both vasoconstriction and release of aldosterone.
- ACEI also increase bradykinin levels, a potent vasodilator.
- Generally, ACEI are more effective for antihypertensive treatment in Whites and less effective in Blacks.

## Slide 51

- Lots of Uses for ACE Inhibitors
- ACEIs are used to treat HTN and certain disease states who don’t have HTN.
  - ACEI are the treatment of choice in patients with chronic kidney disease and proteinuria to prevent progression.
  - ACEI reduce morbidity and mortality rates in patients with HFrEF and recent myocardial infarctions.
  - ACEI may halt or may regress remodeling of the left ventricle in HF, CAD and cardiomyopathies

## Slide 52

- ACE Inhibitors: Nephroprotective and Nephrotoxic
- Some patients with a specific disease process have constant or near constant activation of the RAAS in an attempt to keep their glomerular pressure and filtration at normal levels.
- Who are these patients?
- What will happen when you suddenly block their RAAS?

## Slide 53

- ACE Inhibitors: Nephroprotective and Nephrotoxic
- ACE-I can worsen kidney function during times of illness.
  - Primarily this occurs when the patient develops hypovolemia- due to poor po intake, N/V/D, a GI bleed or overdiuresis.

## Slide 54

- ACE Inhibitor Monitoring
- Monitor serum potassium and BUN/Cr within 5-10 days of initiation or dose increase.  Usually, creatinine will temporarily increase a little as GFR falls, then improves.
- The drug must be stopped if the patient develops a persistent hyperkalemia or increasing serum creatinine (>30%).
- A nephrologist may initiate an ACE inhibitor in more severe CKD than an IM/FP provider.

## Slide 55

- Adverse effects of ACE Inhibitors
- Dry cough: typically occurs 1wk-6mth after initiation of the medication however may occur at any time even if the pt has been tolerating the drug for years
  - More common in women
  - Discontinuation of the medication resolves the cough
- Hyperkalemia: particularly in CKD IV or V and diabetics, also if an aldosterone antagonist (spironolactone or eplerone) is used concurrently.

## Slide 56

- Adverse effects of ACE Inhibitors
- Angioedema: Life Threatening
  - Sudden swelling of the lips, tongue and pharynx.
  - More common in Blacks and Asians.
  - 50% of cases occur w/in 1st week of initiation
  - Treat with Benadryl, Solumedrol and Pepcid. Protect airway and intubate if required
  - Discontinue the medication and never prescribe another ACE-I

## Slide 59

- ARBs  (Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers)
- ARBs are generally preferred over ACE inhibitors because they cause less cough and have a lower risk of angioedema.
- ARBs can be utilized in patients who have a history of ACEI associated angioedema. (use caution and provide education)
- ARBs can be trialed in patients with an ACE associated cough, just counsel them to discontinue the ARB if a persistent cough occurs.

## Slide 60

- ARBs  (Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers)
- Like ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers are contraindicated in pregnancy due to the risk of renal agenesis.
- Clinicians need to monitor their patient’s renal status and potassium when initiating an ARB or after a dosage escalation by obtaining a serum chemistry panel in 5-7 days.

## Slide 61

- Should we combine and ACEI and an ARB?
- Absolutely NOT
- ONTARGET Trial: ONgoing Telmisartan Alone and in combination with Ramipril Global Endpoint Trial
- Prospective, randomized, multicenter, double-blind trial; patients randomized to ramipril, telmisartan, or a combination of both
  - 25,620 patients > age 55 yr with DM & end-organ damage or established atherosclerotic vascular dz
- Combination therapy reduces proteinuria more than monotherapy but worsens major renal outcomes

## Slide 62

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- Calcium channel blockers inhibit the influx of calcium (and possibly sodium) across cardiac and smooth muscle cell membranes.
- This results in:
- Slower electrical impulse conduction through the SA & AV node leading to lower heart rate.
- A decreased in cardiac contractility.
- A decrease in blood pressure due to peripheral arterial vasodilation.

## Slide 63

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- There are two main sub-classes of calcium channel blockers: dihydropyridine CCBs and non-dihydropyridine CCBs
- DHP CCBs act more strongly on peripheral vessels while non-DHPs act more strongly at the heart.
- Both have similar antihypertensive efficacy, but one sub-class is often selected over the other when prescribed for hypertension with consideration for their effects on the heart.

## Slide 64

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- Dihydropyridine CCBs primarily block calcium channels in the peripheral vasculature causing vasodilation.  They are considered heart rate neutral.
- Dihydropyridine CCBs:
- Amlodipine (Norvasc)
- Felodipine (Plendil)
- Nifedipine (Procardia, Adalat)
- Nicardipine (Cardene) IV

## Slide 65

- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
- Non-dihydropyridine CCBs block cardiac calcium channels primarily, with less effect on the peripheral vasculature.  Non DHP CCBs will decrease cardiac contractility (negative inotropic effect) and SA & AV node conduction (negative chronotropic effect).
  - Non- Dihydropyridine CCB: AFIB (indication)
  - Diltiazem (Cardizem, Tiazac, Cartia) PO/IV
  - Verapamil (Calan, Verelan) PO/IV
- [picture]

## Slide 67

  - DHP CCBs Adverse Reactions Amlodipine (Norvasc), Felodipine (Plendil)Nifedipine (Procardia, Adalat)
- DHP CCBs can cause flushing, lightheadedness, dizziness, and HA in 10- 20% of patients.
- DHP CCBs, in their short acting form, can cause reflex tachycardia.  This is because the short acting form stimulates a very sudden and significant drop in blood pressure.  For this reason, short acting forms are very rarely used.

## Slide 68

  - DHP CCBs Adverse Reactions Amlodipine (Norvasc), Felodipine (Plendil)Nifedipine (Procardia, Adalat)
- DHP CCBs increase vascular permeability, which may lead to peripheral edema- this is less common when administered at the same time as a RAAS blocker, but the edema is not usually responsive to diuretics because it is not due to increased plasma volume.
- If significant edema occurs the clinician may need to stop the DHP calcium channel blocker.

## Slide 69

- Non-DHP CCBs Adverse ReactionsVerapamil (Calan, Verelan) or Diltiazem (Cardizem, Tiazac, Cartia)
- Verapamil and to a lesser degree diltiazem will decrease cardiac contractility and heart rate- therefore these drugs should not be used in bradyarrhythmias- such as sick sinus syndrome (SSS), or higher degree heart blocks (2nd/3rd AVB).
- Because of their effect on heart rate, the clinician must be cautious when prescribing non-DHP CCBs with a beta blocker.

## Slide 70

- Non-DHP CCBs Adverse ReactionsVerapamil (Calan, Verelan) or Diltiazem (Cardizem, Tiazac, Cartia)
- In addition, non-DHP CCBs may worsen heart failure and should not be used in acutely decompensated HFrEF.
- Once HF decompensation has been addressed (usually 24 hours of diuresis), non DHP CCBs can be resumed.
- Constipation- especially verapamil (25%)

## Slide 71

- CCB Overdose
- At 5-10x the usual dose, patients develop bradycardia and prolongation of the PR interval in addition to hypotension due to the effects of the CCB, even a DHP CCB, on the AV node.
- Patients develop hyperglycemia as the pancreas is prevented from releasing insulin, although this is rarely clinically significant.

## Slide 72

- CCB Overdose
- Despite hypotension, CCB-poisoned persons may maintain clear mentation, but neurologic status may deteriorate precipitously once their cerebral perfusion becomes critically low- recheck their neuro status frequently!!
- Initially give IVF resuscitation for hypotension, atropine for bradycardia- it may not be effective.
- GI decontamination (AC), administration of IV calcium, glucagon, pressors (norepi) and high dose insulin therapy can all be used.

## Slide 73

- β-Receptors
- Distributed throughout the body and concentrated differently in certain organs & tissues
- β1 receptors: heart, kidney
  - stimulation increases HR, heart contractility, renin release
- β2 receptors: lungs, liver, pancreas, arteriolar smooth muscle
  - stimulation causes bronchodilation and vasodilation
  - mediate insulin secretion and glycogenolysis

## Slide 74

- β-Blockers MOA
- Cardioselective Beta-blockers bind to and block the cardiac beta 1 receptors reducing responsiveness to sympathetic activity (epinephrine/norepinephrine); at higher doses they may also target beta 2 receptors in the lungs
- Non-Cardioselective Beta-blockers bind to and block B1 and B2 receptors.
- Therefore, use caution in the setting of asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), regardless of beta-selectivity profile.

## Slide 75

- Indications for β-Blockers
- Beta blockers are not recommended as first line treatment of hypertension except in the case of a compelling cardiac indication:
    - Post-MI: started after STEMI or NSTEMI, beta blockers are first line
    - Compensated HFrEF and any symptomatic or asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction.  Patients with a decrease in LV EF < 50% are begun on beta blockers
    - CAD and Afib are other indications
- BB have not been shown to reduce risk of stroke or mortality associated with HTN

## Slide 76

- Indications for β-Blockers
- Beta blockers are less effective for hypertension in Black patients than in other ethnicities.
  - But remember that compelling indications trump all else- so beta blockers should be used first line for HTN in a Black patient with prior MI, HF or asymptomatic LV dysfunction.
- Beta blockers are to be used with caution in patients older than 60 years in the absence of compelling indications.

## Slide 77

- β-Blockers Side Effects
- Just like the DHP CCBs, beta blockers may also precipitate heart block or bradyarrhythmia.
- If a patient develops a bradycardic arrhythmia or heart block while on a beta blocker, the drug should be d/c.
- Other side effects associated with b-blockers include reduced sexual function in men (impotence), fatigue, and reduced exercise tolerance.

## Slide 78

- Diabetics and β-Blockers
- When a person becomes hypoglycemic, the hypoglycemia activates the sympathetic nervous system → causing tremors, sweating, increased heart rate and +/- palpitations.
- If a patient is taking a beta blocker, the target receptors of the sympathetic system are blocked.
- Therefore, in patients taking beta blockers, these warning signs of hypoglycemia may be absent, allowing dangerously low glucose levels to develop without patient awareness.

## Slide 79

- Diabetics and β-Blockers
- Beta blockers have an adverse effect on glucose metabolism, because they decrease insulin release from pancreatic beta cells.
- Therefore beta blockers are not recommended for HTN treatment in diabetics- unless there is a compelling indication.

## Slide 80

- Types of β-Blockers
- Cardioselective: Indicated to control tachyarrhythmias, such as atrial fib or sinus tachycardia.  But remember: β1 selectivity is lost at higher doses
  - Bisoprolol (Zebeta)
  - Metoprolol succinate (Toprol XL), once daily dosing
  - Metoprolol tartrate (Lopressor), twice daily dosing
  - Atenolol (Tenormin)

## Slide 81

- Types of β-Blockers
- Non- selective: Inhibit both β1 & β2 receptors at all doses, often not used for hypertension.
- Instead, non-selective BB are commonly used for migraine prophylaxis, anxiety, essential tremor, portal hypertension and thyrotoxicosis.
    - Nadolol (Corgard)
    - Propranolol (Inderal)
    - Timolol (Biocadren)

## Slide 82

- Types of β-Blockers
- Intrinsic Sympathomimetic Activity: Partial β-receptor agonists, do not reduce resting heart rate or cardiac output.
- ISAs have a niche place in therapy, possibly patients with bradycardia in need of BB, but they are contraindicated post-MI or in high-risk patients.
- Not as cardioprotective as a selective BB.
- Acebutolol (Sectral)

## Slide 83

- β-Blockers
- Beta blockers must be started at a low dose and  titrated up cautiously in persons with LV dysfunction and HFrEF due to the drug’s depressive effects on myocardial contractility and heart rate.
- Beta blockers should not be abruptly discontinued, if possible, since this may cause rebound HTN, worsening angina or MI.  If able, slowly wean the beta blocker dose down over 1-2 weeks.

## Slide 84

- β-Blocker Overdose
- Findings of beta blocker overdose include hypotension,  bradyarrhythmia, confusion, hyperkalemia and hypoglycemia.  Profound myocardial depression and cardiogenic shock are sometimes seen.
- Propanolol and carvedilol are the most lipophilic of the beta blockers and because they can cross the BBB easily, will therefore cause the most neurotoxicity, precipitating seizures and delirium.

## Slide 85

- β-Blocker Overdose
- BB overdose is treated with IV fluids and atropine initially.
- Additional treatments include
  - IV calcium: increases heart rate
  - IV glucagon: augments cardiac contractility
  - Vasopressors
  - High dose IV insulin and glucose: insulin improves cardiac contractility
  - Lipid emulsion: surrounds lipophilic meds rendering them ineffective

## Slide 86

- Mixed α1- & β-blockers
- These drugs block β receptors which decreases HR and myocardial oxygen demand as well as blocking α1 receptors causing peripheral vasodilation.
- Carvedilol has been shown to reduce mortality in patients with systolic dysfunction and HFrEF.
- Adverse effects: Lowers standing BP more than supine BP, so the additional blockade produces more orthostatic hypotension than a BB alone.

## Slide 87

- Mixed α1- & β-blockers
- Carvedilol (Coreg): Frequently used for BP lowering in patients with HFrEF, has been shown to have mortality benefit.
- Considered to have less of an effect on glucose metabolism than a straight BB.
- Labetalol (Trandate):
  - Used as an oral med for BP
  - Frequently given IV prn to control BP or when pts cannot take their usual dose of oral BB.
  - Used in pregnancy- lots of experience, safe.

## Slide 88

- α1-Blockers
- Terazosin (Hytrin)
- Doxazosin (Cardura)
- Prazosin (Minipress)
- Not appropriate monotherapy for HTN but are often used when both HTN and comorbid BPH needs to be treated.
- Blockade of α1 causes peripheral vasodilation of veins and arteries leading to BP lowering; these drugs also block α1 receptors on the prostate, allowing for easier urine flow

## Slide 89

- α1-Blockers
- Dosed at bedtime to limit the effect of orthostasis
- Adverse effects:
  - Orthostatic hypotension: especially in older patients
  - 1st dose phenomenon: transient dizziness, faintness, palpitations, syncope within 1 to 3 hours of the 1st dose
  - Edema, may give with a diuretic, but monitor for worsening orthostasis

## Slide 90

- Central α2-Agonists
- Clonidine (Catapres) PO or Patch
- Methyldopa (Aldomet)
- Guanfacine (Tenex)
- These drugs stimulate α2-adrenergic receptors in the brain
  - Reduces sympathetic outflow from the CNS and increases vagal tone
  - Peripheral stimulation of presynaptic α2-receptors may further reduce sympathetic tone

## Slide 91

- Central α2-Agonists
- Methyldopa: one of the safest drugs to use in pregnant hypertensives.  Rarely used otherwise.
- Clonidine:
  - Compliance issues: PO bid-tid; may be given by patch once weekly ($$$).
  - ESRD: safe in renal failure and fairly strong- used in ESRD pts with severe HTN.
  - Alcohol withdrawal: directly counteracts the withdrawal symptoms mediated by increased sympathetic CNS tone.

## Slide 92

- Central α2-Agonists: Adverse Effects
- Abrupt discontinuation may cause rebound HTN (clonidine), this is a significant risk.
- Orthostatic hypotension
- Bradycardia
- Dizziness, drowsiness, HA and fatigue
- Anticholinergic side effects (clonidine)- dry mouth, constipation, flushing, abdominal pain from a slow gut.
- Sodium/water retention leading to edema

## Slide 93

- Central α2-Agonists Overdose
- Overdose with tablets becomes symptomatic in one hour, OD with patch maybe longer.
- Clonidine overdose presents like an opioid overdose with pinpoint pupils, depressed LOC, hypotension, coma and respiratory depression.
- Patients also develop hypothermia.
- Treatment with naloxone, IV fluids, atropine, pacing, and vasopressors.

## Slide 94

- Direct Arterial Vasodilators
- Directly relaxes arterial smooth muscle causing an antihypertensive effect with little/no venous vasodilation.
- Hydralazine: commonly used IV but also used PO to control BP, is heart rate neutral.  May be used as part of a combination of drugs in a pts with heart failure.   Also used for ESRD patients, safe in CKD/ AKI.
- Minoxidil: often used for renal failure patients with difficult to control hypertension.

## Slide 95

- Direct Arterial Vasodilators: Adverse Effects
- Sodium/water retention causing edema, may use with diuretic (loop) in order to minimize
- Angina, caution in patients with CAD, may cause vascular steal.
- Reflex tachycardia due to excessive vasodilation.
- Hydralazine is known to cause drug induced lupus.

- Excessive hair growth with minoxidil.

- Balance issues, urinary incontinence, SSRIs, gout or hyponatremia avoid thiazide diuretics

## Slide 104

- Resistant Hypertension
- Resistant hypertension is defined as hypertension that is not controlled to goal using the maximum tolerated dosage of three anti-hypertensives, one of which is a thiazide diuretic.
- The most widely used three-drug combination for hypertension is an ACEI + CCB + thiazide or an ARB + CCB + thiazide, which will control BP in 80% of patients.

## Slide 105

- Resistant Hypertension
- If BP is not controlled by adding a third drug, ensure medication compliance, monitor BP at home, and assure no use of another med/ intake that would cause resistant HTN.
- Add a 4th drug: an aldosterone antagonist, b-blocker, centrally acting agent, a-blocker, or a direct vasodilator.
- Consider referral to a HTN specialist and/or a work-up for secondary HTN.
