## Slide 3

- Approaching the Cardiovascular Disease History
- Is the blood supply to the heart adequate?
- Is the electrical system of the heart functioning normally?
- Is the heart adequately moving blood through circulation and supplying the organs?

## Slide 4

- Common Symptoms of Cardiovascular Disease
- Angina (chest pain)
- Palpitations
- Shortness of breath: dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
- Edema
- Peripheral vascular (pain, skin changes, hair changes, etc.)
- Syncope
- Abdominal, back, flank pain

## Slide 5

- Angina (Chest Pain)
- One of the most serious of all patient complaints
- Patients with ACS (acute coronary syndrome) may present with classic symptoms of angina
  - Classic symptoms include exertional pain, pressure, or discomfort in chest
  - However, females (particularly those over the age of 65) are more likely to report atypical symptoms that may go unrecognized
    - Upper back pain, neck pain, jaw pain, shortness of breath, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, nausea, vomiting, and/or fatigue
  - Careful history taking is especially important

## Slide 6

- Angina (Chest Pain)
- Begin with open ended questions
  - “Please tell me about any symptoms you might be having in your chest.”
  - Ask the patient to point to the pain
- Then start to ask more associated questions as you start to relate to more likely diagnoses
  - Does the pain get worse or better with exertion?
  - How would you characterize the pain?
  - Does it radiate into your shoulder, back, or down your arm?
  - Do you have SOB, sweating, palpitations, or nausea?
  - Does it ever wake you up at night?

## Slide 7

- An unpleasant awareness of the heartbeat
- Patients use various terms to describe palpitations:
  - Skipping
  - Racing
  - Fluttering
  - Pounding
  - Stopping of the heart
- May be irregular, rapidly slow down or accelerate, or arise from increased forcefulness of cardiac contraction
- May ask the patient to tap out the rhythm they experience with a hand or a finger (or investigate their smart watch!)
- Palpitations

## Slide 8

- Clues in the history (need EKG to confirm):
  - Transient skips and flip-flops: possible premature contractions
  - Rapid regular beating of sudden onset and offset: possible paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia
  - Rapid regular rate of <120 beats per minute, especially if gradually starting and stopping: possible sinus tachycardia
- Palpitations

## Slide 9

- Shortness of Breath (SOB)
- Dyspnea: an uncomfortable awareness of breathing that is inappropriate to a given level of exertion
  - Common in cardiac or pulmonary problems
- Orthopnea: dyspnea that occurs when the patient is supine and improves when the patient sits up
  - Do they sleep with lots of pillows at night?
- Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND): episodes of sudden dyspnea and orthopnea that awaken the patient from sleep, usually 1-2 hours after going to bed, prompting the patient to sit up, stand up, or go to a window for air

## Slide 10

- Accumulation of excess fluid in the extravascular interstitial space
- Focus on the location (unilateral vs. bilateral), timing, and setting of the swelling and any associated symptoms
- Consider asking patients who retain fluid to record daily morning weights because edema may not be obvious until several liters of extra fluid have accumulated
- Causes are frequently cardiac or pulmonary but can also be nutritional and/or positional
- Edema (Swelling)

## Slide 11

- Peripheral Vascular
- Pain or cramping in legs with exertion (clarify how far they can walk before pain comes on) or at rest
  - Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)
- Associated edema
  - Deep venous thromboembolism (DVT)
  - Venous insufficiency
- Coldness, numbness, pallor, or discoloration of extremities or hair loss particularly over anterior tibial surfaces
  - Decreased arterial perfusion

## Slide 12

- Transient loss of consciousness followed by recovery
- Most common cause is neurocardiogenic
  - Vasovagal syncope (most common), postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), carotid sinus syncope, and orthostatic hypotension
  - Cardiac arrhythmias: especially ventricular tachycardia and brady arrhythmias
- Syncope

## Slide 13

- Abdominal, Back, Flank Pain
- Acute symptoms raise suspicion of arterial thrombosis
- May also be related to oxygen supply-demand mismatch (i.e., pain worse with eating when abdominal viscera needs greater oxygen supply)
- Sudden tearing pain suggests concern for abdominal aortic dissection

- Chronic Care Questions

- Current concerns:  Does the patient have any concerns or problems regarding/relating to their chronic condition they were hoping to discuss today?

  - Heart failure: home weights

## Slide 25

- Risk factors:  What are their risk factors that may worsen their condition or increase their likelihood of further complications from this chronic condition?
- Associated symptoms:  Are they having any new symptoms related to their chronic condition?
  - Specifically ask about symptoms including headaches, vision changes, chest pain/pressure, shortness of breath, difficulty sleeping, fatigue, leg swelling or pain, etc.

## Slide 42

- Assess Skin
- Palpate for lower extremity edema by pressing firmly but gently with your thumb for 1-2 seconds over the dorsum of each foot, behind each medial malleolus, and over the anterior tibia
- Look for pitting: a depression caused by pressure from your thumb
  - No pitting = normal
  - 1+ = barely detectable impression (< 2 mm) when finger is pressed into skin
  - 2+ = slight indentation (2-4 mm), 15 seconds to rebound
  - 3+ = deeper indentation (4-6 mm), 30 seconds to rebound
  - 4+ = very deep indentation (6-8 mm), > 30 seconds to rebound

## Slide 44

- Identify Point of Maximal Impulse (PMI)
- Inspect the left anterior chest for a visible PMI
- Using your finger pads, palpate at the apex for the PMI
- Locate the PMI by intercostal space (usually the 5th or 4th, gives vertical location) and the distance in centimeters from the midclavicular line (gives horizontal location)
  - Assess the diameter of the PMI: should occupy one interspace and be less than 2.5 cm
  - Assess the amplitude and duration of the PMI: should be brisk and non-sustained

## Slide 45

- Identify Point of Maximal Impulse (PMI)
- The PMI may be:
  - Tapping: normal
  - Forceful and terminates quickly: suggests hyperkinesis from hypermetabolic states or aortic regurgitation
  - Diffuse and/or displaced: suggests a dilated ventricle from heart failure or cardiomyopathy

## Slide 54

- S1 Heart Sound
- Generated by the closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves
- Identified during the cardiac cycle by auscultation of the heart while palpating the carotid pulse
  - S1 falls just before the carotid upstroke
  - S2 follows the carotid upstroke
- Best heard with the diaphragm of the stethoscope
- Best heard in the left fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line at the PMI

## Slide 55

- S2 Heart Sound
- Generated by the closure of the aortic (A2) and pulmonic valves (P2)
- Best heard with the diaphragm of the stethoscope over the left second intercostal space close to the sternal border
- Intensity of the S2 heart sound is usually single during expiration
- Separation of A2 and P2 may occur during inspiration, this is referred to as the “physiological split”

## Slide 57

- S3 Heart Sound
- Also called the ventricular diastolic gallop
- Low-pitched sound occurring at the beginning of diastole shortly after S2
- Best heard at the cardiac apex with the bell and with the patient in the left lateral decubitus position
- Produced by blood entering the ventricle during the rapid filling phase of diastole
  - Heard in hyperdynamic states, where it likely results from rapid early diastolic filling
- Maneuvers that increase venous return accentuate S3 and maneuvers that decrease venous return soften S3

## Slide 58

- S4 Heart Sound
- Also called the atrial diastolic gallop
- Low-pitched sound occurring at the end of diastole immediately before S1
- Best heard at the cardiac apex with the bell of the stethoscope
- Produced by the sound of blood being forced into a stiff or hypertrophic ventricle
  - Heard in left ventricular hypertrophy, (i.e., hypertension, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), acute myocardial ischemia, and hyperkinetic states
- Frequently, S4 is also palpable at the cardiac apex

## Slide 61

- Estimate Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP)
- Reflects right atrial pressure, which, in turn, equals central venous pressure and right ventricular end-diastolic pressure
- Best estimated from the right internal jugular vein, which has the most direct channel into the right atrium
- Find the highest point of oscillation in the right internal jugular vein (near where it appears to then collapse)

## Slide 63

- Estimate Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP)
- Raise the head of the bed or examining table to about 30°, turn the patient’s head slightly away from the side you are inspecting, and ensure they are comfortable to relax the SCM muscles
- Use tangential lighting and examine both sides of the neck to identify the internal jugular venous pulsations
- If necessary, raise or lower the head of the bed until you can see the oscillation point or meniscus of the internal jugular venous pulsations in the lower half of the neck

## Slide 64

- Estimate Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP)
- Look for pulsations in the suprasternal notch, between the attachments of the SCM muscle on the sternum and clavicle, or just posterior to the SCM
- Distinguish the pulsations of the internal jugular vein from those of the carotid artery

## Slide 65

- Estimate Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP)
- Identify the highest point of pulsation in the right jugular vein
- Extend a long rectangular object horizontally from this point and a centimeter ruler vertically from the sternal angle, making an exact right angle
- Measure the vertical distance in centimeters above the sternal angle where the horizontal object crosses the ruler
- Add to this distance 5 cm (the distance from the sternal angle to the center of the right atrium) to get the JVP
  - Normal is 6 to 8 cm

## Slide 67

- Palpate Pulses
- Use the pads of your second and third fingers and compare bilaterally
  - Temporal, carotid, brachial, radial, ulnar, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibialis, dorsalis pedis
- Use the entire hand to palpate the aortic pulse by pressing deeply in the upper abdomen, slightly left of midline
- Grade pulses based on the amplitude
  - 3+ = bounding
  - 2+ = brisk, expected (normal)
  - 1+ = diminished, weak
  - 0 = absent, cannot palpate

## Slide 75

- Special Tests: Allen Test
- Compares patency of ulnar and radial arteries
  - Performed before an arterial blood gas (ABG) to ensure patency
- Ask patient to make tight fist with one hand
- Compress radial and ulnar arteries firmly between thumb and fingers
- Ask patient to open hand into relaxed, slightly flexed position
- Release compression on radial artery and look for blood return to hand (3-5 seconds)
- Repeat compression of both arteries again, but the second time, release the ulnar artery compression

## Slide 77

- Special Tests: Thigh and Calf Circumference
- Used to evaluate for edema and occlusion
- Measure 15 cm from the proximal patella (thigh) and 15 cm from the distal patella (calf) and compare bilaterally
  - > 1 cm difference is abnormal

## Slide 78

- Special Tests: Thigh Tenderness
- Suggests venous insufficiency or large deep venous thrombosis (DVT)
- With an appropriate pressure, squeeze the back of the thigh for tenderness
- Repeat bilaterally

## Slide 79

- Special Tests: Homan Sign
- Not very sensitive or specific, but can help determine next step in diagnosing DVT
- Dorsiflex foot and palpate calf for tenderness
- Repeat bilaterally

## Slide 80

- Special Tests: Stemmer Sign
- Assess for lymphedema
- Pinch and lift skin fold at base of second toe and second finger
- If you cannot pinch the skin of the dorsum of the foot or hand, then this positive finding is associated with lymphedema

## Slide 81

- Special Tests: Lower Extremity Arterial Insufficiency Test
- Assess for arterial insufficiency in lower extremities, such as in peripheral arterial disease (PAD)
- Patient begins by laying supine on the examination table
- Examiner raises both legs to 60° until maximal pallor of the feet develops (usually within 30-60 seconds)
- Examiner lowers legs as patient sits up, so legs dangle down over the table
- Compare both feet, noting the time required for return of color to the feet
  - Normal is < 10 seconds

## Slide 82

- Special Tests: Ankle Brachial Index
- Tests for subclinical or suspected peripheral arterial disease (PAD)
- Quick and noninvasive test
- Detects stenosis of 50% or more in major vessels of legs

## Slide 83

- Special Tests: Ankle Brachial Index
- Compares lower extremity systolic BP to upper extremity systolic BP using Doppler ultrasonography
  - Right ABI = highest pressure obtained in right foot divided by the highest pressure obtained in either arm
  - Left ABI = highest pressure obtained in left foot divided by the highest pressure obtained in either arm

## Slide 89

- Cardiovascular Cultural Considerations
- Minorities have been shown to receive inferior healthcare in areas such as referral for cardiac diagnostics and care for heart failure (among others)
- Minorities also have less health insurance coverage, less healthcare access, more acute and chronic illnesses, and lower life expectancies
- There may also be less effective communication with ethnic minorities
- For groups who have been historically marginalized based on their race or ethnicity, mistrust in the healthcare system may be borne out of experiences of discrimination

## Slide 90

- Cardiovascular Cultural Considerations
- Studies have shown that compared with non-Hispanic white patients, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic patients are more likely to report mistrust in health professionals
- Researchers found a strong association between perceived discrimination and medical mistrust as well as not having a consistent health professional correlated with greater levels of mistrust
- Drawing on previous research, researchers suggest that this mistrust is due to implicit racial and ethnic bias in health professionals
- Solution is multilevel community- and theory-based training models to counter implicit bias

## Slide 91

- Cardiovascular Cultural Considerations
- Ethnic groups from Asia or South Asia may report feeling like their “heart is empty” or “there is a fire elevation” in their chest to describe emotionally upsetting conditions not necessarily related to cardiac symptoms
- Eliciting a history may require a “talk story” (Hawaiian term): chat and converse about patients’ lives to establish rapport before asking medical symptoms (which may give you valuable family history or social history)
- The lifestyle of certain ethnic groups may increase risk of coronary artery disease

## Slide 92

- Cardiovascular Cultural Considerations
- The cardiac exam involves exposing the chest, requiring protection of patients’ modesty
- Be cognizant of patients’ ethnic or personal background when asking permission to expose patients for the exam
  - Keep in mind that private or erotic zones may differ among cultural groups and require additional sensitivity and respect to preserve the dignity and comfort of patients
  - Also consider the differences in “personal space” among various ethnic groups

## Slide 93

- Cardiovascular Cultural Considerations
- Patients may request examination by the same gender if possible
  - If not possible, consider having a nurse of the same gender or family member present for the exam (with permission of course) to ease the patient’s discomfort
- Patients have the right to refuse any exam, which then must be documented accordingly
