## Slide 16

- [picture]
- The Physiologic Split of S2
- Normal Variant
- At times, you may hear a split S2
  - NORMAL if it’s during inspiration only
  - Pathological if Fixed, Wide, or paradoxical
- Negative pressure in chest during inspiration  a bit more blood to right side of heart  aortic valve closes on time, but pulmonic valve closes slightly after
- Tip: Ask patient to hold their breath while you listen… what would happen to a physiologically split S2?

## Slide 20

- [picture]
- S3 – gallop rhythm
- Lub Dub Ta
- “Kentucky”
- Early diastole
- If the ventricles fill up quickly, the fluid waves vibrate the ventricular walls
- Bell is best, quiet room, hold breath
            - Indicates volume overload: i.e., CHF, DCM
            - However, likely normal in an athletic heart or pregnancy or young patients

## Slide 22

- [picture]
- S4 – gallop rhythm
- Late diastole
- Atria contracting against stiff or non-compliant ventricles
- Bell is best, quiet room, hold breath
            - Indicates pressure overload: i.e., severe HTN, HCM, AS
            - Always pathological
- Ta Lub Dub
- “Tennessee”

## Slide 24

| Feature | S3 | S4 |
| Timing | Early diastole (just after S2) | Late diastole (just before S1) |
| Mechanism | Rapid filling → ventricular wall vibration | Atrial contraction against stiff ventricle |
| Cadence | “Lub-Dub-Ta” → “Kentucky” | “Ta-Lub-Dub” → “Tennessee” |
| Normal in… | Athletes, pregnancy, youth | Rarely normal |
| Pathologic in… | CHF, DCM | HTN, aortic stenosis, HCM |
| Key Idea | Too much blood (volume) | Too stiff ventricle (pressure) |

## Slide 28

- [picture]
- Mid-Systolic Click
- Classic for mitral valve prolapse
- Often followed by a late systolic murmur (MR)
- Sound of the mitral leaflet suddenly snapping back into the atrium, as it’s stopped by the chordae tendineae
- Standing/Valsalva – click is earlier, murmur longer
- Squatting – click is later, murmur is shorter

## Slide 30

- [picture]
- Opening Snap
- High-pitched, early-mid diastole
- The snap is the sound we hear as blood forces open a stenotic AV valve
- This is classic for mitral stenosis (but can also be tricuspid)
- Typically followed by a ‘diastolic rumble’ as the blood squeezes through the small opening

## Slide 47

- Further descriptions of murmurs
- Quality may be described as:
- Blowing (usually regurgitant murmurs)
- Harsh (usually stenotic murmurs)
- Rumbling (low pitched diastolic murmurs)
- Musical/Vibratory (innocent or Still’s murmur)
- Scratchy/Rub (pericardial friction rub)

## Slide 48

- Further descriptions of murmurs
- Radiation:
- Some well-known murmurs project sound along the blood flow
- To carotids: aortic stenosis
- To left axilla: mitral regurgitation
- Augmentation:
- Some murmurs are classically increased or decreased by certain maneuvers, such as:
- Squatting
- Standing
- Valsalva
- Leaning forward

## Slide 53

- Aortic Stenosis (AS)
- [picture]
- Systolic murmur
- Classically described as Harsh, high-pitched, crescendo-decrescendo
- Systolic Ejection click present in early stenosis
- When severe, may cause paradoxical split S2
- Best at the right 2nd intercostal space (RUSB) and radiates to the carotids
- Loudest with squatting, softer with Valsalva

## Slide 54

- Pulmonic Stenosis (PS)
- [picture]
- Systolic murmur
- Classically described as Harsh, high-pitched, crescendo-decrescendo
- Systolic Ejection Click may be present
- Best heard at the left 2nd intercostal space (LUSB)
- May radiate to left neck or left shoulder
- Wide Split S2 may be present

## Slide 55

- Mitral Regurgitation (MR)
- Systolic murmur
- Classically described as Blowing, high-pitched, holosystolic
- Best heard at apex and radiates to the left axilla
- Associated with S3 in severe cases
- [picture]

## Slide 56

- Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP)
- Systolic murmur
- Classically described as mid-systolic click, ± late systolic murmur
- Standing/valsalva shifts the click/murmur earlier in systole, but squatting shifts it later
- Best heard at apex
- All MVP can lead to MR, but not all MR is MVP

## Slide 57

- Tricuspid Regurgitation (TR)
- Systolic murmur
- Classically described as Blowing, high-pitched, holosystolic
- Best heard at LLSB
- Can also lead to an S3 in severe cases
- Carvallo’s  sign: Murmur becomes louder during inspiration

## Slide 59

- Aortic Regurgitation (AR)
- Diastolic murmur
- Classically described as blowing, high-pitched, early diastolic decrescendo
- Best at the left 2-4th ICS and may radiate to apex
- Augment: pt leaning forward, exhale & hold
- https://youtu.be/uZysrKXHJMM?feature=shared
- Pulmonic Regurgitation (PR)
- Diastolic murmur; Similar blowing, high-pitched, early diastolic decrescendo
- But gets louder with inspiration

## Slide 60

- Mitral Stenosis
- Diastolic murmur
- Classically described as Rumble, Low-pitched with an Opening Snap after S2
- Best heard at apex (with bell)
- No radiation
- Augment: left lateral decubitus positioning
- *Tricuspid stenosis (rare) may produce similar sounds but loudest at LLSB and with inspiration; no radiation

## Slide 63

- Patent Ductus Arteriosus
- Systolic + Diastolic murmur
- Classically described as Harsh, continuous, machinery sound
- Crescendo during systole, decrescendo during diastole
- Best heard at the pulmonic position
- Congenital - newborns
- [picture]
- [picture]

## Slide 64

- Benign (pediatric) murmurs
- Innocent murmur: Still’s murmur (most common)
- Systolic murmur, described as soft, musical or vibratory
- Loudest at left sternal border/apex and supine; disappears with standing
- Venus Hum:
- Systolic + Diastolic murmur
- Classically described as a continuous, soft humming murmur
- Louder right supraclavicular and sitting up, disappears supine or compressing jugular veins/head rotated
- [picture]

## Slide 65

- Pericardial Friction Rub
- Systolic + Diastolic murmur
- Classically described as scratchy, high-pitched; mono-, bi-, or tri-phasic
- Best heard at LSB, when leaning forward
- Increases with expiration
- [picture]

## Slide 66

- Ventricular Septal Defect
- Systolic murmur
- Classically described as Holosystolic, harsh/blowing
- Best heard at 3-5th ICS
- Due to congenital condition where the ventricular septum fails to close normally and blood is shunted from the left ventricle directly into the right ventricle
