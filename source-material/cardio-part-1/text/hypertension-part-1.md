## Slide 4

- HTN: GLOBAL EPIDEMIOLOGY
- Hypertension is a global problem and is the leading cause of cardiovascular disease and premature death worldwide
  - Affects > 1 billion people worldwide
    - Highest prevalence of HTN in Africa (27%)
    - Lowest prevalence of HTN in Americas (18%)
  - Accounts for more than 9.4 million deaths/year worldwide

  - Less than 50% who have HTN are treated
  - 1 in 5 have HTN under control

## Slide 7

- Do US citizens have control?

- 1 of every 5 adults are unaware that they have HTN and another 10% know they have hypertension and chose not to treat it.

## Slide 13

- Normal Hemodynamics
- Maintaining mean arterial pressure (MAP) between 70-100mmHg is required to provide a constant flow of blood to all of the body tissues, delivering oxygen and nutrients to the tissues and removing CO2 and waste
- This helps push the blood from the arteries into the capillaries
- In the capillaries, gas exchange, nutrients and waste occur
- MAP = CO X total peripheral resistance (TPR)

## Slide 14

- Normal Systole Hemodynamics
- During systole (ventricular ejection), the pressure (force of blood) on the arteries increases as the heart pumps blood into the arterial system, accounting for the higher systolic BP than diastolic BP.
- As the pressure increases, the elastic walls of the arteries stretch (compliance).  This can be felt as a pulse in the arteries

## Slide 15

- In diastole, there is recoil of the elastic arteries. The diastolic pressure generated is the remaining force of blood on the walls of the artery when the heart is relaxing and recorded as the minimum pressure just before the start of systole.

## Slide 17

- Important Terms
- Preload
- Pre-Contraction load. The load on cardiac musculature, the stretch, caused by blood entering the heart from the venous system, PRIOR to heart contracting. Measured as end diastolic volume (EDV)
- Inotrope
- A medicine that changes the force of contraction (“Ino” meaning “fiber” or muscle fiber and “tropic” meaning “turning” or “changing”) A muscle fiber changing medication.
- Chronotrope
- A medicine that changes the rate of contraction (“chrono” as in “chronological” or timing) A “time changing” medication
- Afterload
- The amount of pressure that the heart needs to exert to eject the blood during ventricular contraction. This is the systolic blood pressure.

## Slide 18

- Normal BP Physiology
- BP regulation is very complex, depending on:
- Cardiac output: the amount of blood pumped from the heart every minute (SV x HR), which is affected by sodium intake, renal function, mineralocorticoids and sympathetic/parasympathetic nervous system.
- Peripheral vascular resistance: the resistance to arterial distention dependent upon the sympathetic nervous system, humoral factors, and local vasculature autoregulation.

## Slide 21

- Normal Blood Pressure Regulation
- Short-term regulation of BP (minutes- hours) is neural.
  - The medulla and pons in the deep brain integrate autonomic input from carotid and aortic baroreceptors (stretch receptors)  & chemoreceptors (sampling O2, CO2 and H+)
- Baroreceptors sense arterial pressure indirectly from the degree of stretch of the elastic arterial walls
  - Increased stretch will increase action potential frequency to signal increased pressure to the medullary cardiovascular center in the medulla oblongata
  - Increased input simultaneously inhibits the sympathetic nervous system and triggers the parasympathetic nervous system
  - The opposite occurs with decreased stretch, activating the sympathetic nervous system

## Slide 24

- Normal Blood Pressure Regulation
- Long term control of the BP is humoral:
- RAAS activation increases the vascular pressure and promotes sodium and water retention by the kidney and is regulated by 3 methods:
  - Indirect activation of renal sympathetic nerve fibers in response to the carotid and aortic baroreceptors sensing low stretch. This leads to NE release acting upon β1 adrenergic receptors of juxtaglomerular cells, causing release of renin.
  - Baroreceptors in the juxtaglomerular cells which sense decreased volume and pressure in the afferent arteriole the same as in the carotids. This decreased pressure allow for increased renin secretion.
  - Macula densa, which is found in the thick ascending limb, will sense sodium concentration via Tubulo-glomerular feedback
  - Either low sodium or low pressure will stimulate release of renin

## Slide 25

- Normal Blood Pressure Regulation
- Tubulo-glomerular feedback refers to the ability of the kidney to regulate the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in response to the solute concentration in the distal renal tubule.
- Tubulo-glomerular feedback:
- When GFR (sodium concentration) is moderately increased, the macula densa releases ATP causing afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction, thereby reducing GFR and allowing for sodium reabsorption to occur.
- Low tubular sodium at the macula densa releases prostaglandins causing vasodilation and increased filtration.

## Slide 28

- Normal Blood Pressure RegulationOther Regulators
- Peripheral Chemoreceptors- located in common carotid artery and aortic arch. Stimulated by decreasing arterial PO2, leading to arteriolar vasoconstriction in the skeletal muscle, renal and splanchnic vascular beds
- Central Chemoreceptors- located in the medulla. Stimulated by change in the arterial PCO2 and the cerebrospinal fluid pH leading to sympathetic outflow and arteriolar vasoconstriction in several organs
- Volume Receptors- located on the venous side. Sensitive to blood volume. Increase stretch leads to decreased ADH, increased atrial natriuretic peptide (causing vasodilation) and decreased RAAS.

## Slide 30

- Low BP Physiology
- As BP falls, neural mechanisms (short term regulation) will kick in to compensate for lower BP and preserve essential organ perfusion.  Humoral mechanisms will also activate to provide a slower but more durable correction of BP.
- The MAP or mean arterial pressure can be calculated with a clinical calculator: [(2x DBP) + SBP]/3.  MAP is used clinically to determine the adequacy of BP following an intervention for hypotension.  A “good” MAP is between 70 and 100.

## Slide 34

- Pathophysiology of Primary HTN
- Multifactorial and complex.  Possible mechanisms include:
  - Sympathetic nervous system hyperactivity: over-activity of the sympathetic tone leads to increased vascular tone and HTN.
  - Genetics: HTN is twice as common in subjects who have 1-2 hypertensive parents and multiple epidemiologic studies support this conclusion.

## Slide 35

- Pathophysiology of Primary HTN
- Multifactorial and complex.  Possible mechanisms include:
  - Reduced adult nephron mass may predispose to hypertension, may be related to genetic factors, fetal and lifetime events.
  - Sodium intake: increased salt intake triggers an increase in blood pressure followed by increased sodium excretion in urine normally by natriuretic peptides.  In primary HTN this process may be disordered.

## Slide 36

- Pathophysiology of Primary HTN
- Multifactorial and complex.  Possible mechanisms include:
  - Immunologic: HTN is associated with renal infiltration of immune cells. Pharmacological immunosuppression decreases BP.
  - Cardiac malformation: abnormal development of aortic elasticity or reduced development of the microvascular capillary network in fetal life (prematurity) causes adult onset HTN.

## Slide 37

- Non-Modifiable Risk Factors for HTN
- Genetics: Hypertension is twice as common in persons with 1-2 first degree relatives with HTN
- African American race: On average African Americans develop hypertension about 10 years prior to Caucasians, is more severe, and associated with more complications than other ethnic groups
- Males
- Psychosocial stress- Personality traits (hostile attitude, impatience), and depression.
- Low socioeconomic status
- Age: advancing age increases the risk, particularly of systolic hypertension.

## Slide 38

- Modifiable Risk Factors for HTN
- Obesity:  associated with an increase in intravascular volume, elevated cardiac output, increased sympathetic outflow, and activation of the renin-angiotensin system. Weight reduction lowers BP modestly.
- Diabetes and dyslipidemia: Both cardiovascular risk factors themselves, these diseases also increase the risk of developing hypertension.
- CKD: any decrease in adult nephron mass increases the risk.  The higher the stage of CKD, the more likely the patient is to have hypertension, and often, difficult to control HTN.

## Slide 39

- Modifiable Risk Factors for HTN
- Excessive alcohol intake: Greater than 2 drinks/day will increase the risk of developing HTN.
  - raises BP, perhaps by increasing plasma catecholamines. HTN can be difficult to control in patients who consume more than 40 g of ethanol (two drinks) daily or those who drink in "binges."
- High sodium, low potassium diet: excessive sodium intake increases the risk of HTN, and sodium restriction lowers BP
  - increased salt probably increases BP in some individuals so dietary salt restriction of 2 gm per day is recommended in patients with hypertension.  Low serum potassium level may also contribute to hypertension.  Intake of 90 mmol/day of potassium is recommended.

## Slide 40

- Modifiable Risk Factor for HTN
- Lifestyle: Weight gain and obesity, sedentary lifestyle and lack of physical activity will all increase the risk of developing HTN
  - Aerobic exercise lowers BP in previously sedentary individuals, but increasingly strenuous exercise in already active subjects has less effect.
- Smoking raises BP by increasing plasma norepinephrine.
  - Synergistic effects of smoking and high blood pressure on cardiovascular risk are well documented.
- Insufficient sleep: <7hr nightly associated with HTN

## Slide 41

- Polycythemia: increases blood viscosity raises blood pressure.
- NSAIDs produce increases in BP averaging 5mm Hg and are best avoided in patients with borderline or elevated blood pressures.
- Other Contributors to Primary HTN

## Slide 44

- Secondary HTN Classifications
- In 5% of cases, the elevated blood pressure is secondary to another identifiable illness which causes HTN.
- Often found in those diagnosed prior to age 20 or after age 50.
- Although these cases constitute a small part of overall hypertension, these disorders account for a considerable amount (20%) of resistant HTN.
  - Resistant HTN: BP that remains above goal despite treatment with maximal meds from 3 drug classes, including a thiazide diuretic

## Slide 47

- Secondary Hypertension EtiologiesRenovascular Hypertension
- When renal artery stenosis occurs, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is constantly activated by the kidney that has low blood flow.
- High levels of plasma renin causes conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, causing severe arteriolar vasoconstriction and aldosterone release-> plasma volume expansion and elevated BP.

## Slide 49

- Secondary Hypertension EtiologiesRenovascular Hypertension
- Volume expansion and increasing the BP don’t solve the low blood flow problem to the affected kidney though, because there is a fixed obstruction- the stenosis.
- Constant high BP increases cardiac work and causes remodeling of the heart and blood vessels.  It also stresses the normal kidney by creating very high vascular pressures.

## Slide 50

- Secondary Hypertension EtiologiesRenovascular Hypertension
- Suspect renal artery stenosis if:
  - Acute kidney injury develops with initiation of an ACEI/ARB- always check a chemistry panel within a week of drug initiation.
  - Refractory HTN occurs in a patient with risk factors for renal artery stenosis.
  - The patient has recurrent flash pulmonary edema.
  - An abdominal bruit is noted over the renal artery on exam.

## Slide 59

- Secondary HTN Etiologies
- Pheochromocytoma
  - A pheochromocytoma is a rare tumor of the adrenal medulla.
  - Usually benign, the tumor is made of cells that synthesize catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine) and the tumor will often release the catecholamines in an uncontrolled and sudden fashion.
  - This causes paroxysms of very high blood pressure, palpitations, diaphoresis and pallor when it dumps catecholamines into the bloodstream unexpectedly.

## Slide 60

- Secondary HTN Etiologies
- Pheochromocytoma
  - The patient will have normal to modestly elevated BP most of the time, but can have very severe and sudden elevations of blood pressure leading to flash pulmonary edema, MI (heart attack) and stroke.

- --A 24-hour urine collection to measure metanephrines and catecholamines is ordered, and if elevated then adrenal CT or MRI with IV contrast is obtained.

## Slide 62

- Secondary HTN EtiologiesHyperaldosteronism
- In primary hyperaldosteronism the patient secretes excessive aldosterone, causing sodium and water retention.
- Constantly reabsorbing sodium creates a metabolic alkalosis and persistent hypokalemia, as H+ and K+ are wasted in the urine
- Patients primarily complain of generalized weakness due to persistent hypokalemia.

## Slide 63

- Secondary HTN Etiologies
- Cushing’s syndrome: glucocorticoid excess from
  - An autonomous pituitary adenoma that over-secretes ACTH
  - An autonomous adrenal adenoma/hyperplasia that oversecretes cortisol
  - A paraneoplastic syndrome from an ectopic source- usually a tumor that oversecretes ACTH, most often SCLC
  - Excess glucocorticoid causes retention of Na and water  HTN from volume expansion and mild increase in aldosterone levels.

## Slide 64

- Secondary HTN Etiologies
- OSA
  - Occurs in >50% of individuals with HTN
  - Should be considered in individuals with resistant HTN, snoring, obesity
  - Hypoxia triggers SNS activity leading to HTN
  - CPAP is effective treatment for OSA, however may not relieve HTN but make antihypertensive medication more responsive.
