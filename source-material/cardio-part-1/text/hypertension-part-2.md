## Slide 5

- Making the Diagnosis of HTN
- BP varies dramatically in a 24-hour period and from day to day, so a single BP reading is ALMOST NEVER used to make a diagnosis of HTN.
  - Exception: BP > 180 systolic or >120 diastolic
          - BP > 160 systolic or >100 diastolic with known end organ target damage
- Clinical criteria for defining hypertension:
  - Average of 2 or more seated BP readings separated by at least 1-2 min, during each of 2 or more outpatient visits.
  - Out of office monitoring is preferred via home BP monitoring or ambulatory BP monitoring when making the diagnosis.
    - If out of office monitoring can’t be achieved, office BP reading should be repeated within 1-2wk after initial screening.

## Slide 7

- Measurement of BP- OBPM Technique
- No exercise, caffeine or smoking for 30 minutes prior to checking the BP.  The patient should have an empty bladder prior to sitting down.
- Seat the patient in a chair with feet firmly on the floor and back supported firmly for at least 5 minutes prior to readings (taken two times in both arms-at initial visit).
- Blood pressure cuff should be applied to bare arm (avoid simply rolling up the sleeve as this acts as a tourniquet)

## Slide 8

- Measurement of BP- OBPM Technique
- The arm should be supported at the level of the heart, otherwise the BP will be elevated by 10-12 mmHg due to added hydrostatic pressure from gravity.
- The person taking the blood pressure and the patient should NOT TALK at all.
- Ideally, BP is measured with an automated oscillometric meter with no clinical staff in the room.

## Slide 9

- Measurement of BP-OBPM Technique
- BP should be measured in both arms, at least at the initial visit, and if disparity exists, the arm with the higher reading should be used for future readings.
- BP should also be measured after 5 min supine and then at 1 to 3 minutes after standing to evaluate for the effect of postural change (particularly important in the older adult)
  - Systolic drop of 20mmHg and/or Diastolic drop of 10mmHg

## Slide 11

- Measurement of BP-HBPM Technique
- The accuracy of the home device should be verified in the clinician's office
- The patient should measure BP properly- seated (with feet flat on the floor), arm supported and after >5min rest
- Measure BP for 2wk after medication change or 1wk prior to clinic visit.
  - Measure BP twice in the morning before medication and in the evening prior to dinner, with each reading separated by 1min.
  - Patients BP should be stored in the memory of the automated oscillometric meter or recorded on paper.
- Home blood pressure should not be used for diagnosis and management if it cannot be performed adequately.

## Slide 12

- Measurement of BP-ABPM Technique
- ABPM: the preferred method for diagnosing hypertension and white coat hypertension.
- ABPM predicts target-organ damage and cardiovascular events better than OBPM.
- ABPM records the BP at preset intervals over 24 hours-usually every 15-20 minutes during day and every 30 to 60 minutes during sleep.
- It is also the only method of BP measurement that can reliably obtain nocturnal readings.

## Slide 17

- Making the HTN Diagnosis
- A diagnosis of HTN can be made, without further confirmatory readings, in the following uncommon scenarios:
- A patient with hypertensive urgency or emergency (blood pressure ≥180/≥120)
- A patient who presents with an initial screening blood pressure ≥160/≥100 mmHg and who also has known target end-organ damage (coronary artery disease, heart failure, left ventricular hypertrophy, stroke, hypertensive retinopathy, chronic kidney disease)

## Slide 21

- Hypertension Defined
- These definitions apply to adults on no anti-HTN medications and who are not acutely ill.
- If there is a disparity in category between the systolic and diastolic pressures, the higher value determines the severity of the hypertension.
- Once on anti-HTN meds, patients are always referred to as hypertensive regardless of BP, unless they make lifestyle changes and come off drug treatment, maintaining normal BP

## Slide 22

- All Hypertension Is NOT Created EqualNon-Dippers
- Blood pressure generally decreases by 15% during periods of sleep, however some persons do not have this decline.
- Failure of the BP to decrease nocturnally in these “non-dippers” confers additional cardiovascular disease risk
- This is only readily detectable on ambulatory BP monitoring.

## Slide 23

- All Hypertension Is NOT Created EqualYoung vs Old patients
- In younger patients without comorbidities, it is the diastolic BP that is most closely associated with cardiovascular risk.
- As we age, many older adults have isolated systolic hypertension with normal diastolic BP’s and therefore a wider pulse pressure (systolic BP- diastolic BP), reflecting vascular non-compliance.
- In adults over 60, systolic BP remains the primary determiner of cardiovascular risk.

## Slide 24

- All Hypertension Is NOT Created EqualBlack Patients
- Hypertension is a particularly common finding in black individuals.
- Hypertension in black patients is generally more responsive to sodium intake, obesity and diet than white persons.
- Hypertension occurs at a younger age and is often more severe in terms of blood pressure levels in black patients than in white persons.

## Slide 25

- All Hypertension Is NOT Created EqualBlack Patients
- Black patients with hypertension are particularly vulnerable to strokes and hypertensive kidney disease.
- Blacks have a 1.3 times greater risk of nonfatal stroke, 1.8 times greater risk of fatal strokes.
- Blacks are 4 times as likely as whites to have renal complications and end-stage kidney disease due to hypertension.
- There are significant medication efficacy differences between blacks and whites.

## Slide 27

- Natural History of HTN
- Prehypertension: asymptomatic increased cardiac output and intermittently elevated BP
- Early hypertension: persistently increased peripheral resistance
- Established hypertension: vascular remodeling changes are occurring and there is progressive damage
- Complicated hypertension causes end-organ damage

## Slide 28

- HTN Complications
- Chronic hypertension contributes to the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD).
- Left ventricular hypertrophy
- Heart failure, both systolic and diastolic
- Ischemic stroke
- Intracerebral hemorrhage
- Ischemic heart disease, including MI and coronary interventions
- Hypertensive retinopathy

## Slide 29

- Vascular Remodeling of HTN
- Chronic hypertension (increases afterload) causing increased arterial wall stiffness (less compliance), increased systolic blood pressure (BP), and widened pulse pressures.

## Slide 30

- Cardiac Pathophysiology of HTN
- The increased afterload of hypertension
  - Decreases coronary perfusion pressures to an already overworked myocardium,
  - Increases myocardial oxygen consumption
- [picture]
- Leads to hypertrophy of the left ventricular myocytes (LVH) hypertensive heart disease.

## Slide 31

- Cardiac Pathophysiology of HTN
- When left ventricular hypertrophy is present, the myocardium has undergone structural changes in response to increased afterload.
- Cardiac myocytes have hypertrophied, allowing the heart to pump more strongly against the elevated vascular pressure in the aorta due to vascular resistance.
- Diastolic dysfunction occurs first in HTN and is caused by an incomplete passive relaxation of the stiff, hypertrophied left ventricle during diastole.

## Slide 32

- Cardiac Pathophysiology of HTN
- As the hypertrophied LV does not relax completely during early diastole, left ventricular end-diastolic pressure increases, further increasing left atrial pressure in late diastole, causing distortion of the cardiac and great vessel architecture.

## Slide 33

- Cardiac Pathophysiology of HTN
- Eventually, LVH begins to impinge on the heart cavity, limiting diastolic filling and stroke volume.
- Once this occurs, the long preserved systolic function of the heart begins to fail.

## Slide 34

- Cardiac Pathophysiology of HTN
- Hypertensive heart disease manifests as a continuum of changes:
  - Left ventricular hypertrophy and HFpEF (diastolic)
  - Left atrial enlargement
  - Systolic heart failure, HFrEF, leads to . . .
  - Aortic root dilatation and valve incompetence
  - Atrial and ventricular arrhythmias
  - Ischemic heart disease/ cardiomyopathy
- LVH is associated with an increased risk of premature death and morbidity.

## Slide 35

- Pathophysiology of HTN in the CNS
- In normotensive or mildly hypertensive patients, a rapid rise in BP can cause:
  - Hyperperfusion/increased cerebral blood flow
  - Increased intracranial pressure
  - Cerebral edema (end organ damage)
  - Cerebral/ carotid artery dissection
  - Ischemic stroke due to in situ thrombus or arteriosclerotic embolus
  - Hemorrhagic stroke
  - CPP = MAP − ICP

## Slide 36

- Pathophysiology of HTN in the CNS
- Cerebral autoregulation is the ability of the cerebral vasculature to maintain a constant cerebral blood flow across a wide range of perfusion pressures coming from the heart.
  - If systemic blood pressure drops, cerebral perfusion is preserved through vasodilation of arteries in the brain to allow for increased blood volume to flow in the brain to continue perfusion
  - If systemic blood pressure increases, arteriolar vasoconstriction occurs to prevent hyperperfusion
- When chronic elevated BP is present, the CNS will, over time, become accustomed to these elevated perfusion pressures and begin to consider them normal.

## Slide 38

- Pathophysiology of HTN in the CNS
- Chronic hypertensive patients adapt by increasing the cerebral vascular resistance and are more prone to cerebral ischemia when flow decreases, especially if the BP is decreased rapidly into the normotensive range.
- Therefore, for patients with long-standing HTN, it can be just as dangerous to lower their BP too precipitously as it is to let it stay elevated.
- In patients with long-standing HTN it is best to allow the BP to normalize slowly over a period of a few weeks as medications are gradually titrated to allow for autoregulation to return back to the normal set point.

## Slide 39

- Pathophysiology of HTN in the Kidney
- Chronic HTN causes pathologic sclerotic changes to the small arteries of the kidney, resulting in endothelial cell dysfunction and impaired vasodilation.
- This damage alters renal autoregulation of blood flow to the glomerulus.
- [picture]
- When the renal autoregulatory system is disrupted, the intraglomerular pressure starts to vary directly with the systemic arterial pressure, which means that the kidney has no protection during swings in BP.
- Volume expansion plays a key role in hypertension in patients with glomerular disease (nephrotic and nephritic syndrome), as the patient becomes volume overloaded.

## Slide 40

- Pathophysiology of HTN in the Kidney
- In patients with renal vascular disease (RAS), hypertension is the result of the near- constant activation of the renin-angiotensin system, which is often secondary to tissue ischemia.
- The combination of volume expansion and the activation of the renin-angiotensin system is believed to be the main factor behind HTN in patients with chronic renal failure.

## Slide 42

- History in the Diagnosis of HTN
- Often, high blood pressure is only one of several cardiovascular risk factors that require attention when a person is diagnosed with hypertension.
- When diagnosing a patient with hypertension, the clinician is assessing the history for the possibility of end organ damage from hypertension as well as evaluating risk factors for cardiometabolic disease that may be modified to improve future health outcomes.

## Slide 43

- History in the Diagnosis of HTN
- Important previous events include:
  - Heart failure or s/s suggestive of heart failure
  - Obstructive sleep apnea
  - Chronic kidney disease
  - Stroke or TIA
  - Diabetes or glucose intolerance
  - Peripheral arterial disease

## Slide 44

## Slide 45

- Physical Exam in HTN
- Vitals: BP, height, weight, waist circumference
- Eyes: Cotton wool spots, AV nicking, arteriolar narrowing, hemorrhages, exudate, papilledema
- Vessels: Palpation and auscultation of pulses
  - Delay in the femoral pulse when compared to the radial pulse could indicate coarctation of aorta
  - Weak or absent pedal pulses could indicate PAD
  - Bruits in carotid, abdomen or femoral could indicate stenosis or atherosclerosis
- Heart: Inspect and palpate for heaves. Palpate for displaced apical impulse. Auscultate for S4
- Lungs: Auscultate for rales
- Brain: Neurologic examination

## Slide 47

- History in the Diagnosis of HTN
- Assessment of a patient’s cardiovascular risk status
  - Age >55 years for men or >65 years for women: increased risk begins at the respective ages
  - Estimated GFR < 60 mL/min (stage III CKD)
  - Microalbuminuria: spot urine protein to creatinine ratio
  - Family history of premature CAD (male relatives < 55; female relatives < 65 yrs)

## Slide 48

- History in the Diagnosis of HTN
- We must also exclude secondary causes of hypertension
  - OTC meds, oral contraceptives, EtOH and illicit drugs (meth, cocaine)
  - Elevated creatinine, abdominal masses or bruits, anemia suggests CKD or RAS
  - Episodes of sweating, labile hypertension, HA and palpitations suggests pheochromocytoma.

## Slide 49

- History in the Diagnosis of HTN
- We must also exclude secondary causes of hypertension
  - H/o cold or heat tolerance, sweating, lack of energy, and bradycardia or tachycardia may indicate thyroid disease.
  - A history of obstructive sleep apnea, heavy snoring, daytime sleepiness
  - A history of generalized weakness coupled with hypokalemia suggests hyperaldosteronism.

## Slide 50

- Screening for Secondary HTN
- Testing is expensive and requires clinical suspicion and knowledge of limitations of different tests
- When to screen:
  - New onset HTN if younger than 20-25 years old
    - Can occur at younger age in black patients
  - HTN refractory to medical Rx (>3-4 meds)
  - Specific clinical/lab features typical for disease
    - Persistent hypokalemia, epigastric bruits, different BP in arms, episodic HTN/flushing/palpitations, etc

- Diagnostic Work-up of New Onset HTN
