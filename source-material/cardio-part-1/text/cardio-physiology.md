## Slide 7

- Pressure, Resistance, & Flow
- Flow, a volume measurement, determined by difference of pressure at each end of a vessel or group of vessels.
- Resistance is opposition to that flow; caused by friction between blood components and vessel walls. Blood viscosity is part of resistance.
  - Resistance of all vessels combined is termed Peripheral Vascular Resistance or Systemic Vascular Resistance.
- Pressure is determined by force from behind and against the vessel walls by the blood volume
- Flow = Change in pressure
- Resistance

## Slide 8

- Laminar vs. Turbulent Flow
- Laminar flow is smooth or streamlined flow which requires less pressure to move forward
- Turbulent flow creates a movement of flow in all directions and requires greater pressure to move forward.
- Atherosclerotic plaque disrupts the smooth vessel lining creating a turbulent flow and thus raises pressure in order to move blood forward
- Higher viscosity (friction between molecules of a flowing fluid) also creates increased turbulence
- Heart murmurs result from turbulent blood flow through a diseased heart valve. The turbulence of the flow is what you perceive as the sound of a murmur

## Slide 10

- Wall Tension, Radius & Pressure
- Wall tension = force in vessel wall that opposes distending pressure inside.
- Pressure inside vessel MUST be higher than wall tension pressure of vessel wall or it will collapse. This is called critical closing pressure.
- Circulatory shock - pressure drops in small vessels to the point of “critical closing pressure” and perfusion of tissues lessens.

## Slide 11

- Distensibility & Compliance
- Distensibility = ability of vessel to be stretched and accommodate increase in volume of blood
- Compliance = total quantity of blood that can be stored in a given portion of the circulation for each millimeter mercury rise in pressure
- Compliance = Change in volume
- Change in pressure
- The higher the compliance of a structure, the more easily it can be stretched

## Slide 12

- Distensibility and Compliance
- Contraction of ventricles ejects blood into the pulmonary and systemic arteries during systole
- If there was an equal quantity of blood flow, the volume of blood in the arteries would remain constant and arterial pressure would not change
  - Only a 1/3 of stroke volume leaves the arteries and the rest remains during systole which distends them and increases the pressure
  - When ventricular contraction ends, the stretched arterial walls recoil and blood continues to be driven into the arterioles during diastole
  - As blood leaves the arteries, the arterial volume and pressure slowly fall but the next ventricular contraction occurs,  so pressure does not fall to 0

## Slide 13

- Heart as a “Pump”
- Pericardium
  - Sac surrounding heart that protects it from rubbing on external structures
  - Anchors heart & large vessels to diaphragm and mediastinum
  - Fibrous layer and serous layer
  - Narrow space between the parietal and visceral layer of pericardium filled with fluid that helps lubricate as heart moves
- Epicardium
  - Membrane closely affixed to the heart
- Endocardium
  - Thin layer of cells that line chambers of heart and is continuous with endothelium of blood vessels
  - Reduces friction and hence, turbulence

## Slide 14

- Heart as a “Pump”
- Myocardium
  - The muscle layer, thickest in left ventricle for more contraction power
  - Cardiac muscle combines properties of both skeletal and smooth muscle
    - Cells are striated (thick myosin and thin actin filaments)
  - Intercalated disks join individual muscle cells to make depolarization faster
  - Cells depolarize so as to contract as a unit, squeezing from superior to inferior in atria and inferior to superior in ventricles, thus moving blood efficiently

## Slide 16

- Cardiac Conducting System
- The sinoatrial (SA) node “normally” is the initiator of the “pace” or rate of the heart
- Its depolarization generates the action potential that leads to depolarization of all other cardiac muscle cells
  - Its discharge rate determines the heart rate
    - Number of times the heart contracts per minute
- It depolarizes at a rate of 60 to 100 times per minute (which is faster than other myocardial cells)
- Impulse from the SA node travels through the internodal pathways in both atria to the Atrioventricular (AV) node, while causing depolarization of the atrial myocardium and thus contraction.

## Slide 17

- Cardiac Conducting System
- Within the AV node, atrial fibers connect with junctional fibers of the node itself
- These connections, the zone surrounding, and the AV node is referred to as the AV junctional area
- Depolarization is very slow through these fibers before arriving at the AV node itself, which also delays the depolarization of the ventricles
- This allows the atria to complete its depolarization and contraction fully

## Slide 18

- Cardiac Conducting System
- As depolarization continues, it passes through the AV node into the AV bundle (Bundle of His) and down the right and left bundle branches in the respective ventricular septal walls to the Purkinje fibers in the ventricular walls.
- From here depolarization of the ventricles begins and contraction progresses from the apex toward the base.

## Slide 19

- Cardiac Conducting System
- If SA node does not fire, AV node CAN fire on its own BUT at its internal rate of 40 to 60 times per minute
- If there is a problem with the AV node not firing, the Purkinje fibers themselves can fire, but at a rate between 15 and 40 per minute.
- Disruption anywhere along the conducting system can be seen on the tracings of an ECG.

## Slide 21

- Action Potentials of Myocardium
- Major charge (+) carriers in myocardial cells are Sodium (Na+), Potassium (K+), and Calcium (Ca2+)
- They change the polarity of the myocardial cell membrane depending on the opening of the ion channels
- The “action potential” of each cell membrane is divided into 5 phases of state of ion movement (starting with phase 0)

## Slide 22

- Action Potentials of Myocardium
- Phase 0 – rapid depolarization
- Phase 1 – early repolarization
- Phase 2 – the plateau
- Phase 3 – rapid repolarization
- Phase 4 – resting membrane

## Slide 23

- Action Potentials of Myocardium
- Three types of ion channels in myocardial cells
- Fast sodium channels
- Slow calcium-sodium channels
- Potassium channels
- Phase 0- fast sodium channels open allowing rapid inflow of sodium
  - Changing the membrane potential from -90 mV to +20 mV and rapid depolarization begins.

## Slide 24

- Action Potentials of Myocardium
- Phase 1 – is at the peak of the action potential and inactivation of the rapid sodium channels
- Phase 2 – slow opening of calcium channels occurs thus extending the action potential by 3 to 15 times longer than that of skeletal muscle, thereby extending contraction
  - This is called the “plateau” phase

## Slide 25

- Action Potentials of Myocardium
- Phase 3 -  Rapid repolarization occurs
  - Calcium/Sodium channels close and Potassium channels open allowing K+ to move to the extracellular space thus reversing the polarity of the cell membrane.
- Phase 4 – Resting phase
  - Sodium/Potassium pump returns sodium to the extracellular space and Potassium back intracellular.
- Then the phases all start again

## Slide 26

- Absolute & Relative Refractory Periods
- Absolute Refractory Period – the period of time in the action potential curve where NO stimuli can generate another action potential
  - This includes Phases 0, 1, 2, and part of 3. This period is almost as long as the contraction, thus blocking another contraction from occurring until the first is over.
- Relative Refractory Period – repolarization has returned membrane potential to a level just below that of threshold potential, but cell is capable of re-depolarization to a greater than normal stimulus.
- Supernormal Excitatory Period – very short time during which a weak stimulus can evoke a response. Period where arrhythmias can develop.

## Slide 27

- Absolute & Relative Refractory Periods
- TP = Threshold Potential
- RMP = Resting Membrane Potential
- ARP = Absolute Refractory Period
- RRP = Relative Refractory Period
- SN = Supernormal Excitatory Period
- Numbers represent Phases of Action Potential (0 to 4)

> Notes: Relative refractory period- cell is capable of responding to a greater than normal stimulus. It begins when membrane potential is phase 3 reaches TP and ends just before end of phase 3
> SN- a weak stimulus can evoke a response

## Slide 28

- Cardiac Cycle
- Divided into two distinct parts – Systole & Diastole
- Systole = phase of ventricular contraction and pumping of blood out of the ventricles.
- Diastole = phase of ventricular relaxation and filling of the ventricle with blood.
- During Systole the AV valves are closed preventing backflow into the atria and forcing blood out the open Semilunar valves
- During Diastole the AV valves open allowing blood to fill the relaxed ventricles while the Semilunar valves shut preventing backflow of blood from the vessels.

## Slide 29

- Ventricular Systole & Diastole
- Systole – divided into two phases –
  - “isovolumetric contraction period” & “ejection period”
- “Isovolumetric contraction period” – begins with closing of AV valves (1st heart sound) but before opening of semilunar valves. Contraction begins and intraventricular fluid pressure rises.
- “Ejection period” – once pressure in ventricles exceeds the aorta and pulmonary trunk, the semilunar valves open and ejection period occurs
  - It continues until pressure in great vessels becomes higher than in the ventricles thus causing semilunar valves to close (2nd heart sound).

## Slide 30

- Ventricular Systole & Diastole
- Diastole – also divided into two phases
- “Isovolumetric relaxation period”
  - Begins with the closing of the semilunar valves and ventricles begin relaxing
  - Intraventricular pressures fall below that of the atria and AV valves open
- “Rapid filling phase”
  - Begins with opening of AV valves and ends with complete filling of ventricles
  - If a person has an over-distended or non-compliant ventricle, a 3rd heart sound may be heard. It is a sound heard at the end of the filling phase due to vibrations that are caused by abrupt cessation of blood entering the ventricles, but before the atria contract at the end of diastole
  - A 4th heart sound may be heard during the last 1/3rd of diastole when the atria contract and the rush of blood is turbulent.

## Slide 31

- Atrial Filling & Contraction
- NO valves between the great veins and the atria.
- Blood enters atria during both systole and diastole due to such low pressures. This allows ease of flow into the atria.
- Three main phases of atrial pressure waves – “C-wave”, “V-wave”, & “A-wave”

## Slide 32

- Atrial Filling & Contraction
- “C-wave” – begins at start of systole when AV valves close and bulge into atria causing an increase in atrial pressure and stopping inflow of blood for a very short time.
- “V-wave” – begins toward the end of systole with the AV valves still closed, but pressure from systemic vessels is greater than atrial pressure and a filling with blood occurs.
- “A-wave” – during the last phase of diastole and the AV valves are open and the atria contract to push the last amount of blood they can push.

## Slide 33

- Atrial Filling & Contraction
- Right atrial pressure can be seen in the internal jugular veins as pulsations. These can be measured to assess cardiac function.
- Right atrial pressure/filling is predicated on the ability of the right ventricle to pump blood out to the lungs
  - When the heart pumps strongly, atrial pressure drops which increases filling.
- Right atrium pressures also affected by intrathoracic pressures. Decreased with inspiration. Increased with coughing or forced exhalation.

## Slide 34

- Regulation of Cardiac Performance
- Cardiac Output – amount of blood the heart pumps per minute (Liters/minute)
- Stroke volume – amount of blood ejected by each ventricle with each beat
- Heart rate – number of beats per minute
- Cardiac Output = Stroke volume x Heart rate
- CO = SV x HR
- Normal adult CO = 4.0 to 8.0 L/min.

## Slide 35

- Regulation of Cardiac Performance
- Cardiac Reserve – maximum increase in CO that can be achieved above normal CO.
- Normal young healthy adult – 300 to 400% above normal CO.
- Heart’s ability to increase CO mainly depends on 4 factors – the preload, the afterload, cardiac contractility, & heart rate.

## Slide 36

- Preload
- Preload = volume work of the heart
  - Called Preload because it is the work or load that the heart does BEFORE (Pre) the ventricular contraction begins
  - It is the amount of blood the heart must pump with each beat or the volume of blood stretching the ventricular muscle fibers at the end of diastole (End Diastolic Volume)

## Slide 37

- Frank-Starling Mechanism (Starling Law)
- The increased force of contraction that accompanies an increase in ventricular end-diastolic volume is called the Frank-Starling Mechanism or Starling Law
  - The ventricle contracts more forcefully during systole when it has been filled to a greater degree during diastole

## Slide 38

- Afterload
- The pressure or tension work of the heart. The pressure the heart MUST generate to move blood into the aorta.
- Called Afterload because it is the work (Load)  presented to the heart AFTER the contraction has commenced.
- Systemic & Pulmonary B/P is main source of afterload for left and right ventricles respectfully.

## Slide 40

- Cardiac Contractility
- “Cardiac Contractility” = ability of the heart to change its force of contraction without changing its resting length
- Contractility increases CO independent of preload and afterload
- Interaction between actin and myosin filaments during cardiac muscle contraction and relaxation requires energy supplied by the breakdown of ATP and presence of calcium ions

## Slide 41

- Heart Rate
- Depends on the frequency of ventricular contractions AND time spent in diastolic filling
- Faster the rate, the less time to fill
- Less time to fill, less blood fills
- Less blood fills, less CO
- Less CO, less oxygen and nutrients for cells of body
- Less oxygen & nutrients, cells don’t like it and potentially can die

## Slide 42

- Systemic Circulation
- Function is to deliver oxygen & nutrients to tissues and return carbon dioxide & wastes.
- Consists of arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules, and veins.
- Dynamic structures that constrict and relax to adjust B/P & flow to meet varying demands of tissues and organ systems.

## Slide 43

- Blood Vessels
- All vessels (except capillaries) have 3 layers or coats called tunicae.
- Outer layer, loose connective tissue, is called Tunica externa or adventitia. Controls diameter of vessel.
- Middle layer, smooth muscle tissue, is called Tunica media.
- Inner layer, epithelial tissue, is called Tunica intima. Smoothness prevents platelet adherence & blood clotting.

## Slide 44

- Vascular Smooth Muscle
- Tunica media is in control of vasoconstriction and vasodilation.
- Smooth muscle contracts slowly and generates high forces for long periods of time with low energy requirement (uses 1/10 to 1/300 the energy of skeletal muscle).
- There is less developed sarcoplasmic reticulum for intracellular calcium storage, and very few fast sodium channels.
- Depolarization for vasoconstriction therefore relies on extracellular calcium.

## Slide 45

- Vascular Smooth Muscle
- Sympathetic nervous system control of vascular smooth muscle tone occurs thru receptor-activated opening & closing of the calcium channels.
- Α1 & 2-adrenergic receptors are excitatory, causing calcium channels to open producing vasoconstriction.
- β2-adrenergic receptors are inhibitory, causing calcium channels to close producing vasodilation.
- Calcium channel blocking drugs cause vasodilation by blocking calcium entry thru calcium channels in smooth muscle.

## Slide 46

- Arterial System
- High pressure system
- Consists of large and medium-sized arteries and arterioles
- Thick-walled vessels with lots of elastic fibers that allow for flexibility of vessel during systole & diastole
- Arterioles (mostly smooth muscle) serve as resistance vessels for circulatory system and regulate B/P

## Slide 47

- Arterial System
- Arterial Pulse Pressures – waves of pressure generated by intermittent ejection of blood from left ventricle into aorta and large arteries along the entire length of the vessels.
- When taking a pulse, it is this pulse pressure that you are feeling.

## Slide 48

- Arterial System
- Pulse pressure wave gets smaller & smaller the further down the system you move until at the capillaries, there is not much left, just enough to keep the capillaries open.
- Local tissue control can open the system wider for more blood flow to occur (such as with a cut finger) such that you may feel a “pulsing” in the finger. This is allowing more blood to get there to help in healing process.

## Slide 49

- Venous System
- Low pressure system
- Returns blood from capillaries via venules to veins to right atrium.
- Pressure in the right atrium (normal being 0mmHg) is called Central Venous Pressure.
- This pressure is regulated by the ability of the right ventricle to pump blood to the pulmonary circulation AND flow unimpeded into the right atrium.

## Slide 50

- Venous System
- Veins & Venules are thin-walled, distensible, and collapsible.
- Veins can enlarge and store large quantities of blood.
- Veins still have muscle layer that allows for contraction and relaxation based on tissue demand.
- Veins in the extremities (arms & legs) have one-way valves (cups like the semilunar valves) that prevents retrograde flow of blood.
- Muscles in the extremities contract intermittently to help move the blood forward toward the heart. This is called the venous or muscle pump.

## Slide 52

- Microcirculation & Lymphatic System
- Arterioles, capillaries, & venules referred to as the microcirculation
- Arterioles channel blood into the capillaries via precapillary sphincters that open based on venous pressures and local tissue needs
- Capillaries are the site of gas, nutrients, & waste exchange

## Slide 53

- Microcirculation & Lymphatic System
- Oxygen & Nutrients leave the capillary into the extracellular space where they are then picked up by individual cells for use
- Carbon Dioxide & Waste products leave the cells into the extracellular space where it is either picked up by capillaries, if the products are small enough to fit into the pores of the capillary OR by the lymphatic vessels that can channel larger products back to the venous system via the Thoracic duct into the left subclavian vein or the Right lymphatic duct into the right subclavian
- Lymph nodes are interspersed along lymphatic vessels to help filter bacteria via B & T cell lymphocytes (which also mature from differentiation).

- Local & Humoral Control of Blood Flow
- Autoregulation
- Endothelial Control
- Humoral Control

## Slide 55

- Autoregulation
- Local control of blood flow governed by nutritional needs of the local tissue.
- Heart, brain, & kidneys blood flow stays relatively constant, even when systemic B/P changes even over a range from 60 to 180 mmHg. This preserves the vital organ function.
- Mediated by changes in blood vessel tone or local tissue factors (such as low oxygen or accumulation of toxic metabolites).
- Hyperemia (increased blood flow) is an example of autoregulation.

## Slide 56

- Endothelial Control
- Important function of endothelial cells lining arterioles and small arteries is synthesis and release of factors that control vessel dilation
- Endothelium produces nitric oxide which relaxes vascular smooth muscle and has direct vasodilation effects
- Nitric oxide inhibits platelet aggregation and secretion of platelet contents which causes vasoconstriction
- This is under control by several agonists – acetylcholine, bradykinin, histamine, and thrombin
- Sheer Stress from increased blood flow or increased B/P can also cause release of nitric oxide

## Slide 57

- Endothelial Control
- Endothelium also release vasoconstrictor substances – angiotensin II, prostaglandins, and endothelins
- After a blood vessel is damaged, local endothelin is released to vasoconstrict the vessel and prevent extensive bleeding

## Slide 58

- Humoral Control
- Vasoconstrictive & vasodilative hormones circulate in the blood vessels from distant glands OR from local tissue.
- Important hormones are: norepinephrine, epinephrine, angiotensin II, histamine, serotonin, bradykinin, and the prostaglandins.

## Slide 59

- Humoral Control
- Norepinephrine – powerful vasoconstrictor
- Epinephrine – not as powerful a vasoconstrictor; in skeletal muscle acts as a vasodilator.
- Released by sympathetic nerve endings as well as the adrenal medulla.
- Nerve endings release causes immediate reaction while adrenal release takes longer due to circulatory time.

> Notes: Stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system during stress or exercise causes local constriction of veins and arterioles bc of release of norepi from sympathetic nerve endings

## Slide 60

- Humoral Control
- Angiotensin II – powerful vasoconstrictor; acts on arterioles to increase peripheral vascular resistance thereby increasing B/P.
- Histamine – powerful vasodilator of arterioles and increases capillary permeability. Derived from mast cells in injured tissues and basophils in the blood.

## Slide 61

- Humoral Control
- Serotonin – released from aggregating platelets during clotting process; causes vasoconstriction, important role in controlling bleeding.
- Bradykinin – causes intense vasodilation of arterioles, increased capillary permeability, and constriction of venules. Helps regulate blood flow in the skin, salivary glands, & GI tract.

## Slide 62

- Humoral Control
- Prostaglandins – formed from constituents of cell membrane. Tissue injury incites release of these constituents which then initiates synthesis of prostaglandins. Some produce vasoconstriction while others produce vasodilation
- Corticosteroids produce an anti-inflammatory response by blocking the release of constituents (arachidonic acid), preventing prostaglandin synthesis.

## Slide 63

- Neural Control of Circulatory Function
- Neural control centers for regulation of cardiac function and B/P are in reticular formation of the lower Pons & Medulla of the brain stem, where integration of the ANS responses occur.
- Both sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the ANS innervate the heart.
- Parasympathetic via the vagus nerve (CN X) which produces a slowing of the heart rate. Little to no effect on the vasculature.
- Sympathetic has excitatory effect on heart rate and contractility AND controlling smooth muscle tone of the blood vessels.

## Slide 64

- ANS Control of B/P
- Intrinsic & Extrinsic reflexes work to assure adequate B/P.
- Intrinsic = baroreceptors & chemoreceptors
- Extrinsic = reflexes of pain and cold work to shunt blood to central body where it is needed most.

## Slide 65

- ANS Control of B/P
- Baroreceptors – pressure sensitive receptors located in the walls of blood vessels and heart; respond to changes in stretch of vessel walls by sending impulses to CV center in brainstem.
- Chemoreceptors monitor O2 & CO2 & H+ and located in the carotid bodies which lie in the bifurcation of the internal & external carotids; they communicate with both the respiratory and cardiovascular centers of the brainstem.
