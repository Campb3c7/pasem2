## Slide 4

- There have been multiple changes to the classification of cardiomyopathies over the last few decades
  - In the past, all causes of heart muscle damage were included
    - Most notably were the known cardiovascular causes (HTN, CAD, CHD, and valve disease)
  - The current definition is more limiting: a group of conditions in which the heart muscle (myocardium) is structurally and functionally abnormal in the absence of known cardiovascular causes sufficient to explain the abnormality
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 5

- CM had been considered idiopathic but modern advances in genetics and imaging have helped identify the pathological and genetic basis of CM
  - We now know that most cardiomyopathies have a genetic basis
- The classifications of CM are undergoing a transition based on their genetic association rather than the phenotype
  - Genetic
  - Mixed (genetic and acquired)
  - Acquired
- Cardiomyopathies (CM)

## Slide 6

- However, it’s still important to recognize that heart muscle damage can occur as a sequelae of valve disease, congenital heart disease, HTN, coronary artery disease (CAD)  but they are no longer classified as a cardiomyopathy (instead they may cause the generic term = left ventricular hypertrophy)
  - You have separate lectures on those topics
  - “ischemic cardiomyopathy” is an old-fashion term that describes heart muscle damage as a result of vessel disease (CAD)
  - “valvular cardiomyopathy” is again, old-fashion and describes when damage of the heart muscle occurs due to abnormal valves
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 7

- The heart is
- AMAZING
- The myocardium needs to contract more than 100,000 times a day without stopping or tiring
- It requires the ability to respond rapidly to physiologic changes; instantaneously varying the rate and strength of contractions
- A heartbeat can be detected 3 weeks after conception
- Even at rest, the heart is still working twice as hard as the leg muscles of a sprinter
- Because it has its own electrical system, it can beat when removed from the human body
- It generates enough electricity in a day to drive a truck 20 miles

## Slide 8

- The myocardium is composed of myocytes (muscle tissue) which allow for contractility and electrical conduction
- A healthy myocardium requires the ability to respond rapidly to physiologic changes
  - i.e., Exercise, fight or flight response, sleeping, medications, test-taking
- CM can result from changes to the shape, size, or thickness of the myocardium. These abnormalities can impair contraction and electrical conduction
  - The way the myocardium is affected depends on the phenotype
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 9

- Spectrum of symptoms are variable and can range from asymptomatic to severe heart failure or sudden death
  - If symptomatic, generally present with chest pain, exertional intolerance, SOB, palpitations
- PE is normal in many cases but may show a gallop rhythm, atrioventricular valve regurgitation, displaced PMI, cardiac lift/heave, rales (crackles), JVD, peripheral edema, ascites
- The final common pathways of the various CM etiologies usually result in heart failure, arrythmias and death
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 10

- Progression of heart failure in CM
  - Early symptoms are often related to exertional intolerance, usually due to insufficient cardiac reserve during exercise
    - Breathlessness (dyspnea on exertion = DOE), fatigue, dizziness, syncope, chest pain
    - Vague symptoms  may be overlooked or attributed to other causes such as aging or deconditioning
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 11

- Usually over time (though it can be rapid in acute heart failure), fluid retention leads to increased filling pressures in the heart which will worsen symptoms
  - Shortness of breath (dyspnea) during routine activity
  - Wet cough with frothy sputum
  - Orthopnea: dyspnea when lying down, typically described as # of pillows needed to alleviate SOB
  - Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND): SOB or coughing attack waking pt up at night
  - Peripheral edema, ascites, abdominal discomfort
  - Increased JVP
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 12

- Arrythmias
  - Atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias
  - Sudden death is typically due to sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT), ultimately progressing to ventricular fibrillation (VF) and then asystole
- Many patients will have increased risk of thromboembolism
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 13

- Workup typically includes:
- Labs: BNP is helpful in setting of heart failure; troponins may be helpful in acute settings; other labs as indicated to rule out other etiologies
- EKG/Holter monitor: assess electrical activity; provides clues but does not diagnose CM
  - Atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias are common
- CXR: suggestive but not specific; normal in many early cases
  - May show enlarged cardiac silhouette if cardiomegaly is present
    - Incidental cardiomegaly on CXR should prompt a cardio evaluation with echo and EKG
  - May show fluffy infiltrates (“kerley lines”) if CHF is present
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 14

- Echocardiogram (“echo”): the test of choice to diagnose cardiomyopathy; defines both anatomical and functional characteristics of the heart with high sensitivity and specificity for myocardial dysfunction
- Cardiac MRI (aka CMR): used as an additional study after CM is confirmed on echo
  - better definition of anatomy and evaluation of R ventricle
  - identifies/localizes fat, iron or amyloid infiltration, inflammation, fibrosis (scar), focal hypertrophy, aneurysm
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 15

- Cardiac biopsy is rarely needed anymore; provides a definitive diagnosis for tissue pathology in subtypes of CM (such as infiltrative disease) if etiology is equivocal
- Cardiac stress testing (“stress test”) is typically performed at some point during the CM evaluation; helps characterize a patient’s exercise tolerance
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 16

- Generally, there is no cure (some exceptions)
- Management goals: reduce morbidity and prevent mortality
  - Heart rate control: beta-blockers (i.e. metoprolol)
  - Reduce thromboembolism risk: anticoagulants (i.e. warfarin or DOAC)
  - *Reduce fluid retention: diuretics (i.e. furosemide/Lasix)
  - Rhythm control: Implantable pacemakers
  - Rhythm control and shock: implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs)
  - In select cases: surgical excision of myocardial areas or heart transplant
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 17

- Because many CMs have a substantial genetic basis, recommendations are given for families of a patient with confirmed CM
  - Detailed, thorough FHx for at least 3 generations
  - Clinical screening of first-degree relatives which consist of H&P, EKG, and Echo
  - Genetic testing of family members should be considered if index case has a known genetic mutation
- Cardiomyopathies

## Slide 21

- A disorder of the myocardium caused by mutations of the cardiac sarcomere or sarcomere-associated proteins (encodes components of the contractile apparatus)
- The genetics of HCM involve an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, with 60–70% of patients having an affected family member
  - Age-dependent (rarely present at birth, usually develops later)
  - Incomplete penetrance (varied extent of disease and clinical outcomes)
- Results in Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH)
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 22

- Characteristics:
  - Asymmetric thickening of the left ventricular septum, especially the sub-aortic area
  - Ventricular wall thickness and loss of elasticity impairs cardiac relaxation, making it harder to fill the heart (diastolic dysfunction)
  - 2⁄3 of patients experience left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 24

- Clinical features may be variable from no symptoms in one patient to sudden cardiac death in another
- May be identified through familial screening or upon workup of suggestive clinical and EKG findings
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 25

- While many patients are asymptomatic, some may develop symptoms such as dyspnea on exertion, exertional chest pain, exertional syncope (or presyncope), palpitations
- Advanced heart failure symptoms (orthopnea, PND, edema) are possible as disease progresses
- Patients with mild to moderate limitation usually experience slow progression of symptoms with advancing age, related to modest deterioration in LV function
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 26

- The physical exam is normal in many cases but may reveal nonspecific abnormalities such as a 4th heart sound (S4), systolic murmur, or cardiac lift/heave
- LVOT obstruction may have a characteristic harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur heard best at the apex and LLSB
- Mitral regurgitation murmur may also occur
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 27

- Extra Heart Sound:s4
- Heard in hypertrophic cardiomyopathies
- Also called the atrial diastolic gallop or “Tennessee murmur”
- Low-pitched, heard at the end of diastole immediately before S1 (ta-lub-dub)
- Best heard at the cardiac apex with the bell of the stethoscope
- Augmentation: auscultate while pt performs a Valsalva strain or stands from the squatting position

## Slide 28

- An electrocardiogram (ECG) is typically abnormal. It can suggest the diagnosis, but the findings are nonspecific and cannot be used to make a definitive diagnosis of CM
- EKG findings may include:
  - Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH)
    - Compensatory mechanisms may cause atrial hypertrophy as well, resulting in abnormal p waves
      - LVH with abnormal P waves strongly suggests HCM
  - Deep narrow (“dagger-like”) Q waves
  - One variant (apical) may result in deeply inverted T waves, aka “Giant Negative T Waves”
  - May see atrial and ventricular arrythmias
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 29

- ALL patients with the associated abnormal ECG findings or clinical concern for HCM need to have an echocardiogram (“echo”)
- Echo is performed transthoracic (TTE) and uses doppler imaging to evaluate blood flow. It can demonstrate cardiac morphology, systolic and diastolic function, presence and severity of LVOT, and valvular abnormalities
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 30

- HCM is confirmed when there is any increase in LV wall thickness ≥ 15mm on echo, in the absence of another cause
- The increased thickness is usually asymmetric and may occur in any segment of the LV wall
  - Most often in the septum or anterolateral wall
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- [picture]

## Slide 31

- LVOT obstruction ≥50 mmHg is a strong and independent determinant of progressive heart failure (HF) symptoms and stroke death in patients with HCM
- The presence of LVOT obstruction may include additional treatment options for heart failure symptoms, as well as the need to avoid some medications
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 32

- CMR is typically then ordered to more reliably assess LV morphology and maximal LV wall thickness
  - Anatomy is also better defined for select patients considering septal reduction (alcohol septal ablation or surgical myectomy)
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- [picture]

## Slide 33

- Patients will also undergo an exercise stress test as part of the HCM evaluation
  - most have LVOT obstruction following exercise
  - Evaluates for exercise-related ischemia or arrythmias
  - Abnormal findings may include:
    - Symptoms (angina, dyspnea, palpitations, presyncope)
    - Failure of BP to increase appropriately or exercise-induced hypotension
    - Arrythmias (AF,  VT) at maximal intensity or immediately after exercise
    - Severe ST depression (reflecting myocardial ischemia)
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 34

- Due to large genetic influence, all first-degree family members of an affected individual should be evaluated
- Family screening includes: H&P, ECG, and echo
- If an HCM-causing mutation is identified in the index case, then family members should be genetically screened
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 35

- Many cardiac etiologies can result in LVH which must be distinguished from HCM. These include acquired causes such as HTN, aortic stenosis, cardiac amyloid, and Athlete’s heart
- “Athlete’s heart” – Athletic LVH
  - Athletic Left Ventricular Hypertrophy is NOT a cardiomyopathy!
  - With endurance training, there can be physiologic increased thickness of the left ventricular wall
  - These individuals can experience several benign arrhythmias
  - Ventricular wall thickness is symmetric and usually measures between 13-15 mm during echocardiogram
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 36

- Treatment for asymptomatic HCM patients include close clinical observation and follow-up
- All patients should be counseled on activity restriction: avoid competitive and strenuous exercise
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 37

- Heart failure symptoms or angina due to LVOT obstruction are treated with a beta-blocker (BB), such as metoprolol
  - Nondihydropyridine CCB (such as diltiazem or verapamil) is typically used if there are contraindications to BB (such as asthma) or those who are unwilling to tolerate BB due to side effects (i.e. feeling sluggish)
  - Initially monotherapy, but may try a combo of BB and nondihydropyridine CCB if further management needed – greater likelihood of bradyarrhythmia and intolerance
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 38

- If HF symptoms are refractory to drugs, surgical septal myectomy or ETOH septal ablation may help alleviate the LVOT obstruction
  - Surgical septal myectomy: removes a portion of the ventricular septum to enable a wider outflow tract for exit of blood
  - Alcohol septal ablation involves injecting absolute ethanol (1-3mL) percutaneously into a septal perforator branch (usually from the LAD coronary artery) that supplies the hypertrophied portion; this induces a local transmural infarction in that zone
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 39

- AVOID in patients with significant LVOT obstruction as they can worsen LVOT obstruction and symptoms
  - Positive inotropes (i.e. dopamine, dobutamine, digoxin)
  - Vasodilators
    - dihydropyridines CCB (nifedipine, amlodipine)
    - ACE/ARBs (lisinopril/losartan)
    - Nitrates (nitroglycerin)
  - Diuretics (i.e. furosemide)
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 40

- Treatment of HCM patients with atrial fibrillation: due to increased risk of thromboembolism (clot formation), oral anticoagulation with either warfarin (Coumadin) or DOAC
- Treatment of HCM patients with ventricular arrythmias or higher risk for sudden cardiac death (SCD) is an Implantable Cardio Defibrillator (ICD) - only effective prevention strategy against sudden cardiac death
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Slide 41

- HCM is the leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes
- Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA)/death is rare, but immediate and drastic
  - A person (typically young adult athlete in HCM) suddenly collapses, with absent pulse and respirations. Occasional there is chest discomfort/dyspnea/palpitations immediately prior to the arrest, but it often occurs without warning
  - Caused by ventricular arrythmias (sustained Ventricular Tachycardia / Ventricular Fibrillation)
- HCM and sudden cardiac arrest

## Slide 42

- Risk is greatest in pts <40yoa, previously identified non-sustained VT, massive hypertrophy, hypotension on stress test, and FH of SCA
- In patients with acute hemodynamic collapse / SCA
  - Life-saving interventions will be discussed in ACLS and arrythmias lecture, but prompt defibrillation and CPR is necessary
- HCM and sudden cardiac arrest

## Slide 44

- Autosomal dominant disorder
- Mutations in genes encoding for desmosomal proteins, which cause myocyte detachment and apoptosis. The myocytes are then replaced by fibro-fatty tissue
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy
- [picture]

## Slide 45

- Presents typically with right-sided heart failure symptoms and various rhythm disturbances, particularly VT
- Often precipitated by release of catecholamines during exercise and is the 2nd MCC of sudden death in athletes
- Often diagnosed in the 2nd or 3rd decade of life
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy

## Slide 46

- Three phases of clinical presentation:
  - Concealed phase- asymptomatic patients
  - Electrical phase - patients present with palpitations or syncope
  - Diffuse phase - patient’s present with right sided heart failure
- Results in:
  - Right sided heart failure (JVD, peripheral edema, ascites, etc.)
  - V-Tach
  - Sudden death
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy

## Slide 47

- EKG is suggestive, but nonspecific
  - 50% of patients will have a normal EKG at presentation
  - However, all patients had one or more EKG findings in a retrospective study performed over 6 years
  - PVCs, QRS prolongation, V-Tach, bundle branch blocks, T wave inversions, Epsilon wave
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy

## Slide 48

- Epsilon waves: notches/blips/wiggles at the end of the QRS complex in precordial leads (particularly V1)
  - Caused by delayed excitation of the myocytes strands that are scattered between fibrous and fatty tissue
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy
- [picture]

## Slide 49

- Echocardiogram is again, the test of choice for diagnosis
  - Demonstrates enlarged/dilated RV with RV wall motion abnormalities and possibly fatty tissue in the RV wall
  - Left ventricle is normal (until later stages)
- Cardiac MRI
  - Used as a follow up study
  - Best study to identify the fibro-fatty infiltrates in patient with RV findings on echo
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy

## Slide 50

- Treatment consists of:
  - Activity restriction (avoid competitive/strenuous exercise)
  - Anti-arrhythmic drugs – first line are BB
  - Implantable cardio defibrillator (ICD) - appropriate for the secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death in those who have experienced a sustained ventricular arrhythmia
  - Last resort is cardiac transplantation
- Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy

## Slide 53

- The most common form of cardiomyopathies
- Characterized by dilation of the ventricle (typically left but can also include right) and impairment of ventricle contraction
  - Systolic dysfunction (ejection fraction ≤ 40%) with left ventricular enlargement
  - Usually presents with features of cardiomegaly and s/s of heart failure that are progressive, often becoming severe
- Dilated cardiomyopathy
- Normal
- DCM

## Slide 54

- Causes are mixed: genetic and acquired
  - Acquired from ETOH use; early stages may be reversible if able to abstain from alcohol
  - Acquired from pregnancy (PPCM); though there may be some underlying genetic factors
  - About 35% of DCM are attributed to genetics
    - Muscular Dystrophy: mutation in the dystrophin encoding gene causes lysis of the cardiomyocytes
  - Drugs/Toxins represent another source of acquired DCM
    - Chemotherapy agents: doxorubicin (Adriamycin) – generally develops within 3 months of last dose
    - Anti-rheumatic agents: hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil)
  - Idiopathic (50%) – family members in half of idiopathic DCM patients are found to also have abnormal cardiac evaluations
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

## Slide 55

- Clinical symptoms:
  - Typical present with left-sided heart failure, which include exertional intolerance/dyspnea early and PND/orthopnea in later stages. Cough with frothy sputum (signifying pulmonary edema), and right heart failure symptoms will then occur, such as anasarca, LE edema, and abdominal distension
- Physical exam:
  - May see signs of left-sided heart failure +/- right-sided HF
    - S3 heart sound and regurg murmurs (tricuspid and mitral)
    - Rales
    - Elevated JVP,  peripheral edema,  ascites
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

## Slide 56

- Extra Heart Sound:s3
- Heard in dilated cardiomyopathies
- Also called the ventricular diastolic gallop or “Kentucky murmur”
- low-pitched sound occurring at the beginning of diastole shortly after S2 (lub-dub-ta)
- Best heard at the cardiac apex with the patient in the left lateral position
- Accentuated in more tachycardic patients

## Slide 57

- Workup includes:
  - Echo will provide the most useful information and is the test of choice for diagnosis
    - Findings: dilation of the cavity of the left ventricle with a normal or decreased wall thickness, reduced inward endocardial systolic motion, EF ≤ 40%
      - May also include enlargement of the atria and RV as well
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

## Slide 58

- Workup includes:
  - CXR: suggestive but not specific enough to diagnosis DCM. Findings include:
    - Cardiomegaly and fluffy infiltrates (known as Kerley lines)
      - Kerley A lines: long (at least 2cm) un-branching lines coursing diagonally from the hila out to the periphery of the lungs
      - Kerley B lines:  short, fat horizontal lines in the periphery
      - Kerley C lines: short, fine lines throughout the lungs (least common)
    - May also see pleural effusions
- Dilated cardiomyopathy
- [picture]

## Slide 59

- Workup includes:
  - ECG: sinus tach and LBBB are most common; a-fib is 2nd most common (again, these are not specific to DCM)
  - BNP: helps establish prognosis and severity of HF (increasingly elevates as heart failure worsens)
  - Troponins not useful except to rule out acute coronary event
  - Stress test
  - Cardiac cath may be included to evaluate for ischemic causes
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

## Slide 60

- Treatment:
  - Correct reversible causes if possible (i.e. ETOH)
  - Treat heart failure
    - Beta-Blockers (reduce heart rate and myocardial O2 demand)
    - Vasodilators:  ACE inhibitors (for afterload reduction) and nitroglycerin (for preload & afterload reduction)
    - Diuretics (i.e. furosemide)
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

## Slide 61

- Treatment:
  - Prevent thromboembolism with anticoagulants
  - Manage dysrhythmias
    - Beta blockers
    - Digoxin (positive inotrope used for dilated cardiomyopathy)
    - Implantable cardioverter/defibrillators (ICD)
  - Cardiac transplantation: DCM is most common reason
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

## Slide 63

- Less common than DCM or HCM
- Distinct characteristics as compared to DCM and HCM:
  - Non-dilated ventricles with (usually) normal wall thickness (although infiltrative disease may cause increase LV wall thickness)
  - Ventricular walls are rigid (“stiff ventricles”), resulting in severe diastolic dysfunction and restrictive filling with elevated filling pressures and dilated atria
  - LV systolic function usually remains normal (though will start to become impaired when RCM is severe)
- Restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM)

## Slide 65

- RCM develops Left and Right heart failure, and progression is usually rapid
  - SOB
  - Orthopnea
  - PND
  - JVD, peripheral edema, ascites
  - Gallop rhythm (S3 or S4) and regurg murmurs
- Restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM)

## Slide 66

- Restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM)
- Workup includes:
- ECG: nonspecific abnormalities, may show decreased QRS amplitude
- CXR: may show cardiomegaly (secondary to enlarged atria) and pulmonary congestion
- Echocardiogram: TOC – shows non-dilated, non-hypertrophied ventricles with bi-atrial enlargement, preserved systolic LV but abnormal diastolic function

## Slide 67

- Restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM)
- Workup includes:
- Cardiac MRI: provides data regarding the presence or absence of myocardial infiltration and fibrosis, patterns which inform the diagnosis of specific forms of RCM
- Endomyocardial biopsy: most accurate, but invasive and less commonly used. It may be necessary to confirm the presence of infiltrative disease if MRI is unclear/equivocal

## Slide 68

- Restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM)
- No cure; management is aimed at HF and arrhythmia
- RCM has the lowest one-year survival rates when compared to DCM and HCM;  most patients die within a few years of diagnosis
- Heart transplant may be performed but seldom advised because of the underlying primary disease

## Slide 71

- An inflammatory process with necrosis that involves the myocardium
- The histologic hallmark is a focal patchy or diffuse inflammatory infiltrate with adjacent myocyte injury
- Course of illness varies:
  - Asymptomatic, self-limiting
  - Fevers, chest pain, chills, N/V/D, arthralgia, myalgia
  - Acute HF,  arrhythmias and death
- Myocarditis

## Slide 72

- Etiologies:
  - Most commonly initiated by viral infection
    - The most common viruses associated with myocarditis in the United States and Western Europe in immunocompetent persons are adenoviruses, coxsackievirus B (enterovirus), parvovirus B19, herpes simplex, influenza A, and cytomegalovirus (CMV)
- Myocarditis

## Slide 73

- Etiologies also include:
  - Bacteria: (mycoplasma, staph, strep, haemophilus, tuberculosis…)
  - Fungus: (toxoplasmosis, coccidiomycosis, histoplasmosis)
  - Parasites: (Amebiasis)
  - Protozoan: (Chagas disease - South America)
  - Autoimmune conditions: (Giant-Cell Myocarditis)
  - Drugs, toxins, hypersensitivity reactions, collagen vascular diseases, and autoimmune reactions may also be contributing factors
- Myocarditis

## Slide 74

- Myocarditis may initially be overshadowed or completely masked by the constitutional symptoms of the illness
  - i.e. fever, malaise, fatigue, arthralgias, myalgias, and skin rash
- Red flag: frequently seek medical attention days/weeks after an acute flu-like syndrome
  - C/o of chest pain or present with s/s of acute onset of HF (dyspnea, fatigue, decreased exercise tolerance, palpitations, JVD, peripheral edema, ascites)
  - Other presentations may include syncope, ventricular arrhythmias, complete heart block, or emboli (such as a PE)
- Myocarditis

## Slide 75

- Workup:
  - ECG:  Tachycardia and non-specific ST and T wave changes
  - Positive cardiac enzymes
  - +/- cardiomegaly on CXR
  - Echo: many presentations; left ventricular dysfunction is commonly seen but findings can mimic HCM, DCM, RCM and ARVC
- Myocarditis

## Slide 76

- Supportive care
  - Hospitalize and monitor closely for HF,  arrhythmias
  - Rest
  - NSAIDs
  - Oxygen
  - +/- antibiotics
- Myocarditis

## Slide 77

- Heart Failure management
  - Restrict fluids
  - Diuretics (acutely, then continue depending on course)
  - ACE inhibitors or Beta Blockers (ongoing management)
- Anticoagulation necessary if embolic event (such as PE) or mural thrombi detected on ECHO
- In severe cases:  arrhythmia suppression, hemodynamic support, vasopressors, positive inotropes, intra-aortic balloon pumps
- Prolonged courses can result in the need for a heart transplant
- Myocarditis

## Slide 79

- Rare form of CM that is acquired in late pregnancy or early post-partum (mc during 1st month after delivery)
- LV systolic dysfunction with ejection fraction of ≤45%
- LV is frequently dilated; typically presents like a dilated cardiomyopathy
- Etiology is unknown
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM)

## Slide 80

- Risk factors:
  - Age > 30 years
  - African descent
  - Multiple gestation pregnancy
  - Hx preeclampsia, eclampsia, or postpartum hypertension
  - Maternal cocaine use
  - Prolonged tocolytic therapy with oral beta-adrenergic agonists (i.e. terbutaline)
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy

## Slide 81

- The clinical presentation is variable and similar to the other CMs
  - Dyspnea, cough, orthopnea, PND, pedal edema
    - Initial diagnosis may be delayed because of ‘normal’ pregnancy symptoms such as nonspecific fatigue, SOB, swelling of feet
  - PE findings may include an elevated JVP, a displaced apical impulse, S3, mitral regurgitation murmur
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy

## Slide 82

- For diagnosis, must meet all 3 criteria:
  - HF toward end of pregnancy or within 6 months of delivery
  - Absence of another identifiable cause of HF
  - LV systolic dysfunction = LVEF ≤ 45%
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy

## Slide 83

- Workup needs to include Echo and ECG
- Other studies such as BNP, CXR, cardiac MRI, cardiac catheterization, and endomyocardial biopsy may be helpful in selected cases
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy

## Slide 84

- Treatment of PPCM is similar to other forms of dilated CM but modifications are usually needed for safety of mother and unborn/breastfeeding baby
  - Avoid ACEi/ARBs in pregnancy/breastfeeding
- Prompt delivery is suggested in women with PPCM with advanced HF and hemodynamic instability
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy

## Slide 85

- Half of patients with PPCM will spontaneously recover; the other half will have persistent left ventricular dysfunction, heart failure and early death
  - 1 in 10 PPCM diagnoses will be fatal
- Advised to avoid future pregnancies due to risk of recurrence, HF progression and maternal death
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy

## Slide 87

- AKA “stress-induced CM” and “broken-heart syndrome”
- Transient LV dysfunction without significant coronary artery disease
- Precipitated by acute emotional or psychological distress
- Surge of catecholamines causes coronary artery spasm – presents like a STEMI
- Tako-tsubo Cardiomyopathy

## Slide 88

  - Results in transient apical ballooning, resembling its Japanese namesake (a pot used to trap octopus)
