## Slide 2

- Cardiac Devices
- More than 3 million people in the US have a conventional permanent pacemaker (PPM) and more than 300,000 have an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) with pacing capabilities.
- Pacemakers are generally placed for disease of the AV node or the His-Purkinje system.  Sometimes they are used for sinus node disease as well.

## Slide 3

- Do They Need a Pacemaker?
- There are plenty of instances in which a patient has a bradyarrhythmia when a PPM is not warranted, including bradycardia/blocks occurring
- While “under the influence” of medications that block the AV node
- In the presence of severe electrolyte disturbances, or toxic/ metabolic abnormalities
- During myocardial ischemia or infarction
- Due to infectious etiology, such as Lyme dz or IE
- After chest trauma

- Do They Need a Pacemaker?

## Slide 7

- Types of Pacemakers- Temporary
- Temporary transcutaneous or transvenous pacing is only used for a short time when there is life-threatening lack of intrinsic cardiac pacing.
- Major indications include:
  - Bradyarrhythmias causing hemodynamic instability
  - Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR)
  - Prophylaxis during the post-op period surrounding cardiac valvular surgery
  - Lyme disease or other infections (Chagas disease) that cause interval changes

## Slide 8

- Types of Pacemakers- Permanent
- Single: one lead is connected to the right ventricle.
- Dual chamber: two leads, one each to the right atria and right ventricle.
- Biventricular: three leads, one to the RA and one each to the right ventricle and left ventricle, a resynchronization device that allows the heart to beat more efficiently.  Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is used in HFrEF when altering the cardiac cycle may improve the cardiac output.

## Slide 9

- Pacemakers Parts
- Pacemakers consist of a pulse generator, located in a subcutaneous pocket in the chest wall and electrical leads, which travel through a major vein of the chest to reach the right side of the heart (in most patients).
- The pulse generator is the battery and brain of the pacemaker, creating the electrical impulse that travels though one or more leads to depolarize the heart chamber or augment the heart’s own electrical beat.

## Slide 10

- Pulse Generators
- The battery typically has a 10-year lifespan, although technologic advances are constantly improving PPMs.
- Pulse generators can be set to fixed-rate (asynchronous) or demand (synchronous) modes.
  - In the asynchronous mode, impulses are produced at a set rate, independent of intrinsic cardiac activity. There is a small, but inherent, risk of causing a lethal arrhythmia if the impulse coincides with the T-wave’s vulnerable period.
  - In the synchronous mode, the sensing circuit searches for an intrinsic depolarization potential. If this is absent, a pacing impulse is generated.

## Slide 11

- Pacemakers Parts
- The leads carry the electrical impulse from the pulse generator to the heart or may be sensing leads only.
- Most leads are transvenous- traveling through the subclavian vein to the heart.
- Leads may also be epicardial, threaded through the chest tissue and surgically attached to the heart.  This type of lead is used when there is an anatomical reason to avoid transvenous placement, such as venous stenosis.

## Slide 12

- Pacemakers Leads- Clinical Implications
- Transvenous pacemaker leads are associated with relatively small risks:
  - Trauma to cardiac structures at the time of insertion (tamponade, perforation)
  - Infection: bacteremia can seed a small clot on the leads
  - Venous thrombosis and resultant subclavian vein occlusion
  - Tricuspid valve injury, resulting in tricuspid regurgitation.

## Slide 13

- Pacemaker Indications
- Symptomatic bradycardia due to sinus node dysfunction (sick sinus syndrome), usually seen with heart rates less than 40 bpm
- Symptomatic AV block, usually complete heart block or second-degree AV block, type II
- Periods of asystole longer than 3 seconds or at least 5 seconds in persons with atrial fibrillation
- To facilitate AV node ablation
- Cardiac resynchronization therapy with biventricular pacing

## Slide 14

- Pacemaker Codes
- Electrophysiologists have developed a 5-letter code which describes the pacing mode of a device.  Typically, the first 3 letters are documented.
- Pacemaker Code:
  - First letter: refers to the chamber being paced (A =atria, V = ventricle, D =dual, both)
  - Second letter is the chamber being sensed       (A =atria, V = ventricle, D =dual/ both, O =none)

## Slide 15

- Pacemaker Code
- Third letter is the response mode of the pacemaker to the native cardiac electrical activity
  - I = the device is inhibited if it senses the patient’s own native beat
  - T =the device is triggered by the native atrial beat (PPM provides additive depolarization)
  - D =the pacemaker is triggered after a sensed atrial event but inhibited after a sensed ventricular event
  - O = none, usually means that the device is pacing continuously.

## Slide 16

- Pacemaker Code
- The fourth letter refers to the rate modulation- an automatic response of the pacemaker to chronotropic incompetence.
- R - Rate-response ("physiologic") pacing
- O - No programmability or rate modulation
- The fifth letter refers to multisite pacing (if the device is for CRT) and refers to the sites being electrically stimulated by the device.
- A = Atrial, V =Ventricle, D =Dual/ both, O = none

## Slide 27

- Implantable Cardioverter- Defibrillators (ICD)
- The ICD has revolutionized the treatment of patients at risk for sudden cardiac death due to ventricular tachyarrhythmias.
- First used in 1980 and approved by the FDA in 1985, the initial ICD device was big and bulky,  requiring abdominal cavity placement and a thoracotomy to place epicardial leads.

## Slide 28

- ICD
- Early ICDs were simple “shock boxes” and their battery life was limited to 2 years.
- Modern ICDs are considerably smaller, with an average battery life of 8 years, and have a much more detailed algorithm for arrhythmias, in addition to recording cardiac events.
- ICDs can perform dual-chamber, rate-responsive pacing for bradycardia for a short time.

## Slide 29

- ICDs
- Modern ICDs have sophisticated discrimination algorithms minimize shocks for AF, sinus tach, and other non–life-threatening supraventricular tachyarrhythmias.
- ICDs have a pulse generator and a shocking lead which travels to the ventricle.
- Most ICD leads are implanted transvenously (TV-ICD) and the technique is very similar to implantation of a pacemaker.

## Slide 30

- ICDs and Pacemakers
- In 2012, a subcutaneous (S-ICD) was FDA approved.  SubQ ICDs have a shocking lead, which is tunneled to the sternum.  These devices have limited pacing capabilities.
- [picture]
- A pacemaker should be combined with an ICD or a cardiac resynchronization device if one is required, so that all devices run off one pulse generator.

## Slide 31

- The Data Behind ICDs
- Multiple trials conducted in survivors of life- threatening ventricular arrhythmias (such as VT) have proven that ICD placement is superior to multiple antiarrhythmics, although a few trials did not show benefit.
  - Data is less robust for women and elderly
- Multiple trials have also shown benefit for persons who have strong risk factors for life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias (such as long QT), although this data is not nearly as robust.

## Slide 33

- Indications for ICD Implantation
- Primary prophylaxis indications
  - LVEF ≤30%, if due to MI, at least 40 days post-MI
  - LVEF ≤35% with NYHA Class II or III symptoms.  If due to prior MI, at least 40 days post-MI.
  - LVEF ≤40% due to prior MI, with inducible VT or VF on an electrophysiology study (EPS)
  - Structural heart disease with sustained VT
  - Syncope of undetermined origin, with inducible VT or VF on an electrophysiology study.
- The above are class I indications, there are additional class IIa indications.

> Notes: Class I means that the treatment is useful, that its benefit greatly outweighs the risk, and that it should be administered.
> Class IIa means that the benefit outweighs the risk and it is reasonable to administer the treatment.  Class IIa indications include:
> 	Unexplained syncope, significant LV dysfunction, nonischemic CM
> 	Sustained VT, normal or near-normal ventricular function
> 	Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (CM) with 1 or more major risk factors
> 	Arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia/cardiomyopathy (ARVD/C) with 1 or more risk factors for sudden cardiac death (SCD)
> 	Long QT syndrome, syncope or VT while receiving beta blockers
> 	Nonhospitalized patients awaiting heart transplant
> 	Brugada syndrome, with syncope or VT
> 	Catecholaminergic polymorphic VT, syncope or VT while receiving beta blockers
> 	Cardiac sarcoidosis, giant cell myocarditis, or Chagas disease

## Slide 34

- How Does an ICD Work?
- ICDs deliver an 80-joule shock for defibrillation of ventricular tachyarrhythmias including monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT), polymorphic VT, and ventricular fibrillation (VF).
- Following the shock, the device will re-evaluate the cardiac rhythm.
- If VT or VF persists following the initial shock, the device will reverse polarity between the electrodes and deliver subsequent shocks.

## Slide 35

- Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillators
- Sometimes the ICD will attempt to “overdrive pace” the arrhythmia rather than deliver a shock.
- The ICD will deliver a maximum number of shocks for a single episode of a ventricular arrhythmia (this is programmed) and then will not deliver more.
- The cardiology office is alerted that the device has delivered one or more shocks.

## Slide 36

- Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillators
- Patients are instructed to go to the ER if their ICD fires and should generally be admitted to correct any reversible cause and see the cardiology service.
- Modern transvenous ICDs are also capable of pacing a bradyarrhythmia and will record cardiac events.  Order a device interrogation.

- Device Precautions- ACLS and ICD
- During an event that requires ACLS, an ICD should act to defibrillate the patient.

## Slide 40

- PPM Precautions- Surgery
- Electrocautery during surgery can sometimes cause a pacemaker to malfunction, because the electromagnetic interference (EMI) from the cautery is picked up and interpreted by the PPM as cardiac electrical activity.

- [picture]

## Slide 41

- PPM Precautions- Surgery
- In this situation, called “oversensing”, the pacemaker does not fire because it thinks that the heart has normal electrical activity.
- However, what is actually happening is a profound bradycardia/ asystole in the pacemaker dependent patient during surgery.

## Slide 42

- ICD Precautions- Surgery
- ICDs can also be problematic during surgery.  EMI from cautery can be “oversensed” by an ICD and cause it to fire inappropriately during surgery, shocking the patient.
- Because unwanted patient movement from an ICD shock would likely create a hazard to the patient or surgical personnel, the ICD should be disabled prior to surgery, and the patient will have defibrillator pads in place during surgery.

## Slide 43

- PPM Precautions- Surgery
- Although electrocautery is the most significant issue, other electric devices used during surgery, such as nerve stimulators, can also create enough EMI to interfere with the PPM.
- Therefore, when surgery is planned, especially a procedure above the umbilicus in a patient with a PPM or ICD, cardiology should assist in pre-op planning.

## Slide 44

- PPM Management During Surgery
- For most permanent pacemakers, applying a magnet over the pacemaker will initiate asynchronous pacing at a fixed rate with a fixed AV delay.
- The PPM will simply pace the heart for the entire surgical procedure.
- However, if the pacemaker is at the end of battery life, the rate may not be adequate.

## Slide 45

- ICD Management During Surgery
- The ICD function is suspended during surgery by placing a magnet over the device.
- The surgical team should apply pacer/defib pads to the chest before prepping and draping, in case of an intra-op emergency.
  - If needed, an external defibrillator/ pacer would be used to deliver a shock or pacing through these pads.

## Slide 46

- Device Precautions- Surgery
- Post-op, the device is reprogrammed by cardiology, or the magnet is removed, and the patient is monitored carefully in the PACU or ICU on telemetry.
- If surgery was emergent or the device delivered shocks or pacing during the surgery, a closer interrogation by cardiology is needed.

## Slide 47

- Device Precautions- Central Lines
- Guidewires used to place central lines can short out or dislodge transvenous ICD or pacemaker wires and cause irreversible damage.
- [picture]
- The risk of dislodging the wire is highest in the first 3 months after PPM or ICD insertion.

## Slide 48

- Device Precautions: EMI
- Patients with PPM or ICDs are counseled to avoid:
- Cell phones, mobile devices, magnets, headphones and wireless chargers: avoid placing these within 6 inches (about 15 cm) of an ICD site when on.
- Security systems. ICDs may set off airport security alarms.  Hand-held metal detectors should be limited to less than 30 seconds over the ICD site or make a request for a manual search.

## Slide 49

- Device Precautions: EMI
- Patients with PPM or ICDs are counseled to avoid:
- Power generators. Stand at least 2 feet (0.6 meters) from welding equipment, high-voltage transformers or motor-generator systems.
- Devices that pose little or no risk to a PPM or an ICD include microwave ovens, televisions and remote controls, AM/FM radios, toasters, electric blankets, electric shavers and electric drills, computers, scanners, printers, and GPS devices.

## Slide 50

- Device Replacement
- Replacement- A pacemaker or ICD battery life is about 7-10 years, depending on how much the device is used.
- A pacemaker will begin slowing or changing their pacing mode as this dwindles.
- When this happens patients are scheduled for a generator change- which replaces the pocket device but leaves the wires alone.

## Slide 54

- Device Infections
- A generator change is more likely to cause a device infection than a de novo implant.
- In addition, the presence of a hematoma in the pocket increases the risk of infection from 0.98% to 22.7%.
- Anytime a pacemaker or ICD becomes infected, the device and the leads must be explanted.
- Patients will require a period of antibiotic therapy before a new device can be implanted.

## Slide 55

- ICD Complication: Inappropriate Shocks
- A major issue with ICDs are inappropriate shocks, which are not precipitated by accurate detection of a malignant ventricular arrhythmia (VT or VF)
  - One major trial revealed that 11.5% of the ICD pts received inappropriate shocks and that 31.2% of all ICD shocks were deemed inappropriate.
- Typically, inappropriate ICD shocks result when an atrial arrhythmia accelerates the ventricular rate beyond the limit set for delivery of an ICD shock.

## Slide 56

- ICD Complication: Inappropriate Shocks
- Inappropriate shocks are a significant issue, since the patient is counseled to go to the ER immediately if their ICD shocks them.
- In addition, ICD shocks HURT!
- Lastly, most cardiologists will recommend that patients with an ICD be shock free for six months before they drive.
