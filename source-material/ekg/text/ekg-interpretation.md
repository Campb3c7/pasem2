## Slide 6

- Atrial Depolarization and Contraction
- An impulse is initiated in the SA node
- The impulse depolarizes the atria, creating the P wave, and descends the intranodal pathways to the AV node
- The atria contract, pushing the remaining 15-30% of blood into the ventricles (the atrial kick)
- [picture]

## Slide 7

- AV Node Depolarization
- After slowly moving through the AV node, the impulse shoots to the bundle of His, bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers
- The impulse slows through the AV node to ensure the atria have ejected their blood into the ventricles first before the ventricles contract
- This slowing through the AV node produces the PR segment on the EKG

## Slide 8

- Ventricular Depolarization and Conduction
- Depolarization of the entire ventricular myocardium (bundle of His, bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers) produces contraction of the ventricles and the QRS complex
- Repolarization of the ventricles is represented by the ST segment and T wave

## Slide 18

- [picture]
- Portions of the EKG: QRS Complex
- QRS: ventricular depolarization
  - Q wave: first downward deflection after the P wave, not always present
  - R wave: first upward deflection after the P
  - S wave: first negative deflection after the R wave that extends below the baseline

## Slide 19

- QRS Complex: Amplitude
- QRS complexes: 5-30 mm in total height, a larger complex suggests ventricular hypertrophy or abnormal atrial repolarization
- Q waves
  - Physiologic Q waves are no more than 25% of the height of the R wave in the same lead
  - Pathologic Q waves are greater than 1/3rd the height of the R wave in the same lead

## Slide 20

- QRS Complex: Duration
- QRS complex: normally 0.06-0.12 seconds (1.5-3 little boxes)
- QT interval: normally 0.36-0.44 seconds (9-11 little boxes), from the onset of the QRS to the end of the T wave

## Slide 30

- 12 Lead EKG
- 12 leads are derived from 10 electrodes placed on the body
  - Electrical potential difference is the difference between two or more electrodes
- Electrical currents travel negative (reference) to positive (exploring) electrodes
  - When depolarization moves towards a positive electrode, it is represented as an upright deflection on EKG
  - Exception is aVR, which is typically a negative deflection due to its placement (some facilities use –aVR to correct this)

## Slide 31

- Limb Leads
- Examine the frontal plane (superior-inferior)
- Designated as I, II, III and aVF, aVL, aVR
- Leads are placed on right and left upper extremities and left lower extremity
  - Leads I, II, and III: difference between two electrodes
  - aVF, aVL, and aVR: average two electrodes against another one (difference really between three electrodes)

- Characteristics of a Normal EKG
- Regularity: regular

- Axis: no axis deviation

## Slide 56

- Determine the Rate
- The 300, 150, 100 method can only be used with accuracy on a regular rhythm

## Slide 77

- Abnormal QRS Complexes
- May be slightly abnormal to extremely wide, notched, or slurred
- Tall amplitude QRS complexes are usually caused by hypertrophy of one or both ventricles, an abnormal pacemaker, or aberrantly conducted beat
- Abnormally wide QRS complexes occur with aberrantly conducted or originated beats

## Slide 78

- Wide QRS: Supraventricular Origin
- A wide QRS may have several etiologies:
- Interventricular conduction defect: BBB, a block of the right or left bundle branches
- Ventricular pre-excitation: a portion of the ventricle is depolarized prematurely, causing an abnormal slurring or notching called the delta wave
- Pacemaker induced QRS: very narrow biphasic pacer spike followed by a wide QRS

## Slide 80

- Wide QRS: Ventricular Origin
- 4. A rhythm that initiates from the ventricles
  - Idioventricular rhythm: rate 20-40 bpm
  - Ventricular tachycardia: may be in bursts or sustained, a wide QRS without P waves, rate 100-250 bpm
  - 3rd degree AV block: ventricle pacing itself
  - Premature ventricular contractions: PVCs, intermittent wide complexes (not a rhythm alone)

## Slide 81

- QTc Interval
- QT interval: represents the time for ventricular depolarization and repolarization, 0.36-0.44 seconds
- QTc (corrected QT) interval: since QT interval will vary with faster or slower heart rates, a precise QT interval is calculated, correcting for heart rate
  - Normal QTc < 470ms for men, QTc < 480ms for women
  - One size does not fit all and the correction formula may be wrong at the extremes of HR

## Slide 82

- QTc Interval
- Prolonged QT may predispose patient to life threatening dysrhythmia (especially in the setting of MI or myocardial ischemia)
- Prolonged QT may be a dose limiting effect of some antiarrhythmics
- Shortened QT may be found in hypercalcemia or digoxin toxicity
  - Abnormal is QTc < 350ms

## Slide 96

- Atrial Dysrhythmias
- Atrial cardiac rhythm disturbance
- Affect ventricular filling time and diminish the atrial kick, decreasing cardiac output and ultimately tissue perfusion
- Three mechanisms believed to be responsible are enhanced automaticity, triggered activity, and reentry

## Slide 97

- Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)
- Early ectopic beats (not a rhythm) originating outside the SA node, there may be multiple sites
- May be brought about through enhanced automaticity or reentry mechanisms
- Healthy hearts: pregnancy, caffeine, alcohol
- Other causes: digoxin toxicity, electrolyte imbalance, hypoxia, high catecholamines

## Slide 98

- Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)
- In healthy hearts, considered insignificant, although patient may complain of palpitations or skipped beats
- In heart disease, frequent PACs may predispose the patient to worsening atrial dysrhythmias
- PACs may indicate electrolyte imbalance in AMI or CHF

- Atrial Tachycardia
- An ectopic focus that overrides the SA node at a rate > 100 bpm, often greater than 160 bpm
- Because of decreased filling time, patients with underlying heart disease may have significantly compromised cardiac output

## Slide 103

- QT interval: usually normal, may shorten

## Slide 106

- A Note AboutSupraventricular Tachycardia (SVT)
- When heart rates are very fast, it may be difficult to distinguish whether the tachycardia arises from the atria or AV junction because P waves are buried
- SVT is the catch-all that includes paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (pSVT), non-paroxysmal atrial tachycardia, multifocal atrial tachycardia, and junctional tachycardia

## Slide 107

- Wandering Atrial Pacemaker
- Shifting of the pacemaker between the SA node, atria, and/or AV junction
- At least 3 different P wave configurations in the same lead to diagnose
- May be physiologic or associated with organic heart disease or drug toxicity, particularly digitalis

## Slide 113

- Atrial Flutter
- Atrial rate of 250-350 bpm, ventricular rate varies but usually 50-150 bpm
- Results from reentry or increased automaticity
- Flutter waves are noted on EKG in a “sawtooth” pattern
- In healthy hearts, it is caused by acute enlargement of the atria and elevated atrial pressures

## Slide 114

- Atrial Flutter
- May also occur in the setting of severe mitral regurgitation, hyperthyroidism, pericardial disease, COPD, hypoxia, or in post-op patients
- May have a 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, etc. conduction ratio to the ventricles and the ventricular rhythm may or may not be regular (variable ratio)
- Fast or slow ventricular rates may lead to patient compromise

## Slide 115

- Atrial Flutter
- Regularity: usually regular
- Rate: atrial rate of 250-350 bpm, any ventricular rate
- P waves: sawtooth pattern, all the same shape, seemingly uncorrelated to the QRS
- QRS complex: narrow, 0.06-0.12 sec
- PR interval: unmeasurable
- T waves: often indistinguishable from P waves

## Slide 117

- Atrial Fibrillation
- Chaotic, asynchronous firing of multiple areas within the atria
  - Multiple, micro reentry circuits
- Atria quiver, and there is irregular, sporadic conduction through the AV node to the ventricles
- Results in loss of atrial kick and may result in formation of a clot within the atria

## Slide 118

- Atrial Fibrillation
- May occur with excess caffeine, alcohol, or tobacco ingestion
- Usually seen following cardiac surgery or other surgeries, with mitral stenosis, CHF or CAD, hyperthyroidism
- Defining characteristic is the presence of a chaotic baseline of fibrillatory waves and no discernable P waves
- Ventricular rate > 100 bpm is referred to as Rapid Ventricular Response (RVR) (i.e., atrial fibrillation with RVR)

## Slide 119

- Atrial Fibrillation
- Regularity: irregular
- Rate: atrial rate > 350 bpm, any ventricular rate
- P waves: wavy baseline with indistinguishable P waves, seemingly uncorrelated to the QRS
- QRS complex: narrow, 0.06-0.12 sec
- PR interval: unmeasurable
- T waves: often indistinguishable

## Slide 122

- Junctional Dysrhythmias
- Rhythms originate around the AV node and bundle of His
- Caused by suppression of the SA node or a block in conduction
- Commonly caused by hypoxia, ischemia, MI, and drug toxicity
- Atria depolarize in a retrograde fashion, which may lead to burial of the P in the QRS, abnormal P waves, or P waves after the QRS

## Slide 124

- Premature Junctional Contraction (PJC)
- Single electrical impulse (not a rhythm) in the AV junction occurring before the next expected sinus impulse
- Mechanism: enhanced automaticity or reentry
- > 4-6 per minute warrant further evaluation
- Causes include digoxin toxicity, excessive caffeine, inferior MI, rheumatic heart disease, or AV junction swelling after surgery

## Slide 142

- Ventricular Dysrhythmias
- Most are potentially life threatening due to compromise of cardiac output
- Result when the atria and AV junction are unable to initiate an electrical impulse or due to increased automaticity, most commonly seen in ischemia
- Rhythms coming from the ventricle have a wide and bizarre QRS and P waves are not visible

## Slide 143

- Premature Ventricular Contraction (PVC)
- Originates from an irritable focus in the ventricular myocardium or conduction system
- Caused by enhanced automaticity or reentry
- May be physiologic or pathologic
- PVCs may precipitate more serious dysrhythmias or may result in decreased cardiac output due to decreased diastolic filling time and loss of atrial kick

## Slide 144

- PVCs in Myocardial Ischemia
- PVCs serve as a strong warning of the potential development of life-threatening dysrhythmias if they are:
- Frequent
- Multifocal
- Early (R on T)
- Patterns of grouped PVCs (i.e., bigeminy, trigeminy, couplets, runs of V tach)

## Slide 164

- Heart Blocks
- Range from partial delays to complete interruptions in the cardiac conduction pathway between the atria and ventricles
- Most common causes include ischemia, myocardial infarction, degenerative disease of the conduction system, congenital anomalies, and certain medications (especially digitalis)

## Slide 165

- 1st Degree Heart Block
- Not a true block as all atrial impulses are conducted, rather a delay of conduction which prolongs the PR interval > 0.20 sec
- May occur in healthy or diseased hearts
- Usually of no clinical significance and patients may be asymptomatic
- However, it may progress to a higher block, especially in the setting of an inferior wall MI

## Slide 166

- 1st Degree Heart Block

## Slide 167

- 2nd Degree Block, Type I or Wenckebach
- An intermittent block of the AV node resulting in a progressive lengthening in the PR interval until a QRS is dropped, repeating in a cyclic pattern
- Often occurs in acute MI (inferior wall) or acute myocarditis
- Other causes include acute cardiac disease, drug toxicity, head injury, or electrolyte imbalance

## Slide 168

- 2nd Degree Block, Type I or Wenckebach
- May progress to a more serious block, especially if it occurs early in MI
- Patients may be symptomatic if dropped QRS complexes occur frequently due to loss of cardiac output
- In healthy hearts it may be transient and spontaneously resolve once the underlying abnormality is corrected

## Slide 169

- 2nd Degree Heart Block, Type I

## Slide 170

- 2nd Degree Heart Block, Type II
- An intermittent block at the level of the Bundle of His or bundle branches, resulting in non-conducted atrial beats
- The PR interval for conducted beats will be constant, and the QRS is intermittently dropped
- May be regular or irregular depending on the consistency of the conducted atrial beats
- QRS either normal or wide

## Slide 171

- 2nd Degree Heart Block, Type II
- More serious than type I as it more frequently progresses to complete heart block or asystole
- Usually associated with left CAD, anterior wall MI, or degeneration of conduction system
- Life threatening when symptomatic
- Slow ventricular rates result in decreased cardiac output

## Slide 172

- 2nd Degree Heart Block, Type II

- Electrical Axis
- Depolarization and repolarization of the myocardium produces many instantaneous vectors, the sum of which are recorded by the EKG machine
- The direction and magnitude of all the instantaneous vectors as they travel through the heart is the heart’s electrical axis

## Slide 188

- Electrical Axis
- Right ventricular vectors go to the right and left ventricular vectors to the left
- Left ventricular vectors are larger and persist longer due to the larger size of the LV in normal hearts, normally positioned in the chest
- When we look at a 12-lead EKG, some QRS complexes are positive, some are negative, and some are biphasic

## Slide 189

- [picture]
- The QRS is typically:
  - Positive in leads I, II, III, aVL, aVF, and V4-V6
  - Negative in leads aVR, V1-V3
  - May be biphasic in leads III, aVL, and V2-V4
- Remember that any lead on the EKG will be positive if an impulse is traveling towards it and negative if an impulse is traveling away from it

## Slide 202

- Altered Electrical Axis
- Displacing the heart will alter the axis
- Pregnancy, abdominal obesity, and any condition which significantly elevates the diaphragm may produce left axis deviation as the heart is pushed to a more horizontal position
- Thin individuals and children may produce right axis deviation as the heart is pushed to a more vertical position

## Slide 203

- Altered Electrical Axis
- Hypertrophy causes axis displacement toward the enlarged region due to larger muscle mass with greater electrical energy
- Infarction leads to necrotic tissue, which cannot depolarize, meaning that its vector is lost
  - The axis will point away from the infarction as the opposite vectors are now unopposed

## Slide 204

- Hypertrophy and Enlargement
- Hypertrophy: thicker myocardium than normal, which may cause the chamber of the heart to decrease in size
- Enlargement or dilation: thinner myocardium than normal as it is stretched, which may cause the chamber of the heart to increase in size
- Usually occur concomitantly although dilation is often associated with atria and hypertrophy with the ventricles

## Slide 205

- EKG Indicators of Hypertrophy or Enlargement
- Increased duration of a waveform
- Increased amplitude of a waveform
- Axis deviation

## Slide 206

- Lead V1: directly over the atria and allows easy separation of RA/LA components of the P wave
  - A biphasic P wave in lead V1 indicates enlargement of one or both atria

## Slide 238

- Stages of Myocardial Damage
- Ischemia: deprivation of oxygen and nutrients to the myocardium due to greater than normal need or a loss of blood supply
- Injury: progression of ischemia resulting in myocardial cell injury
- Infarction: death of injured myocardial cells, eventually infarcted tissue is replaced by fibrous scar tissue

## Slide 239

- Progressive EKG Indicators ofIschemia, Injury, and Infarction
- Changes in the T wave (peaking or inversion)
- Changes in the ST segment (depression or elevation)
- Enlarged Q waves or the appearance of new Q waves
- If an old EKG is available, a new BBB may be an indicator

## Slide 240

- T Wave Changes
- Symmetrical inverted T waves are characteristic of myocardial ischemia and indicate that ischemia is present through the full thickness of the myocardium
- Inverted T waves will revert to normal if ischemia subsides

## Slide 241

- T Wave Changes
- If myocardial infarction occurs, they may remain inverted for months to years because the damaged myocardium cannot repolarize normally
- T wave inversion may also be caused by pericarditis, ventricular hypertrophy, bundle branch block, shock, electrolyte imbalance, and subarachnoid hemorrhage
- Therefore, T wave inversion by itself is only indicative of ischemia if it is consistent with the patient’s overall clinical picture and is not diagnostic of infarction

- ST Segment Depression

  - 2 mm at the same point in the precordial leads
- In two or more leads facing the same anatomical area of the heart
