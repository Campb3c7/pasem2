## Slide 6

- Surgery Implications

## Slide 7

- DM and Surgery
- Perioperative Goals:
- Hyperglycemia is associated with increased infection and postoperative complications
- Excessively aggressive treatment increases hypoglycemia risk
- Perioperative glucose goal: 100–180 mg/dL
- For elective surgery: aim for A1C <8% within 3 months, when appropriate
- Monitor glucose throughout the perioperative period

## Slide 8

- DM and Surgery
- Medication Management:
- Metformin and most oral agents: hold day of surgery
- SGLT2 inhibitors: hold 3–4 days before surgery due to risk of perioperative/euglycemic DKA
- GLP-1–based therapy: individualize based on GI symptoms, dose, procedure/anesthesia, and aspiration risk
- Basal insulin: generally reduce rather than eliminate
- NPH: ~50%
- Long-acting basal analog: ~75–80%
- T1DM always requires basal insulin — avoid complete insulin interruption
- Insulin pumps may sometimes continue depending on procedure and institutional protocol

## Slide 10

- Pregnancy and DM - Patho
- Pregnancy is a progressively insulin-resistant state, particularly during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters
- The placenta produces hormones that oppose insulin action, helping ensure glucose and other nutrients remain available to the developing fetus
- Normally, pancreatic β-cells compensate by increasing insulin secretion
- If β-cell compensation is inadequate → maternal hyperglycemia / gestational diabetes

## Slide 11

- Pregnancy and DM - Screening
- Screen for GDM at 24–28 weeks in individuals not already known to have diabetes
- Earlier pregnancy: evaluate for preexisting diabetes when risk factors are present
- Screening may use either a one-step 75-g OGTT or two-step approach
- In the two-step approach:
  - 50-g 1-hour glucose challenge
  - If abnormal → 100-g 3-hour OGTT

## Slide 12

- Pregnancy and DM – Preexisting DM
- Preconception counseling is essential
- Goal before conception: A1C <6.5%, if safely achievable
- Assess diabetes complications, particularly retinopathy and kidney disease
- Review medications for pregnancy safety
- Hyperglycemia around conception/early pregnancy increases risk of congenital anomalies
- Later pregnancy hyperglycemia increases risk of macrosomia/LGA, obstetric complications, stillbirth, and neonatal complications

## Slide 13

- Pregnancy and DM – Treatment
- Lifestyle and medical nutrition therapy are first-line for GDM
- If medication is required, insulin is preferred
  - Metformin may be considered but has less data
- Glucose goals:
- Fasting <95 mg/dL
- 1-hour postprandial <140 mg/dL
- 2-hour postprandial <120 mg/dL
- Delivery timing is individualized based on diabetes type/control and maternal-fetal status.

## Slide 15

- Weight Management in T2DM
- Weight management is a primary treatment goal alongside glycemic management
- Lifestyle/behavioral treatment remains foundational
- 5–7% weight loss improves glycemia and cardiovascular risk factors
- >10% sustained weight loss generally provides greater metabolic benefit and may lead to diabetes remission
- Consider anti-obesity pharmacotherapy in appropriate patients

## Slide 16

- Weight Management in T2DM
- In T2DM with overweight/obesity, prioritize diabetes medications that also promote weight loss
  - Semaglutide and tirzepatide have high weight-loss efficacy
- Treatment should be individualized based on comorbidities, goals, risks, access, and patient preference

## Slide 17

- Weight Management in T2DM
- Consider metabolic surgery in appropriate surgical candidates with T2DM and:
- BMI ≥30 kg/m²
- BMI ≥27.5 kg/m² in Asian American individuals
- Potential benefits include:
- Substantial and durable weight loss
- Improved glycemic control
- Reduced medication requirements
- Possible T2DM remission
- Improved cardiovascular outcomes and mortality
- Requires experienced multidisciplinary care and long-term nutritional/metabolic monitoring.

## Slide 19

- Chronic Complications of Diabetes
- Diabetes causes both microvascular and macrovascular complications.
- Microvascular
  - Retinopathy
  - Kidney disease
  - Neuropathy
- Macrovascular (ASCVD)
  - Coronary artery disease / MI
  - Cerebrovascular disease / stroke
  - Peripheral artery disease
  - Risk increases with duration of diabetes and cumulative glycemic exposure.

- Why Does Diabetes Cause Vascular Damage?
- Chronic hyperglycemia contributes to:
- Formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs)
- Oxidative stress
- Inflammation
- Endothelial dysfunction
- Vascular injury

- Macrovascular: Lipoprotein Abnormalities

## Slide 26

- Macrovascular: Hypertension
- Hypertension is common in diabetes and increases cardiovascular and kidney risk.
- Goal BP <130/80 mmHg, if safely attainable
- Lifestyle management:
  - Weight management
  - Physical activity
  - Sodium reduction
  - Appropriate alcohol intake
- Antihypertensive therapy is often required
- Choice of medication should consider ASCVD, heart failure, and kidney disease

## Slide 67

- Diabetic Kidney Disease
- Diabetes is a major cause of CKD and kidney failure.
- DKD may manifest as:
- Albuminuria
- Declining eGFR
- Or both
- Chronic hyperglycemia and hypertension contribute to progressive glomerular injury.
- Microvascular: Nephropathy

## Slide 68

- Assess both:
- Spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)
- eGFR
- Begin:
- T1DM: after ≥5 years duration
- T2DM: at diagnosis
- If normal → at least annually
- If CKD present → monitor 1–4 times/year, depending on severity.
- Microvascular: Nephropathy

## Slide 69

- Albuminuria
- UACR <30 mg/g: normal to mildly increased
- UACR 30–299 mg/g: moderately increased albuminuria
- UACR ≥300 mg/g: severely increased albuminuria
- Because UACR varies, abnormal results should generally be confirmed with repeat testing
- Microvascular: Nephropathy

## Slide 70

- DKD Treatment:
- Optimize glycemic and blood pressure control
- ACE inhibitor or ARB recommended in diabetes + hypertension with UACR ≥30 mg/g
- Strongly recommended with UACR ≥300 mg/g and/or eGFR <60
- Monitor creatinine/eGFR and potassium
- Do not discontinue ACEi/ARB for a mild–moderate creatinine increase (≤30%) without volume depletion
- SGLT2 inhibitors provide important kidney and cardiovascular protection in appropriate patients with T2DM + CKD
- Microvascular: Nephropathy

- Diabetic Ketoacidosis

## Slide 79

- Diabetic Ketoacidosis
- Life-threatening acute complication of diabetes
- Hospitalizations for DKA have increased substantially in recent decades
- Mortality is now <1% with appropriate treatment, but risk increases substantially with severe illness/comorbidities
- Can occur in T1DM or T2DM

## Slide 80

- Diabetic Ketoacidosis
  - Diagnosis requires ALL 3:
- Diabetes/hyperglycemia: glucose ≥200 mg/dL OR known diabetes
- Ketosis: β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/L or urine ketones ≥2+
- Metabolic acidosis: pH <7.3 and/or HCO₃ <18 mEq/L

## Slide 81

- Diabetic Ketoacidosis Etiologies
- Missed or inadequate insulin
- Infection- usually UTI or pneumonia
- The initial presentation of acute type I DM
- In the setting of illness: MI, pancreatitis, surgery or trauma when insulin requirements increase
- Severe stress in type II DM, such as sepsis or trauma
- Complication of SGLT-2 usage (euglycemic DKA)
- As a complication of insulin pump failure or insulin leakage

## Slide 82

- Symptoms of DKA
- Onset within 24-48 hours
    - Polyuria and polydipsia
    - Fatigue/ weakness
    - Anorexia/ nausea/ vomiting
    - Tachypnea
    - Mental stupor progressing to coma
    - Abdominal pain
- May be subtle, especially early in course, but progresses rapidly.

## Slide 83

- Signs of DKA
- Evidence of dehydration: dry MM,  ↓ skin turgor, initially ↑ UOP that decreases
- Rapid deep breathing (Kussmaul respirations)
- Fruity breath odor
- Hypotension and tachycardia 2/2 to fluid/ electrolyte loss
- Abdominal pain: corresponds with severity of hyperglycemia
- Progressively worsening mental status/ lethargic stuporous patient- reflects the worsening acidosis

## Slide 84

- Lab Findings in DKA
- Hyperglycemia, degree varies: often 250-600 mg/dL
  - May be <200 in euglycemic DKA
- ↑ β-hydroxybutyrate: ≥ 3.0 mmol/L
- ↓ pH: <7.30
  - Severe DKA may be <7.0
- ↓ bicarbonate: <18 mEq/L
- Usually ↑ anion gap: often >12 mEq/L
- Urine/serum ketones positive
- Blood β-hydroxybutyrate is preferred for diagnosis and monitoring

## Slide 85

- Lab Findings in DKA
- ↑ BUN/creatinine from dehydration
- Sodium may be low due to hyperglycemia
- Serum K⁺ may initially be normal or elevated
- BUT total-body potassium is depleted
- Leukocytosis may occur from stress and does not automatically indicate infection

## Slide 86

- Why has my patient become so ill, so quickly?
- Hyperglycemia
- In DKA there is insufficient insulin causing increased hepatic production of glucose and diminished glucose uptake by peripheral tissues.
- In DKA there are ↑ counterregulatory hormones, especially glucagon,  released in response to intracellular energy deficit, causing increased hepatic glucose output

## Slide 87

- Why has my patient gotten so ill, so quickly?
- Ketoacidemia
- In DKA there is too little insulin and an excess of stress hormones causing breakdown of fat and creation of ketone bodies.
- Insulin insufficiency also causes reduced ketone breakdown in peripheral tissues
- There is a too much glucagon causing increased lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis

## Slide 88

- Why has my patient gotten so ill, so quickly?
- Fluid and Electrolyte Depletion
- Osmotic diuresis results from hyperglycemia with loss of water and electrolytes.
- Ketonuria causes an osmotic diuresis as well
- The average water loss in DKA is 5L

## Slide 89

- Prevention of DKA
- Patient education to recognize early s/s, as well as situations when DKA is likely
- Home measurement of urine ketones if s/s of infection or if unexpected persistently high glucose levels noted
- Education in the way to obtain supplemental liquids and electrolytes
- Extra insulin on a sliding scale, education

## Slide 90

- Prevention of DKA
  - If persistent ketonuria occurs x2 on urine tests: patients should contact their physician
  - Especially if vomiting develops, or if increased insulin doesn’t correct the hyperglycemia
  - Patient will likely need ED eval/ hospitalization
  - Recurrent episodes of DKA usually indicate poor compliance with therapy and diet.  PCP counseling and more provider oversight is required.

## Slide 91

- Emergent Treatment of DKA
- Step 1:
- Begin with a brief H&P, looking for precipitants
- Place patient on tele, large bore IV of NS wide open
- Check CBC, lytes, cultures, ABG if critically ill.  EKG, CXR and UA.
- Where are the major sources of infection?  What will you expect to see on labs?

## Slide 92

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- Fluid Replacement:
- Begin isotonic saline or balanced crystalloid
- Initial rate: 500–1,000 mL/hr for the first 2–4 hours
- Then adjust based on:
  - hemodynamics
  - fluid balance/urine output
  - sodium
  - comorbidities
- Use smaller boluses/caution in older adults, HF, or kidney disease

## Slide 93

- Emergent Treatment of DKA
- Step 2:
- More detailed H&P while awaiting labs
- Next step depends on the serum potassium
- K+> 5 mEq- begin an insulin gtt
- K+ of 3.5-5 mEq - begin K+ replacement and insulin gtt
- K+ < 3.5 mEq - begin K+ replacement and hold off on insulin gtt

## Slide 94

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- Insulin Replacement
- IV regular insulin: 0.1 units/kg/hr
- Routine IV insulin bolus is not necessary
- Prevent cerebral edema from a rapid decline in serum osmolality with IV fluid choice, initially 0.9 or 0.45% NS.
- Convert to D5 once BG < 250.  Continue insulin until ketoacidosis resolves; do not stop solely because glucose normalizes.

## Slide 95

- What does insulin replacement do physiologically?
- Insulin corrects acidosis by:
  - reducing hepatic ketone production
  - improves ketone removal from the blood
  - reducing fatty acids
- Insulin reduces hyperosmolality by:
  - increasing tissue uptake of glucose
  - decreasing hepatic production of glucose.
- Insulin reduces hyperglucagonemia

## Slide 96

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- Potassium Replacement
  - Potassium loss occurs from vomiting/ polyuria
  - Acidosis shifts K+ into the extracellular space so initially measurements may be high or  normal
  - Correction of acidosis causes K+ to shift into cells so begin replacement in the 2nd or 3rd hours as acidosis resolves
  - (10-30mEq/ hr)
  - If pts are uremic, have poor UOP or persistent K >5, hold off on potassium supplementation until UOP improves.

## Slide 97

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- Phosphate replacement
  - May or may not be needed, replace slowly
- Hyperchloremia
  - Hyperchloremia should self resolve within 12-24 hours of stopping saline administration
- Sodium Bicarbonate
  - Used to correct a very severe acidosis (pH< 7.0)
  - Use is highly controversial as it may predispose to harm, reserved for the most critical acidosis

## Slide 98

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- Treatment of associated infections
- Meticulous documentation/ communication with family and other providers
- Strict I/ Os
- Anti-emetics
- May need an NG tube placement to decompress the stomach and prevent aspiration and continued vomiting.
- Avoid sedatives/ narcotics

## Slide 99

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- DKA Resolution
- DKA is considered resolved when:
  - β-hydroxybutyrate <0.6 mmol/L
  - AND venous pH ≥7.3 OR HCO₃⁻ ≥18 mEq/L
  - Ideally glucose <200 mg/dL

## Slide 100

- Hospital Treatment of DKA
- Once the acidosis resolves and the precipitant improves, the patient may be restarted on their home DM meds.
- Often, DKA indicates poor control, so consider medication adjustment and DM education to address this.
- Discuss with the patient that they were seriously/ critically ill.
- Arrange for close PCP/endo follow up

## Slide 101

- Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State

## Slide 102

- Hyperglycemic, Hyperosmolar State
- Life-threatening hyperglycemic emergency
- Occurs most commonly in older adults with T2DM, but can occur with any type of diabetes
- Develops more gradually than DKA, typically days to weeks
- Characterized by severe hyperglycemia, hyperosmolality, and profound dehydration
- Mortality remains approximately 5–10× higher than DKA

## Slide 103

- Hyperglycemic, Hyperosmolar State
- Common precipitating factors:
  - Infection
  - MI or stroke
  - Surgery/acute illness
  - Inadequate diabetes treatment or newly diagnosed diabetes
  - Medications that worsen hyperglycemia or dehydration
  - Limited access to fluids / inability to maintain hydration

## Slide 104

- Pathogenesis of HHS
- A partial or relative insulin deficiency reduces glucose utilization in peripheral tissues.
- There is usually enough insulin to largely suppress lipolysis and ketogenesis, but not enough to prevent severe hyperglycemia.
- ↑ counterregulatory hormones (including glucagon) further increase hepatic glucose production.

## Slide 105

- Pathogenesis of HHS
- Severe hyperglycemia exceeds the renal threshold → glucosuria and osmotic diuresis
- If the patient cannot maintain adequate fluid intake, profound dehydration develops.
- ↓ circulating volume → ↓ renal perfusion → decreased glucose clearance → worsening hyperglycemia

## Slide 106

- Pathogenesis of HHS
- Declining renal perfusion/function develops, the kidney is less and less able to rid the body of glucose.
- Increasing glucose + profound free-water loss → hyperosmolality
- Increasing hyperosmolality can cause progressive neurologic dysfunction
- Confusion, lethargy, focal neurologic deficits, seizures, and coma may occur.

## Slide 107

- HHS Clinical Findings
- Onset may be insidious over days or weeks
- Weakness, polyuria, and polydipsia
- Lethargy, confusion and progressive decrease in cognition.
- Convulsions and deep coma if untreated
- Low fluid intake may occur in
  - Nausea, inappropriate lack of thirst
  - Elderly, bedridden pts with inaccessibility to fluids

## Slide 108

- HHS Laboratory Findings
- Diagnosis of HHS Requires ALL 4
- Glucose ≥600 mg/dL
- Hyperosmolality
  - effective serum osmolality >300 mOsm/kg
  - OR total serum osmolality >320 mOsm/kg
- No significant ketonemia
  - β-hydroxybutyrate <3.0 mmol/L
  - or urine ketones <2+
- No significant acidosis
  - pH ≥7.3
  - bicarbonate ≥15 mEq/L

## Slide 109

- HHS Laboratory Findings
- CBC: hemoconcentration; leukocytosis may occur from physiologic stress
- BUN/Cr: often elevated from profound dehydration/AKI
- Na⁺: variable; may be low, normal, or high
- K⁺: variable initially, despite total-body potassium depletion
- Ketones: absent to mild
- Acid-base status: generally no significant metabolic acidosis

## Slide 110

- Treatment of HHS
- Fluid Replacement
- Fluid replacement is the cornerstone of treatment
- Begin 0.9% saline or balanced crystalloid
- Typical initial rate: 500–1,000 mL/hr for first 2–4 hours
- Then adjust based on:
  - hemodynamics
  - sodium
  - urine output/fluid balance
  - serum osmolality
- Use caution with older adults, HF, or kidney disease

## Slide 111

- Treatment of HHS
- Insulin
- Fluid replacement alone may substantially lower glucose
- After initial fluid resuscitation, IV regular insulin may be started
- IV insulin: 0.05 units/kg/hr
- Monitor glucose and potassium closely
- Add dextrose as glucose approaches 250 mg/dL so insulin can continue while hyperosmolality resolves

## Slide 112

- DKA vs HHS
|  | DKA | HHS |
| Onset | Hours–days | Days–weeks |
| Glucose | Variable; may be <200 | ≥600 mg/dL |
| Ketosis | Significant | Minimal/absent |
| Acidosis | Present | Absent/minimal |
| Hyperosmolality | Variable | Marked |
| Dehydration | Significant | Profound |
| Neuro changes | Variable | Often prominent |
| Initial insulin | 0.1 U/kg/hr | 0.05 U/kg/hr |
| Mortality | Lower | 5–10× higher |
