## Slide 10

## Slide 11

- Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis (HPA)
- The hypothalamus produces corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) in response to sympathetic nervous system activation
- Increased CRH levels trigger the pituitary gland to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)
- ACTH binds to receptors on the adrenal cortex, stimulating the secretion of cortisol
- Once a certain level of cortisol is achieved, negative feedback shuts off secretion of CRH and ACTH

## Slide 13

- Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Thyroid Axis (HPT Axis)
- The hypothalamus produces hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH)
- TRH stimulates pituitary production of thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
- TSH stimulates the thyroid gland to produce T3 and T4
- T3 and T4 shut off production of TSH and TRH via negative feedback

## Slide 15

- Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal Axis (HPG)
- The hypothalamus secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH).
- GNRH stimulates pituitary production of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)
- LH stimulates production of testosterone from the testicles in males; FSH stimulates estrogen production in females by the ovaries
- Presence of testosterone in males and estrogen/ progesterone in females shuts off production of GnRH and LH/FSH

- The hypothalamus produces growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH)

- Secretion of growth hormone stimulates production of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) in the tissues, primarily the liver

## Slide 22

- Classifying Endocrine Dysfunction
- Hypofunction: Insufficient hormone secreted by the gland
- Hyperfunction: Excess hormone secretion by the gland
- Level of the hypothalamic-pituitary-target organ axis at which the problem originates:
  - Primary (most common): Caused by the endocrine gland itself
  - Secondary: Dysfunction from abnormal pituitary activity
  - Tertiary: Dysfunction originating at the level of the hypothalamus

## Slide 23

- Common Causes of Pituitary Dysfunction
- Hormone deficiency: autoimmune glandular destruction, infection, inflammatory disease, genetic mutations, surgery, mass effect
- Excess hormone production: benign tumors, neoplasm, autoimmune disease, excess hormone administration, genetic activating mutations
- Resistance to hormones: genetic defects cause dysfunctional membrane receptors and signaling

## Slide 24

- Hypopituitarism

## Slide 25

- Hypopituitarism
- Inadequate production of one or more anterior pituitary hormones from disease of the pituitary or hypothalamus
- Diagnostic lab pattern – a LOW pituitary hormone level in the setting of a LOW target hormone level
  - Ex: a low TSH with a low T4 level
- Treatment typically involves hormone replacement therapy
- Congenital cases often present gradually, with short stature and growth failure due to GH and TSH deficiency
  - Followed by lack of pubertal development due to LH/FSH deficiencies
- Patients with long-standing hypopituitarism tend to have dry, pale, fine, wrinkled facial skin and an apathetic affect.

## Slide 26

- Hypopituitarism Etiologies
- Mass lesions: Pituitary adenomas, cysts
- Pituitary surgery/radiation
- Infiltrative disease: Sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis
- Infection: TB, syphilis
- Infarction: Sheehan Syndrome (postpartum necrosis)
- Genetic mutations

## Slide 27

- Hypothalamic Etiologies
- Mass lesions: Benign (craniopharyngiomas) or malignant metastatic lesions
- Radiation: CNS or nasopharyngeal cancers
- Infiltrative disease: Sarcoidosis
- Infections: TB, syphilis
- Other: Traumatic brain injury, stroke

## Slide 28

- Hypopituitarism: Clinical Manifestations
- The clinical manifestations depend on the cause as well as the type and degree of hormonal insufficiency.
- Patients may be asymptomatic, present with symptoms related to specific hormone deficiency or mass lesion, or present with nonspecific symptoms (fatigue).

## Slide 29

- Growth Hormone (GH) Deficiency
- GH is the most abundant anterior pituitary hormone
- GH promotes linear growth in children by stimulating cartilage growth, particularly at the epiphyseal plate.
- Regulates metabolism in adults, particularly glucose control, while also contributing to muscle development, bone density, cholesterol levels and brain function

## Slide 30

- GH Function
- Many of GH physiologic effects are mediated through IGF-1 and the liver is a major source of circulating IGF-1

## Slide 31

- Growth Hormone Effects
- Bones
  - Children: GH stimulates longitudinal growth by increasing the formation of new bone and cartilage, beginning gradually during the 1st year of life and peak at puberty
  - Adults: Increases bone turnover leading to bone formation and to a lesser extent bone resorption
- Adipose tissue- Release and oxidation of free fatty acids by breaking down stored triglycerides.
- Skeletal muscle- GH stimulates amino acid uptake to be used for protein synthesis and cell proliferation of skeletal muscle while limiting breakdown of existing protein

## Slide 32

- Growth Hormone Effects
- Liver
  - GH stimulates IGF-1 production and release
  - GH reduces glucose uptake in the liver and promotes gluconeogenesis resulting in increased hepatic glucose output
- Immune system- GH influences the responses of antibody production by B-cells, T-lymphocyte function, macrophage activity and natural killer cell activity
- CNS- GH modulates mood and behavior

## Slide 33

- GH Deficiency Children “Pituitary Dwarfism”Clinical Manifestations
- Congenital GH deficiency: presents in newborns with hypoglycemia, jaundice, and decreased rate of growth later (6-12 months)
- Acquired GHD in children: presents with severe growth failure, delayed bone age, and increased weight: height ratios
  - Causes of acquired GHD include intracranial tumors, cranial irradiation, and head trauma

## Slide 35

- GH Deficiency: Why are children hypoglycemic?
- GH has anti-insulin activity - GH suppresses the ability of insulin to stimulate uptake of glucose in the tissues; GH also stimulates gluconeogenesis in the liver
- Therefore, in GH deficiency, there is unopposed insulin activity allowing for hypoglycemia

## Slide 36

- Hypopituitarism: GH Deficiency Workup
- GH is secreted in a pulsatile manner and peaks ~1hr after falling asleep
  - Therefore, it may be undetectable when drawn between pulses
- Diagnosis involves clinical assessment, growth analysis, bone age study and IGF-1 testing
  - PRO: IGF-1 levels are stable throughout the entire day
  - CON: IGF-1 levels alone are not specific to GH deficiency, as they are also low in GH insensitivity, fasting state, malnutrition, hypothyroidism, diabetes, or renal and hepatic disease
- When congenital the diagnosis can be made if noted severe growth failure, delayed bone age, and very low serum concentrations of GH, IGF-1

## Slide 37

- Hypopituitarism: GH Deficiency Workup
- Pituitary MRI should be performed to eval for structural defects or masses
- When diagnosis is in doubt, provocative stimuli can be used to aid in the diagnosis.
  - Glucagon induced hyperglycemia or insulin-induced hypoglycemia will provoke GH secretion to a level > 7μg/L in children without deficiency
- If pituitary insufficiency is found, test for and correct any additional hormone deficits
- Diagnosis is excluded in children with normal bone age study and height velocities

## Slide 42

- Treating GH Deficiency and Resistance in Children
- Replacement therapy with daily recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) via subcutaneous injection restores growth velocity
  - Continued until linear growth is completed
- In patients with GH insensitivity and growth retardation due to mutations of the GH receptor, treatment with biosynthetic IGF-I bypasses the dysfunctional GH receptor.

## Slide 43

- GH Deficiency in Adults
- Often caused by acquired hypothalamic or pituitary somatotrope damage and often undiagnosed
  - Why?
- Causes reflect general hypopituitarism etiologies
  - Pituitary tumor or consequence of treatment of a tumor (76%)
  - Extrapituitary tumor (ex: craniopharyngioma) (13%)
  - Unknown cause (8%)
  - Sarcoidosis (1%)
  - Sheehan Syndrome (0.5%)

## Slide 44

- GH Deficiency in Adults Symptoms and Clinical Manifestation
- Variable symptoms: central obesity, reduced physical and mental energy, impaired concentration and memory, depression, low self esteem
  - Patients may also have reduced muscle mass, ↑ LDL levels, LV dysfunction and reduced cardiac output with exercise. (↑cardiovascular disease and related mortality)
  - Chronic GH deficiency leads to osteopenia and an increased risk of fractures.

## Slide 45

- Evaluating for GH deficiency in Adults
- Overall GHD in adults is rare
- Reserve testing for patients with any of the predisposing factors:
  - 1. Pituitary surgery
  - 2. Pituitary or hypothalamic lesion
  - 3. History of cranial irradiation
  - 4. Radiologic evidence of pituitary lesion
  - 5. Childhood requirement for GH replacement therapy
- GH deficiency is present in 96% of patients and can be diagnosed if 3+ other pituitary hormone deficiencies + a low serum IGF-1 <84mcg/L.

## Slide 46

- Evaluating for GH Deficiency in Adults
- Approx. ¼ GH-deficient adults have low-normal IGF-1
- Glucagon Stimulation Test
  - Administer 1mg glucagon IM, weight<90kg or 1.5mg>90kg fasting for 8-9hr
  - GH sampled before administration and every 30min for 4hr
  - GH <3mcg/L diagnostic

## Slide 47

- Evaluating for GH Deficiency in Adults
- Insulin-induced hypoglycemia test
  - Administer 0.05-0.1U/kg regular insulin, IV
  - When glucose levels reach ~40 mg/dL, GH will surge, peaking at60 mins and remaining elevated for 2 hours
  - Adult GHD is defined by a peak GH< 3 μg/L
  - Contraindicated in the following: patients with diabetes,ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, or epilepsy andin elderly patients

## Slide 48

- GH deficiency in Adults: Treatment
- Recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) injections 3x weekly
  - Dose is titrated until IGF-1 levels are normal
  - Side effects: reversible dose-related fluid retention, joint pain, and carpal tunnel syndrome (30%); myalgias and paresthesia (40%).
  - Treatment is associated with increased muscle mass, decreased body fat, and an improvement in some parameters of cardiac function.

## Slide 49

- Dwarfism due to Achondroplasia
- The most common cause of short-limb dwarfism
- A bone dysplasia resulting from genetic mutation of fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) gene
  - Spontaneous mutation in 80% of cases and then autosomal dominant when passed to offspring
  - Not pituitary in nature
- Characteristic features: short arms and legs, an enlarged head, and an average-sized trunk.

## Slide 51

- Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH) Deficiency
- Primarily stimulates the production and release of cortisol  and androgens (DHEA) by the adrenal cortex
  - To a lesser degree stimulates mineralocorticoid (Aldosterone)
- Cortisol is the primary stress hormone involved in regulation of the body’s stress response, glucose regulation, metabolism and more
- [picture]

## Slide 52

- Hypopituitarism: ACTH Deficiency
- Low cortisol production due to destruction or compression of the pituitary gland can lead to secondary adrenal insufficiency
  - Pathogenesis: Damage of the pituitary gland leads to functional atrophy of the adrenal cortex within 2 weeks, resulting in diminished cortisol production
- ACTH deficiency may occur in isolation or in conjunction with other anterior pituitary hormone deficiencies

## Slide 53

- Causes of ACTH Deficiency
- MC- withdrawal of chronic glucocorticoid treatment (typically ≥5mg prednisone daily for greater than 2wk
- Hypopituitarism etiology- (slide 26-27)
- Isolated ACTH deficiency: Rare
  - Autoimmune disease (most common)
  - Genetic mutations (rare)
- Drugs –
  - High dose progestins (Megestrol acetate) used in metastatic breast cancer treatment and appetite stimulant in patients with cancer anorexia. This drug binds to glucocorticoid receptors and exerts some activity however with withdrawal can cause secondary adrenal insufficiency.
  - Chronic opiate use- interferes with cortisol secretion

## Slide 54

- Secondary ACTH Deficiency: Symptoms
- Patients tend to have symptoms of chronic adrenal insufficiency or signs of deficient secretion of other anterior pituitary hormones
- However, adrenal crisis can occur when the loss of pituitary function is sudden and severe, as in pituitary apoplexy
  - The symptoms in these patients are due mainly to acute cortisol deficiency and present as shock
  - Pt may c/o severe headache and visual change (loss or reduced visual fields)

## Slide 55

- Secondary ACTH Deficiency: Symptoms
- Symptoms: fatigue, weakness, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, myalgias/arthralgias, occasional hypoglycemia
  - Others: amenorrhea, psychiatric symptoms
  - Mild symptoms may only be seen when under stress
- In contrast to primary adrenal failure (Addison’s disease), secondary is not accompanied by hyperpigmentation or aldosterone deficiency
  - There is also less hypovolemia than in primary disease

## Slide 56

- ACTH Deficiency: Initial Evaluation
- Serum cortisol level (8 am) and plasma ACTH level
  - A LOW ACTH level + LOW cortisol level <3mcg/dL indicates secondary (or tertiary) adrenal insufficiency
    - Cortisol level between 3-18mcg/dL is inconclusive and requires ACTH stimulation test
    - Cortisol level >18mcg/Dl excludes adrenal insufficiency
- ACTH Stimulation test: Administration of IM or IV cosyntropin 0.25mg(synthetic ACTH) followed by serum cortisol level measurement 45min after injection
  - Testing requires all corticosteroids be held for 18-24hr prior to lab draw.
  - Cortisol <18mcg/dL indicates probable adrenal insufficiency
  - Atrophied adrenal cortex will cause a suboptimal rise of cortisol

## Slide 57

- ACTH Deficiency: Additional Evaluation
- Additional labs:
  - Hyponatremia (70-80%) - can occur early in secondary adrenal insufficiency and may be the initial manifestation
  - Low DHEA helps to confirm
- Assess for additional anterior pituitary hormone deficiencies
- Consider MRI of the brain for pituitary tumors

## Slide 58

- Treating ACTH Deficiency
- Treatment involves corticoid replacement with PO hydrocortisone or PO Prednisone
  - Hydrocortisone (short acting) is divided into 2-3 daily doses to mimic normal diurnal rhythm and not likely to exceed 20mg daily
  - Prednisone (long acting) 5mg daily
  - Doses must be increased in times of acute illness or stress
- Treat any additional hormone deficiencies
  - Replacement of thyroid hormone without replacement of glucocorticoids can precipitate acute adrenal insufficiency.

## Slide 59

- Patient Education
- Patients should wear medical alert bracelets and/or carry identification cards with information about their glucocorticoid requirements.
- Provide injectable glucocorticoid with vials of saline and educate patient and family about use
  - Administer IM in the following scenarios: an injury with substantial blood loss or fracture, nausea and vomiting with inability to tolerate PO intake, symptoms of acute adrenal insufficiency, unresponsiveness

## Slide 61

- Patient Education
- 3x3 rule: During minor illnesses (ex: URIs), increase the dose of glucocorticoid to 2-3x the usual daily dose for three days
- Patients are more likely to have complications as a result of fasting during Ramadan.
  - Implement sick day rules, optimize steroid dosing, and consider alternative fasting (nonconsecutive or trial fasting) if high risk
  - Pregnant or breastfeeding patients should avoid fasting
  - Secondary adrenal insufficiency carries lower risk than primary

## Slide 62

- Gonadotropin DeficiencyAKA: Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism
- Hormones of the anterior pituitary, follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) that regulate ovarian and testicular function
  - FSH- regulate ovarian estrogen production and ovarian follicle development in women. Spermatogenesis in men
  - LH- regulate ovulation and maintenance of corpus luteum in women and testosterone function of men

## Slide 63

- Congenital Gonadotropin Deficiency Clinical Manifestations
- Insufficiencies of LH and FSH cause hypogonadism and infertility
  - Congenital gonadotropin deficiency: caused by genetic mutation
    - Micropenis at birth in males, partial or complete lack of pubertal development, abnormal olfaction
    - Girls potential amenorrhea or, menarche followed by amenorrhea
    - Midline craniofacial defects-cleft palate, cleft lip, absent nasal cartilage
    - Neurologic deficits- decreased cognition, ataxia, neurosensory hearing loss, oculomotor dysfunction, color blindness
    - MSK malformation-pectus excavatum, syndactyly

## Slide 64

- Acquired Gonadotropin Deficiency Clinical Manifestation
- Acquired Hypogonadism- caused by any disease process that affects the hypothalamus-pituitary axis.
- Acquired Hypogonadism is the most common manifested presentation of hypopituitarism in adults
- In premenopausal women: diminished ovarian function -oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea, infertility, decreased vaginal secretions, decreased libido, and breast atrophy.
- In adult men: decreased libido and potency, infertility, decreased muscle mass with weakness, reduced beard and body hair growth, soft testes, and characteristic fine facial wrinkles
- Osteoporosis occurs in untreated women and men

## Slide 65

- Acquired Secondary Hypogonadism
- A mass lesion in the pituitary or hypothalamus is more likely to diminish secretion of gonadotropins than corticotropin (ACTH) or TSH.
  - Therefore, patients may present with isolated hypogonadism
- Other causes of secondary hypogonadism: hyperprolactinemia, chronic steroid use, chronic opioid use, critical illness (surgery), anorexia nervosa, starvation, extreme exercise, stress, damage to gonadotroph cells

## Slide 66

- Gonadotropin Deficiency Workup
- Males: draw fasting labs between 8-10 AM
  - They will have low testosterone with a low/normal serum LH
  - Sperm counts will also be low
- Females: suspected in nonpregnant women with amenorrhea or oligomenorrhea (in the absence of acute illness, hyperthyroidism or hyperandrogenism)
  - Low serum estradiol  with a low/normal serum FSH

## Slide 67

- Gonadotropin Deficiency Workup
- Evaluate for additional hormonal abnormalities
  - Prolactin- hyperprolactinemia can result in negative feedback on gonadotropins
  - Cortisol and thyroid function
- Men with isolated hypogonadotropic hypogonadism should consider genetic testing, esp. if fertility is desired
- Pituitary MRI should always be performed if there are other pituitary hormone abnormalities, visual field deficits or neurologic symptoms

## Slide 68

- Treating Gonadotropin Deficiency
- Treatment of puberty and sex characteristics: testosterone replacement in males; cyclical estrogen and progesterone replacement in females
- If fertility is desired:
  - Women: FSH injections are used for follicular development followed by LH injection for ovulation induction
  - Men are treated with pulsatile GnRH therapy (infusion pump)

## Slide 69

- Thyrotropin Deficiency
- Thyroid Stimulating Hormone (TSH) released from the anterior pituitary following stimulation by Thyrotropin-Releasing Hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus
- TSH stimulates release of T3 and T4 which are responsible for metabolism, weight, temperature regulation, energy levels, and skin, hair and nail health

## Slide 70

- Hypopituitarism: TSH Deficiency Symptoms
- Common symptoms: fatigue, cold intolerance, muscle cramps, headache, and weight gain
  - Symptoms may be milder than those of primary disease
- Growth hormone replacement therapy in GH deficient adults may unmask central hypothyroidism

## Slide 71

- Hypopituitarism: TSH Deficiency
- TSH deficiency is a rare cause of central hypothyroidism
- Pituitary mass lesions, especially pituitary adenomas, are the most common cause of central hypothyroidism
  - Compression of pituitary thyrotrophs result in ↓ TSH secretion
  - Etiologies also include the other hypopituitarism etiologies
- Patients with central hypothyroidism frequently have other pituitary hormone deficiencies.

## Slide 72

- Hypopituitarism: TSH Deficiency Lab & Treatment
- Patients will have a low T4 with a TSH that may be low, normal or mildly elevated
  - Normal or elevated is partly due to TSH that is secreted but has abnormally reduced biologic activity
  - Suspicious labs warrant MRI imaging of the hypothalamic-pituitary region
- Treat with Levothyroxine (Synthroid)
  - Special consideration: Treat after the patient is assessed for cortisol deficiency or is already receiving corticosteroids.

- Sellar Masses: Other Manifestations

## Slide 80

- Pituitary Adenomas
- Most common benign tumor of the pituitary gland
- Adenomas are benign neoplasms that typically arise from one of the five anterior pituitary cell types
- Most adenomas are nonfunctional but can cause pituitary hypersecretion and hyposecretion syndromes
- [picture]

## Slide 81

- Pituitary Adenomas
- If functional, tumors arising from anterior pituitary cells hypersecrete their respective hormones
  - The most common hypersecretion syndrome is that of lactotroph cells, resulting in hyperprolactinemia

## Slide 82

- Pituitary Adenomas
- Also classified by size
  - Microadenoma – up to 1 cm
    - If functional, most commonly secrete prolactin (PRL)
  - Macroadenoma – greater than 1 cm
    - If functional, most commonly secrete growth hormone (GH)

## Slide 83

- Pituitary Adenoma Work-up
- Often times pituitary adenomas are found incidentally (pituitary incidentaloma) on imaging obtained for other reasons
- MRI with contrast is the test of choice to evaluate sellar masses
- If adenoma is ≥1cm:
  - Refer optho for formal visual acuity and fields
  - Eval for hypersecretion: prolactin, IGF-1, ACTH and 24h-urine cortisol
  - Eval for hypopituitarism
- If adenoma is <1cm eval prolactin
- Refer to neurology

## Slide 86

- Prolactin Hypersecretion
- Primarily responsible for milk production and the development of mammary glands within breast tissue.
- Serum prolactin concentrations normally increase substantially during pregnancy and to a lesser degree in response to nipple stimulation during breastfeeding, physical exertion, and stress.

## Slide 87

- Hyperprolactinemia
- The most common pituitary hormone hypersecretion syndrome in both men and women
  - Microprolactinomas, are more common in women and typically do not grow even with pregnancy or oral contraceptives
  - Aggressive macroprolactinomas are more common in men and can spread into the cavernous sinuses and suprasellar areas

## Slide 88

- Hyperprolactinemia Clinical Presentation
- In women: amenorrhea, galactorrhea, and infertility are hallmark symptoms
  - Patients may also complain of decreased libido, weight gain, and mild hirsutism
- In men: gynecomastia, diminished libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, and visual loss (optic nerve compression)
  - Pathogenesis: gonadotropin suppression leads to reduced testosterone, impotence, and oligospermia

## Slide 89

- Hyperprolactinemia: Diagnosis
- Elevated fasting AM prolactin levels
  - Normal is < 20mcg/L
- Evaluate for other causes of hyperprolactinemia:
  - Pregnancy (hCG), primary hypothyroidism (TSH + T4), kidney disease (Cr, BUN), cirrhosis (LFTs); medications (antipsychotics-Haldol, Risperdal; Metoclopramide; Verapamil)
- Pituitary prolactinomas may cosecrete GH

## Slide 90

- Diagnosing a Prolactinoma
- A prolactinoma is likely with a PRL level >200 mcg/L
  - After exclusion of physiologic, medication-induced or other medical conditions that cause elevated PRL
  - Levels < 100 mcg/L may be caused by microadenomas or other causes of elevated PRL
- Pituitary MRI should be performed in all patients with hyperprolactinemia

## Slide 92

- Prolactinomas: Treatment
- Because microadenomas rarely progress to become macroadenomas, no treatment may be needed if patients are asymptomatic, and fertility is not desired
  - Monitor: regular serial PRL measurements and MRIs
- Symptomatic patients: dopamine agonists – titrated to normal PRL levels and resumption of fertility
  - Cabergoline (Dostinex) or
  - Bromocriptine (Parlodel)

## Slide 106

- Acromegaly: Complications
- Cardiomyopathy with arrhythmias, LVH, decreased diastolic function, and HTN (most patients if untreated)
- Upper airway obstruction and sleep apnea (>60%)
- Diabetes mellitus (25%)
  - Most patients are intolerant of a glucose load
- Increased risk of colon polyps and mortality from colonic malignancy

## Slide 107

- Gigantism/Acromegaly : Diagnosis
- For screening, a random serum IGF-1 can be obtained
  - If it is normal for age, acromegaly is ruled out.
- If serum IGF-1 is elevated, the gold standard for diagnosis is the GH suppression test
  - An oral glucose load 75g is given, followed by GH test 2 hours later
  - Diagnosis is made if GH is greater than 1 ng/mL
- Once confirmed, obtain an MRI with contrast to evaluate for a pituitary adenoma

## Slide 108

- Gigantism/Acromegaly: Treatment
- Pituitary transsphenoidal surgery is the treatment of choice
  - Achieves remission in about 70% of patients
  - Complications: CSF leak, infection, postop diabetes insipidus, hyponatremia
- For those who have residual disease after surgery:
  - Somatostatin analogues (octreotide and lanreotide) suppress GH and normalize IGF-1 levels

## Slide 109

- Other Functional Adenomas
- Corticotropic adenomas: ACTH secreting, result in elevated cortisol levels
  - Clinical syndrome: Cushing’s Disease
- Gonadotroph adenomas: Normal puberty and fertility
  - Most common type of non-functioning adenoma
  - Clinical syndrome is often due to compression: visual changes or hypogonadism (compression of cells then decreases FSH and LH secretion)

## Slide 110

- Cushing’s Disease

- Body habitus: “moon facies”, “buffalo hump,” protuberant abdomen, thin extremities (muscle atrophy)
- Metabolic: glucose intolerance
- Skin: Purple striae (trunk, breasts, abdomen), skin atrophy, easy bruising

## Slide 111

- Other Functioning Adenomas
- TSH-secreting adenomas – very rare but often large and locally invasive when they occur
  - Presentation: thyroid goiter and hyperthyroidism
  - Diagnosis: ↑ free T4 levels + inappropriately normal or high TSH levels + MRI evidence of a pituitary adenoma
  - Treatment: remove or debulk the tumor mass surgically, followed by thyroid ablation, antithyroid medications (methimazole, propylthiauracil) or somatostatin analogues (octreotide)

## Slide 112

- Other Sellar Masses
- Craniopharyngiomas – benign, suprasellar cystic masses that are often large, invasive and recurrent
  - Derived from Rathke’s Pouch and grow near the pituitary stalk
  - > 50% present before age 20, with signs of increased intracranial pressure, hypopituitarism (90%) or DI (10%)
  - Treatment: transcranial or transsphenoidal surgical resection followed by postoperative radiation of residual tumor
- Meningiomas – Mostly benign tumor arising from the meninges that are slow growing and always nonfunctional

## Slide 113

- Other Sellar Masses
- Pituitary metastases occur in ~3% of cancer patients
  - Most cases arise from breast cancer (50%), followed by lung and gastrointestinal cancers
  - Found almost exclusively in the posterior pituitary which results in diabetes insipidus
  - Also present with mass effect (visual deficits, headache)

## Slide 115

- Disorders of the Posterior Pituitary
- The posterior pituitary releases two hormones: vasopressin (AVP, ADH) and oxytocin
- Hormones are produced by the hypothalamus and sent to the pituitary gland via axons that make up the pituitary stalk

## Slide 116

- Vasopressin Pathway
- Elevated blood osmolality is sensed by the hypothalamus
- The hypothalamus triggers the sense of thirst and signals posterior pituitary to secrete ADH
- ADH acts to increase water reabsorption from the kidney and concentrate urine while you drink water to quench thirst
- Together, increased water intake + increased reabsorption decreases plasma osmolarity
- [picture]

## Slide 117

- Diabetes Insipidus (DI)
- DI is defined as the passage of large volumes of dilute urine and comes in two forms:
  - Central DI – due to decreased secretion of ADH
  - Nephrogenic DI – decreased ability to concentrate urine because of resistance to ADH at the level of the kidneys
- Patients with untreated central diabetes insipidus (CDI) typically present with polyuria, nocturia, and polydipsia

## Slide 118

- Polyuria
- Normal urine output is 800 -2000 mL of urine per day with fluid intake of approximately 2L
- Differential Dx:
  - Diabetes Insipidus
  - Diabetes mellitus – Osmotic diuresis secondary to glucosuria
  - Primary Polydipsia – A psychologic disorder involving excessive water intake
- Generally defined as > 3L output of urine per day

## Slide 119

- Diabetes Insipidus (DI) Etiology
- Idiopathic (30-50%) – likely an autoimmune process involving destruction of the hypothalamic cells or destruction of neurons in pituitary stalk and posterior pituitary gland
- Primary tumors – craniopharyngioma, pituitary adenomas
- Infiltrative disease – Langerhans histiocytosis
- Neurosurgery (usually transsphenoidal) or head trauma – usually transient
- Secondary cancers – lung cancer, leukemia, or lymphoma

## Slide 120

- Diabetes Insipidus (DI)
- Sodium is often in the high-normal range (to provide the ongoing stimulation of thirst to replace the urinary water losses)
  - Most patients can maintain fluid balance by continuing to ingest large volumes of water
  - However, in patients without free access to water or with a damaged hypothalamic thirst center and altered thirst sensation, DI may present with hypernatremia and dehydration

## Slide 121

- Diabetes Insipidus Workup
- Consider when urinary frequency, nocturia, enuresis, persistent thirst occurs in the absence of glucosuria
- Begin with plasma osmolality
  - Psychogenic polydipsia: plasma osmolality <280mOsm/kg
  - Central or nephrogenic DI: plasma osmolality >300mOsm/kg
- 24-hour urine collection w/ unrestricted fluid intake
  - A urine volume of < 2 L/24 h (in the absence of hypernatremia) rules out DI
  - Urine osmolarity of < 280 mosmol/L and volume >2 L/24h suggests DI and should be evaluated further to determine which type

## Slide 122

- Diabetes Insipidus Workup
- Next: Fluid restriction and “desmopressin challenge test”
- Fluid restrict until a sodium of 145 is achieved (overnight or for up to 12 hours); check sodium and urine osmolarity
- If still dilute, administer Desmopressin 5-10mcg intranasally or 1mcg SC/IV; measure urine osmolarity
  - In central DI, Desmopressin will increase urine osmolarity and resolve the patient’s excessive thirst and urination
  - In nephrogenic DI, the patient will not respond to Desmopressin

## Slide 123

- Diabetes Insipidus (DI) Workup
- Finally, differentiating central DI from primary polydipsia usually requires a pituitary MRI to evaluate for the “bright spot”
  - A hyperintense signal emitted by the posterior pituitary that is visible on T1 midsagittal images in heathy adults and children.
  - “Bright spot” is almost always present in patients with primary polydipsia but is always absent or abnormally small in patients with pituitary DI

## Slide 125

- Diabetes Insipidus (DI): Treatment
- Desmopressin (DDAVP) is the treatment of choice for central DI
  - DDAVP is a synthetic analogue of AVP
  - Administered every 12-24 hours as needed for thirst and polyuria (Available as IN, PO, IV and SubQ)
  - Patients must be on a low salt diet and have access to water
- Nephrogenic DI is treated with low solute diet and HCTZ (thiazide diuretic) +/- Amiloride (K sparing diuretic)
