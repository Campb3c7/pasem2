## Slide 4

- Calcium in the Body
- Only 1% of body calcium circulates, yet that fraction drives nerve, muscle, clotting, and cell signaling.
- [picture]
- 99% of total body calcium is in the skeleton, which serves as both structural framework and the body’s calcium reservoir.
- 1% of total calcium is in blood and soft tissue – this is tightly regulated by the parathyroid glands.
  - Half is ionized, or metabolically active.
  - Half is bound to albumin.

## Slide 5

- The Four Functions of Calcium
- 1. Skeletal mineralization – structural role; bone also acts as a source of calcium when needed
- 2. Intracellular second messenger – the resting cytoplasmic calcium is very low. A tiny influx produces a rapid, reversible rise in intracellular calcium – which triggers muscle contraction, hormone, and neurotransmitter release.

## Slide 6

- The Four Functions of Calcium
- 3. Enzyme cofactor – calcium is required to activate many enzymes. Many clotting factors are inactive without adequate ionized Ca.
- 4. Regulation of membrane excitability – extracellular calcium sets the excitability of nerves and muscles by influencing the threshold of voltage-gated sodium channels.

## Slide 7

- Calcium & the Nerve/Muscle Membrane
- Ionized calcium plays a critical role in setting the threshold for cell membrane depolarization.
- Clinical rule of thumb:
  - Low calcium  more excitable
  - High calcium  less excitable

## Slide 8

- Mechanism: Low ionized calcium lowers the activation threshold of Na channels and partially depolarizes the resting membrane. Nerve and muscle cells then fire with little or no stimulus  neuromuscular hyperexcitability
- Clinical findings:
  - Perioral paresthesias
  - Cramps and carpopedal spasm
- Hypocalcemia – more excitable

> Notes: Intracellularly, the levels of calcium are very low. Even tiny changes of calcium moving into the cell cause rapid reversible changes in the concentration of calcium.
> 
> Think about your early biology days.
> What is a second messenger? A hormone comes along in the blood stream, and it binds to the cell membrane, and by binding to the cell membrane, we get movement of calcium into the cell and we set off a chain reaction. The hormone itself never enters the cell, it’s the movement of calcium into it, that causes that change.

## Slide 15

- Response to Low Ca levels
- Any decrease in serum calcium levels is quickly sensed by the parathyroid gland, and secretion of PTH increases.
- PTH stimulates the kidney to hold on to calcium and the bones to release calcium to quickly bump up serum levels.
- In the long term, PTH also stimulates the kidneys to produce dihydroxy vitamin D, which in turn, stimulates GI absorption of calcium.

## Slide 16

- Response to High Ca levels
- When serum calcium rises, the thyroid gland secretes calcitonin into the blood.
- High calcitonin levels inhibit osteoclasts, reducing bone resorption and the release of calcium from bone into the blood.
- Higher serum calcium suppresses PTH secretion. This causes kidneys to lose calcium in the urine and hold on to phosphorus.

## Slide 17

- Actions of PTH
- Causes immediate mobilization of calcium from the bone by stimulating osteoclasts
- Causes kidneys to retain calcium and excrete phosphorus in its place in the urine
- Stimulates kidney’s production of active vitamin D. Active vitamin D increases absorption of dietary calcium from the gut

> Notes: Parathyroid hormone (PTH) acts in four ways to increase serum calcium levels.

## Slide 25

- Calcium Lab Values

  - Severe hypocalcemia < 7 mg/dL
  - Severe hypercalcemia > 12 mg/dL

## Slide 26

- Lab Confounders
- Decreased or increased albumin are one of the most common causes of an abnormal calcium level.
- Because albumin acts as calcium’s ‘shuttle,’ changes in albumin concentration affect how much total calcium appears on a blood test, even when biologically active free calcium remains normal.
- Half of serum calcium is bound to albumin. If a pt has low albumin, total Ca will look (falsely) low.

## Slide 39

- Treatment of Hypercalcemia
- Asymptomatic hypercalcemia (<12 mg/dL):
- Aggressive oral hydration
- Avoid factors that worsen hypercalcemia: prolonged bed rest, immobility, and volume depletion
- Avoid drugs that worsen hypercalcemia: thiazide diuretics, antacids (Tums), lithium

> Notes: Bed rest and immobility promote bone resorption or breakdown and increase osteoclast activity. 
> 
> Drink lots of water so as little calcium is in the renal tubule as possible to prevent kidney stones
> 
> Aggressive oral hydration – good rule of thumb is take the patient’s weight in lbs and multiply by 0.5. That will be number of oz to aim for. Quite a lot of water!
> Example. Patient weighs 160 lbs, goal is to drink 80 oz or 10 cups of water per day. Day in and day out.

## Slide 60

- “Hungry Bones” syndrome
- Before surgery:
- The patient has too much parathyroid hormone (usually from adenoma)
- High PTH constantly breaks down bone (resorption), dumping Ca into blood
- Before surgery, the body is used to fast rate of bone breakdown and hypercalcemia

## Slide 61

- “Hungry Bones” syndrome
- After surgery:
- Parathyroidectomy removes the overactive glands, and once it’s out:
  - PTH levels crash almost instantly
  - Bone breakdown stops quickly (no PTH to drive it)
  - Kidneys stop holding onto calcium (PTH normally helps to reabsorb it), so more Ca is lost in urine

## Slide 62

- “Hungry Bones” syndrome
- Repeated episodes of hypocalcemia occur despite multiple rounds of supplementation
- Why does calcium drop so rapidly?
- Bone breakdown STOPS fast
- Bone formation KEEPS GOING at the old rate (utilize calcium from blood)
- Result: Calcium pours out of blood and INTO the bone to build it up

      - Cinacalcet (Sensipar): oral, most common
      - Etelcalcetide (Parsabiv): IV after HD, used less often

## Slide 87

- Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism
- Occurs in patients with long-standing CKD after renal transplant.
- Parathyroid glands that have been excessively stimulated for several years by CKD, fail to recognize normalization of serum calcium levels following the renal transplant.
- The parathyroids continue to autonomously produce excessive parathyroid hormone resulting in hypercalcemia and hypophosphatemia.

> Notes: A patient has been on dialysis for a long time and then all of a sudden we drop in a 20-year-old kidney which acts perfectly. The parathyroid gland is so used to producing excessive PTH for such a long time that it fails to recognize the normalization of serum calcium.
