## Slide 2

- Paget’s Disease of the Bone
- Also known as osteitis deformans
- Paget’s is characterized by an accelerated rate of bone remodeling, resulting in overgrowth of bone at single site (28%) or multiple sites (72%) with impaired bone integrity.

## Slide 3

- Paget’s Disease of the Bone
- Most patients with Paget’s are diagnosed after bone lesions are discovered incidentally on XR or a high level of serum alkaline phosphatase is discovered incidentally.
- Paget’s disease usually involves multiple bones of the skeleton.
- Paget’s most commonly affects bones of the axial skeleton; the vertebrae, pelvis, femur, skull, sacrum, humerus and tibia.

## Slide 4

- Paget’s Disease Epidemiology
- The incidence of Paget’s is declining overall and the age of diagnosis is increasing
- Men>Women
- Age>40
- Prevalence is greatest in Western Europe or of European ancestry
  - Europeans immigrated to Australia, New Zealand
- In the US the disease occurs in ~1% of Caucasians ≥ age 55.

## Slide 5

- Paget’s Disease Etiology
- The etiology is unknown however, genetic and viral factors are believed to play a role.
    - Approximately 15-25% of patients with Paget’s report a positive family history of the disease.
        - Increases the prevalence 7-10x in 1st degree relatives
    - There is evidence that measles virus is a causative factor, and the widespread use of the MMR vaccine may be preventative.
- There are no known risk factors for Paget’s disease that are modifiable.

## Slide 6

- Pathophysiology of Paget’s Disease of the Bone
- Paget’s disease has three phases, and different areas of the skeleton may have disease in different phases and may progress at different rates.
- Phases of Paget’s:
- Lytic: rapid bone resorption and hypervascularization (Osteoclasts)
- Mixed lytic and blastic: bone resorption and haphazard bone creation (Osteoclasts & Osteoblasts)
- Sclerotic: Decreased vascularity and bone turnover

## Slide 7

- Pathophysiology of Paget’s: Lytic Phase
- In Paget’s disease there is an increased number and increased activity of osteoclasts.
  - Osteoclasts in Paget’s are large, 7-100x the size of normal and have many more nuclei than normal.
  - These large osteoclasts have 7x the resorptive capacity of normal osteoclasts and in Paget’s the rate of bone resorption/erosion is up to 9µg/d (normal is 1µg/d).
- The first phase of Paget’s is characterized by increased osteoclast activity.

## Slide 8

- Pathophysiology of Paget’s: Lytic Phase
- Increased osteoclast activity causes lytic lesions in bone (“blade of grass” lesion).
- Refers to the lucent leading edge in a long bone seen during the lytic phase.
- Also called the “candle flame sign”
- [picture]

## Slide 9

- Pathophysiology: Lytic/ Blastic Phase
- Following the onset of bone resorption by the abnormal osteoclasts, large numbers of normal osteoblasts are recruited to the sites of bone resorption.
- These normal osteoblasts respond to this abnormally rapid absorption of bone by producing new bone matrix rapidly and haphazardly.
- [picture]
- This bone appears woven and has a “mosaic pattern” or “cotton wool pattern”.

## Slide 10

- Pathophysiology: Lytic/ Blastic Phase
- Additionally, some of the bone marrow is replaced by fibrous tissue and there is a marked increase in the number of blood vessels within the bone.
- [picture]
- This pattern of bone lysis and formation repeats itself again and again, leading to high bone turnover.

## Slide 11

- Paget’s Pathophysiology : Sclerotic Phase
- Finally, bone resorption ceases but abnormal formation continues leading to hard, dense, vascular pagetic (mosaic) bone.
- The "burnt out" sclerotic phase of Paget's; further remodeling is minimal.
- Because of it’s disorganization,
- pagetic bone is actually weaker
- than normal adult bone.
- [picture]

## Slide 12

- Paget’s Pathophysiology
- Paget’s involves multiple bones simultaneously in 72% of cases and only a single bone in 28%.
- Although the disease may progress at a given site, the development of new sites of disease occurs uncommonly.
- [picture]
- Paget’s has a predilection for the axial skeleton and the long bones. (Skull, spine, sacrum, pelvis, femur, humerus, tibia)
- It rarely affects the hands/feet or rib cage.

## Slide 13

- Presenting Clinical Manifestations of Paget’s
- 75% of patients with Paget’s disease are asymptomatic, or mild sx and the disease is found incidentally.
- If symptomatic, the most frequent symptom is bone pain: dull, constant, aching, deep pain, often worse at night.
- Pagetic bone is very prone to fracture as well.

## Slide 14

- What does the pagetic process lead to?
- Fractures most commonly involve the femur
- A higher incidence of non-union and re-fracture occur in pagetic bone as well.

- Bony manifestations:
- Increased risk of fracture: often incomplete (fissure) stress fractures that can transition to complete fractures over time. Fractures are usually transverse and perpendicular to the cortex “chalk stick fractures”.

## Slide 18

- What does the pagetic process lead to?
- Other less common manifestations:
- Increased bleeding: increased blood loss during orthopedic procedures due to hypervascularity.
- Vascular steal: blood flow to hypervascular bone may “steal” the vascular supply of other tissues leading to ischemia.
- CV: high output heart failure (rare).

## Slide 19

- Complications of Paget’s
- The most feared complication is malignant transformation of the pagetic bone into osteosarcoma, which is rapidly fatal.
- Fortunately, malignant transformation in Paget’s disease is quite rare, occurring in 0.2-1% of patients.
- It is marked by increasing bone pain or alkaline phosphatase levels, mass development, or pathologic fracture.

## Slide 20

- Lab Abnormalities of Paget’s
- Serum calcium and phosphorus levels are normal in the untreated patient with Paget’s disease.
- Lab abnormalities include elevated serum alkaline phosphatase (found on LFTs) and elevated uric acid from bone turnover.

## Slide 23

- Radiologic Diagnosis of Paget’s
- X-rays often initial tests as incidental or due to pain. Results: lytic lesions with blade of grass or candle flame sign, mixed lytic and sclerotic lesions, incomplete or complete fractures, ivory appearance of spine, cotton wool
- X-rays also confirm “hot” spots seen on bone scan.
- Bone scan is more sensitive and assess the extent of the disease but is not specific.
- CT or MRI are used for diagnosis of malignant transformation, or to evaluate complications, such as nerve entrapment.
- [picture]
- Polyostotic Paget’s disease involving the skull, left scapula, thoracic vertebrae (T7, T9, T11), left femur, right calcaneus.

## Slide 24

- How do you Make the Diagnosis?
- Combination of clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of disease upon the demonstration of characteristic radiographic changes at one or more affected skeletal sites.

## Slide 25

- Treatment of Paget’s
- Pharmacologic treatment is indicated in all patients with Paget’s who have symptomatic disease and in asymptomatic patients with Paget’s who have a high risk of complications.
    - Young patients with Paget’s disease
    - Skull or vertebral involvement
    - Paget’s around a joint
    - Paget’s of a weight bearing bone
    - Planned surgery at an active pagetic site
    - Patients with high levels of alkaline phosphatase
- Treatment begins with the usual modalities for bone/joint disease: OTC pain relievers, heat/ice and physical therapy.

## Slide 26

- Treatment of Paget’s
- The treatment of choice for Paget’s is Bisphosphonates due to its antiresorptive properties.
- Among bisphosphonates Zoledronic Acid (Reclast) is first line.
  - 5mg single dose IV
  - 96% remission rate after a dose
  - Remission last 5-10 years
- The following oral bisphosphonate drugs all have an indication for Paget’s treatment:
  - Risedronate (Actonel) PO 30mg once daily for 2 months
  - Alendronate (Fosamax) PO 40mg once daily for 6 months

## Slide 27

- Bisphosphonates
- MOA: bisphosphonates inhibit osteoclast activity by attaching to hydroxyapatite binding sites on bony surfaces, especially surfaces which are undergoing active resorption.
- “First dose effect” – patients receiving bisphosphonates will frequently experience a paradoxical increase in pain at sites of active disease soon after commencing therapy.

## Slide 28

- Bisphosphonates
- Oral bisphosphonates are poorly absorbed, from the GI tract so they should NOT be taken concurrently with any food, drink or medications/supplements.
  - Should be taken first thing in the morning 30-60min prior
  - All need to be taken with a full 8oz of water and the patient must sit or stand upright for 30-60 minutes after taking due to the risk of esophageal ulceration.
- All Bisphosphonates are not recommended with kidney disease, GFR less than 30-35ml/min.

## Slide 29

- Adverse Effects of Bisphosphonates
  - Flu-like symptoms- fever, chills, myalgias, arthralgias with initiation of bisphosphonates
    - Self-limited after a few days and can be treated with acetaminophen or NSAIDs.
  - Hypocalcemia: supplement with calcium (1200 mg/day) and vitamin D (400 IU/day), but not at the exact same time as the bisphosphonate.
    - Can be exaggerated by patients taking _loop_ diuretics
  - Urticaria/ angioedema
  - Esophageal ulceration/stricture: these drugs can be very damaging to the esophageal mucosa if not passed fully into the stomach. Relatively contraindicated in patients with achalasia, esophageal stricture, esophagitis, hiatal hernia, dysphagia

## Slide 30

- Adverse Effects of Bisphosphonates
- Osteonecrosis of the jaw: higher risk with tooth extraction, dental surgery or trauma, CA of the mandible, chronic steroid use, CTX, poor oral hygiene.
- [picture]
- Results in exposure of the bone, death of the bone and loss of teeth.
  - MSK Pain- syndrome that can occur days, months or years after starting a bisphosphonate. Pain may not completely resolve after discontinuation

## Slide 31

- Calcitonin
- Salmon Calcitonin is a second line agent in those who can’t tolerate bisphosphonates due to side effects or contraindications
- It has antiresorptive properties but not as potent as bisphosphonates and does not produce a sustained remission
- Subcutaneous administration at bedtime every 1-3 days indefinitely
- Side effects: nausea, vomiting, or flushing

## Slide 32

- Goals of Therapy
- If planned surgery at pagetic site, bisphosphonate therapy should be instituted 3mth prior in hopes of reducing blood loss
- Success of bisphosphonate treatment is measured in the normalization of alkaline phosphatase levels within a short time and improvement of symptoms.
- Initially remission is assessed using alkaline phosphatase levels every 3-6 months, and once normalized can be checked every 6-12 months.
- If alkaline phosphatase levels are normal at baseline, a follow-up bone scan can be obtained in 6 months to assess disease activity.

## Slide 33

- Prognosis of Paget’s Disease
- Prognosis is generally favorable
  - By 6.5 years after initial therapy, the recurrence rate is 12.5% after treatment with zoledronic acid and 62% after risedronate
- Patients who develop the disease at a younger age have the poorest prognosis.
- Rapid institution of effective treatment should reduce the likelihood of complications. Osteosarcoma that arises at sites of Paget disease results in a 2-year survival rate of only 25%.
