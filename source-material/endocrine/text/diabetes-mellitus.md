## Slide 3

- Normal Pancreas Function
- “Exocrine” cells- produce enzymes that help with digestion of food that are released into ducts.
- “Endocrine” cells- Composed of small islands of cells called islets of Langerhans.
- Release their hormones into the blood stream
  - Insulin
  - Glucagon
- Function is to help control blood glucose levels

> Notes: http://pathology.jhu.edu/pancreas/basicoverview3.php?area=ba

## Slide 4

- Normal Pancreas Function
- Islets of Langerhans- Beta cells:
- Produces and releases insulin, amylin, and C-peptide
- When BG levels ↑ beta cells quickly respond by secreting stored insulin while at the same time increasing production of the hormone.
- Response to a spike in BG usually takes about 10 min

> Notes: http://pathology.jhu.edu/pancreas/basicoverview3.php?area=ba

## Slide 5

- Normal Pancreas Function
- Islets of Langerhans-Beta cells  also secrete:
- Amylin slows the rate of glucose entering the blood stream, making it a short-term regulator of BG levels.
- C-peptide a marker of insulin creation.

## Slide 6

- Normal Pancreas Function
- Islets of Langerhans-Alpha cell:
- - Secrete the hormone glucagon
  - Released in response to ↓ BG levels and to events where the body needs additional glucose
    - Stimulate the liver to break down glycogen to be released into the blood as glucose
    - Activate gluconeogenesis, the conversion of amino acids into glucose
    - Break down stored fat (triglycerides) into fatty acids for use as fuel by cells

## Slide 8

- The Burden of Diabetes Mellitus

- In 2017, 1 in 7 healthcare dollars was spent on DM for a total of $327 billion, and ½+ of which was spent on complications of the disease: MI, CVA, foot ulcers, ESRD
- The life expectancy of persons with DM is reduced by 5-10 years and CV dz is the major cause of death

## Slide 14

- T1DM can occur at any age
- Onset is common in childhood and adolescence, but also occurs in adults
- Incidence varies significantly by geographic region and race/ethnicity
- Rates are generally highest in populations of Northern European ancestry, but T1DM occurs in all racial and ethnic groups
- Genetic susceptibility plays an important role
  - Certain HLA genotypes are associated with increased risk
- Environmental factors may contribute to triggering autoimmunity
- Type I Diabetes Epidemiology

## Slide 16

- Prediabetes
- Prediabetes may be diagnosed by:
  - A1C: 5.7–6.4%
  - Fasting plasma glucose: 100–125 mg/dL → impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
  - 2-hour OGTT: 140–199 mg/dL → impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)
  - Clinical significance
  - Increased risk of developing T2DM
  - Associated with increased cardiovascular risk
  - Lifestyle intervention is the foundation of prevention
  - Metformin may be considered in selected high-risk patients

## Slide 18

- Insulin resistance
- ↓ tissue response to endogenous insulin
- Influenced by genetics, aging, physical inactivity, excess visceral adiposity, and dietary patterns
- Diets high in refined carbohydrates, added sugars, and highly processed foods can contribute to metabolic dysfunction and T2DM risk
- Liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue are major sites of insulin resistance
- [picture]
- Type 2 Diabetes Pathophysiology

> Notes: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-B-RVybvffU&t=73s

## Slide 19

- Progressive β-cell dysfunction
- Initially, β-cells compensate by increasing insulin secretion
- Compensatory hyperinsulinemia may maintain near-normal glucose for years
- Over time, β-cell function progressively declines
- Insulin secretion can no longer meet metabolic demands → persistent hyperglycemia and T2DM
- Type 2 Diabetes Pathophysiology

## Slide 20

- Type 2 Diabetes Pathophysiology
- Hyperinsulinemia, hyperglycemia & elevated free fatty acids contribute to:
- Endothelial dysfunction
  - Impaired vasodilation
  - Vascular dysfunction contributes to hypertension and atherosclerotic disease
- Chronic low-grade inflammation
  - Can further worsen insulin resistance
  - Promotes endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis
- Dyslipidemia
  - ↑ triglycerides
  - ↓ HDL
  - Often associated with small, dense LDL particles

> Notes: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-B-RVybvffU&t=73s

## Slide 21

- T2DM has historically been associated with adulthood, but is increasingly diagnosed in children and adolescents
- Prevalence increases significantly with age
- T2DM affects all racial and ethnic groups, but prevalence is not equal across populations
- In the U.S., prevalence is higher among several historically underserved racial/ethnic populations, including American Indian/Alaska Native, Black, and Hispanic populations
- Differences reflect a complex interaction of genetic susceptibility, social determinants of health, environmental exposures, access to care, and metabolic risk factors
- Type 2 Diabetes Epidemiology

## Slide 23

- Distinguishing T1DM from T2DM is not always straightforward
- Both T1DM and T2DM can occur at any age
- All patients with T1DM require insulin, but many patients with T2DM may also require insulin
- DKA strongly suggests insulin deficiency/T1DM, but can also occur in T2DM
- Overweight/obesity does not exclude T1DM
- No single clinical feature definitively distinguishes T1DM from T2DM
- Type 2 Diabetes vs. Type I Diabetes

## Slide 24

- When Should You Question T2DM?
- Consider testing for islet autoantibodies when T1DM is clinically possible, especially with:
- Younger age at diagnosis (particularly <35 years)
- BMI <25 kg/m²
- Unintentional weight loss
- DKA/ketosis at presentation
- Marked hyperglycemia at presentation
- Poor response to noninsulin therapy or rapid progression to insulin requirement
- Personal/family history of autoimmune disease may increase suspicion
- Type 2 Diabetes vs. Type I Diabetes

## Slide 26

- Latent Autoimmune Diabetes in Adults (LADA)
- Slowly progressive autoimmune β-cell destruction occurring in adults
- Islet autoantibodies are present, most commonly GAD antibodies
- Patients may initially appear to have T2DM and may not require insulin at diagnosis
- Progressive β-cell loss typically leads to insulin requirement
- Sometimes called “type 1.5 diabetes”
- ADA classifies LADA within the spectrum of T1DM, rather than as a separate diabetes type

## Slide 27

- Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY)
- Monogenic diabetes caused by a pathogenic genetic variant affecting β-cell function
- Often diagnosed in adolescence or early adulthood
- Usually demonstrates autosomal dominant inheritance
- Often lacks typical features of T1DM or T2DM
- Correct diagnosis matters because treatment differs by MODY subtype

## Slide 28

- Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY)
- Consider MODY when:
- Diabetes occurs at a young age
- Strong family history across successive generations
- Negative islet autoantibodies
- No obesity or other typical features of insulin resistance
- Stable, mild fasting hyperglycemia may be a clue
- → Confirm with genetic testing

## Slide 34

- Diagnosis of Diabetes
- Any of the following:
- A1C ≥6.5% *
- Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL
- 2-hour plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL during 75-g OGTT
- Random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis
- In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, confirm the diagnosis with repeat testing.

## Slide 36

- C-Peptide
- C-peptide is released when proinsulin is cleaved into insulin + C-peptide
- Therefore, C-peptide is a marker of endogenous insulin production
- Low/undetectable C-peptide suggests significant loss of β-cell function
- Normal or elevated C-peptide indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion
- C-peptide can help distinguish insulin deficiency from insulin resistance, particularly when the diagnosis is unclear
- Diagnostic testing- T1DM

## Slide 38

- Treatment will involve a multispecialty team and pharmacologic/ non-pharm treatment.
- Registered dietician will give recommendations on total number of calories and division of calories between meals/snacks as well as division of calories by macro type (fat, proteins, carbs).
- Pts should be encouraged to exercise but may develop hypoglycemia if exercising > 30 min without an adjustment in insulin or a pre-exercise snack.
- Treatment of Type 1 DM

## Slide 39

- T1DM will require lifelong treatment with insulin.
- The goal of glucose control is to provide insulin therapy in a manner that is as physiologic as possible for a goal HgbA1c of 7% as a pediatric patient.
  - Guidelines from American Diabetic Association (ADA)
  - More or less stringent goals need to be tailored to the individual patient
- Treatment of Type 1 DM

## Slide 40

- Pediatric Considerations in T1DM
- Insulin is required for treatment of T1DM
- CGM is recommended for most children and adolescents with T1DM
- Insulin pumps / automated insulin delivery (AID) systems may improve glycemic management
- Hypoglycemia prevention and recognition are especially important in young children
- Insulin and carbohydrate needs vary with growth, activity, illness, and puberty
- Diabetes management should gradually transition toward the child while maintaining age-appropriate caregiver supervision
- Consider psychosocial impact, including diabetes distress, depression, and eating disorders in adolescents
- Treatment of Type 1 DM – Across the lifespan

## Slide 41

- Adults
- In adults, a target A1C of <7 % best balances the risks of long-term sequelae and hypoglycemia
  - Avoiding long term complications of hyperglycemia-
    - Delayed onset of microvascular disease
    - Slowed progression of already-present microvascular disease
    - Decreased the incidence of cardiovascular disease
  - Hypoglycemia
    - Avoiding complications from hypoglycemic events
- Treatment of Type 1 DM – Across the lifespan

## Slide 44

- Type 2 DM Risk Factors
- Overweight/obesity, particularly visceral adiposity
- Physical inactivity
- Family history/genetic susceptibility
- Increasing age
- Higher-risk racial/ethnic ancestry
- History of prediabetes or gestational diabetes
- PCOS
- Cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and dyslipidemia
- Other conditions associated with insulin resistance (e.g., acanthosis nigricans, MASLD)
- Certain medications, including glucocorticoids and second-generation antipsychotics

## Slide 45

- Screening For Prediabetes & T2DM
- Begin screening at age 35 for all adults
- Screen earlier at any age in adults with overweight/obesity plus ≥1 risk factor:
  - First-degree relative with diabetes
  - Higher-risk racial/ethnic ancestry
  - History of CVD
  - HTN
  - HDL <35 and/or triglycerides >250
  - PCOS
  - Physical inactivity
  - Other evidence of insulin resistance
  - If normal: repeat at least every 3 yearsPrediabetes: test yearlyHistory of GDM: test every 1–3 years

## Slide 52

- Medical nutrition therapy
  - MNT involves a more detailed discussion of calories, grams, and other metrics as well as intensive implementation of dietary recommendations aimed at optimizing glycemic control and reducing the risk for complications.
  - Recommendations should be personalized
- Diabetes Education

## Slide 53

- Carb Counting
  - Requires time and effort on the part of the patient and the educator.
  - Usually done with a specialized diabetes educator or nutritionist, but many patients come with internet/app based resources.
  - Carbohydrate intake should be individualized; there is no single ideal amount of carbohydrate for all patients with diabetes.
- Diabetes Education

## Slide 54

- Weight reduction
  - Weight management is an important component of T2DM treatment for patients with overweight or obesity
  - Even modest weight loss can improve glycemia and cardiometabolic risk
  - Greater sustained weight loss may provide greater metabolic benefit
  - Treatment should be individualized and may include lifestyle intervention, pharmacotherapy, and/or metabolic surgery
- Diabetes Education

## Slide 55

- Exercise
  - ≥150 min/week moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity
  - Spread over ≥3 days/week
  - No more than 2 consecutive inactive days
  - Resistance exercise 2–3 times/week
  - Reduce sedentary time; interrupt prolonged sitting
- Diabetes Education

## Slide 56

- Sleep
  - Adequate rest is important for maintaining energy levels and well-being, and all patients should be advised to sleep on average approximately 7+ hours per night.
  - Evidence supports an association of 6 to 9 hours of sleep per night with a reduction in cardiometabolic risk factors.
  - Sleep deprivation aggravates insulin resistance, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia and increases inflammatory cytokines.
- Diabetes Education

## Slide 58

- Health Maintenance in Diabetes - Monitoring
- Follow-up: generally every 3–6 months, individualized
- A1C: typically every 3 months if therapy changed/goals not met; at least twice yearly if stable
- Blood pressure: every routine visit
- Lipids: assess and monitor based on age, treatment, and cardiovascular risk
- Kidney: UACR + eGFR at least annually when indicated
- Feet: visual inspection regularly; comprehensive foot exam at least annually
  - Inspect every visit if sensory loss, prior ulcer, or amputation
- Eye exam: dilated retinal examination according to diabetes type, duration, and prior findings

> Notes: https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/managing/problems.html

## Slide 59

- Health Maintenance in Diabetes – Preventative Care
- Tobacco: assess use and counsel cessation
- Mental/behavioral health: assess for depression, anxiety, diabetes distress, disordered eating, etc.
- Dental care: regular dental/periodontal care
- Vaccinations: follow age-appropriate recommendations; diabetes may affect indications for hepatitis B and pneumococcal vaccination
- Reproductive planning: contraception and preconception counseling when applicable
- Cancer screening: follow routine age- and sex-appropriate recommendations
- Assess hearing, sleep, sexual health, and other comorbidities when indicated

> Notes: https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/managing/problems.html
