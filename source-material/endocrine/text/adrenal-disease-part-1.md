## Slide 2

- The HPA Axis
- The adrenal gland is the target organ of the HPA axis.
  - The hypothalamus produces CRH, corticotropin releasing hormone.
  - CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to produce ACTH, or adrenocorticotropic hormone.
  - ACTH acts at the adrenal gland to stimulate cortisol secretion and to a much lesser extent, to stimulate aldosterone secretion.

## Slide 3

- Adrenal Gland
- The adrenal cortex: consists of three layers which secrete steroid hormones.  From the outside in, the layers of the adrenal gland synthesize:
  - Mineralocorticoids
  - Glucocorticoids
  - Sex hormones- adrenal androgens
- Adrenal medulla: a separate organ in function, the medulla produces epinephrine and norepinephrine.

## Slide 5

- Glucocorticoid Function
- Glucocorticoids are our stress hormones:
  - Decrease the uptake of glucose by non-essential tissues.
  - Increase breakdown of fat and protein for energy.
  - Increase gluconeogenesis (glucose synthesis).
  - Inhibit protein synthesis (tissue building, healing).
  - Suppress the inflammatory reaction.
- Small amounts of glucocorticoid help us respond to stressful events.  Too little or too much glucocorticoid is problematic.

## Slide 6

- Mineralocorticoid &Adrenal Androgens
- Mineralocorticoids help to keep our intravascular fluid balance optimal and are a major part of blood pressure control.
- The major mineralocorticoid produced by the adrenal gland is aldosterone.
- The innermost layer of the adrenal gland produces androgens- which are important in female sexual health but are negligible in a male.

## Slide 7

- Cortisol’s Effect
- Cortisol: the hormone that makes you get up and go!  The human body produces the equivalent of 0.5mg of Decadron per day (therapeutic dose 4mg)
- Cortisol has multiple effects on the body:
- Cardiovascular:
  - Cortisol increases cardiac output and vascular tone
  - Cortisol increases sodium retention by the kidney
- Calcium homeostasis/ connective tissue:
  - Cortisol stimulates bone and connective tissue loss

## Slide 8

- Cortisol’s Effect
- Metabolic:
  - Cortisol increases catabolism (breakdown of tissues).
  - Cortisol increases insulin levels and gluconeogenesis.
  - Inhibits the growth and reproductive functions.
- Immunity:
  - Suppresses inflammation on multiple levels.
  - Cortisol increases intravascular leukocytes.

## Slide 10

- Glucocorticoid Disorders
- Persons with disordered glucocorticoid secretion may either have too much or too little cortisol.
- If a person has too little cortisol, then they have adrenal insufficiency.
- If a person has too much cortisol secretion, then they an endogenous glucocorticoid excess.
- Because steroids are prescribed for a variety of reasons, sometimes patients can have iatrogenic exogenous causes of adrenal insufficiency or excess.

## Slide 11

- Iatrogenic Adrenal Insufficiency
- Adrenal insufficiency is very often acquired, due to the sudden withdrawal of prescribed glucocorticoids.
- Whenever clinicians prescribe long-term steroids, we must be clear to patients that these drugs are not to be discontinued abruptly.
- Oral corticosteroids are far more likely than inhaled corticosteroids to cause adrenal insufficiency if they are withdrawn.

## Slide 12

- Iatrogenic Adrenal Insufficiency
- Oral steroids are particularly likely to cause iatrogenic adrenal insufficiency if they are high dose, if they are taken daily, or for a long time.
- Strategies to avoid this include lower dose or every-other-day steroid therapy for a chronic disease that is under good control.
- As little as two weeks of oral steroid treatment may precipitate adrenal insufficiency if they are abruptly withdrawn. What are the names of these steroids?

  - PGA Type II: Addison’s, autoimmune thyroid disease (Graves/ Hashimoto’s) and T1DM. Generally, presents in the teens and twenties.

## Slide 17

- Addison’s Disease: Autoimmune Adrenal Destruction
- Addison's disease results in destruction of the entire adrenal cortex, affecting cortisol (glucocorticoid), aldosterone (mineralcorticoid) and adrenal androgen synthesis.
- Most commonly occurs in women ages 30-50 years, unless it is part of a polyglandular autoimmune syndrome.  No racial differences.
- Rare: In the US, 40 cases per million persons.

## Slide 34

- Making the Non-Emergent Diagnosis of Cortisol Deficiency
- First, a random serum cortisol level is drawn.  Levels > 25 mcg/dL exclude adrenal insufficiency but should be interpreted with caution in the critically ill patient.  Illness may cause a cortisol surge, masking a borderline or early deficiency.
- If the random cortisol level is not reassuring, testing to assess the synthetic function of the adrenal glands is performed with a cosynotropin stimulation test.

## Slide 35

- How is this done?
- A baseline serum aldosterone level and cortisol level are drawn.
- Cosynotropin (0.25mg of synthetic ACTH) is injected IM or IV and then cortisol and aldosterone levels are remeasured at either 30 or 60 minutes.
- A cosynotropin test is positive if the stimulation fails to raise the level of cortisol by a specific amount in either 30 or 60 minutes.

> Notes: Interpreting the rapid adrenocorticotrophic hormone test:
> Two criteria are necessary for diagnosis: (1) an increase in the baseline cortisol value of 7 mcg/dL or more and (2) the value must rise to 20 mcg/dL or more in 30 or 60 minutes, establishing normal adrenal glucocorticoid function.
> A low aldosterone value of less than 5 ng/100 mL that fails to double or increase by at least 4 ng/100 mL 30 minutes after ACTH administration denotes abnormal mineralocorticoid function of the adrenal cortex.
> The 30-minute aldosterone value is more sensitive than the 60-minute value because aldosterone levels actually have been shown to decrease in the 60-minute sample.
> The absolute 30- or 60-minute cortisol value carries more significance than the incremental value, especially in patients who may be in great stress and at their maximal adrenal output. These patients may not show a significant increase in cortisol output with ACTH stimulation.
> A normal 30- or 60-minute rapid ACTH test excludes Addison disease but may not adequately exclude mild impairment of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis in secondary adrenal insufficiency.

## Slide 38

- Adrenal Insufficiency Treatment
- Hydrocortisone (Cortef): 15-25 mg daily in two doses: 2/3 in AM, 1/3 in PM to mimic circadian rhythm (most physiologic).
  - The Cortef dose is titrated to the lowest amount that stops symptoms of joint pain.
  - Thin patients may need less, and obese patients may need more.
  - Medications that induce cytochrome P450 will reduce serum Cortef concentrations; whereas liver disease will result in higher serum Cortef levels due to decreased clearance.

## Slide 39

- Cytochrome P450- Inducers
- Recall that when drug A INDUCES the metabolism of drug B (Cortef), a higher dose of drug B must be given because the liver will metabolize and excrete drug B more rapidly and its’ clinical effect will be less.
- AEDs: carbamazepine, fosphenytoin, phenytoin, primidone
- Antibiotics active against mycobacteria (TB, MAC): rifampin
- St. John’s wort
- Estrogen/progesterone
- Steroids: Dexamethasone, megace, prednisone

## Slide 40

- Cytochrome P450- Inhibitors
- When drug A INHIBITS the metabolism of drug B (Cortef), a lower dose of drug B must be given because the liver will metabolize and excrete drug B more slowly, magnifying its’ clinical effect and predisposing to the build-up of a high drug level.
- Anti-retrovirals: telapavir, bocepavir, indinavir
- Azole antifungals: ketoconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole
- Macrolide antibiotics: clarithromycin, erythromycin,
- Fluoroquinalone antibiotics: ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin
- CCBs: verapamil, diltiazem

## Slide 41

- Adrenal Insufficiency Treatment
- Fludrocortisone (Florinef): a potent mineralocorticoid, typically given in doses of 0.05- 0.1 mg daily.
      - No need for mineralocorticoid stress dosing.
      - Dose is titrated to maintain normal BP, Na and K+ levels- no orthostasis!
- Consider: DHEA: 50mg daily for women only
      - Converted to testosterone by peripheral tissue
      - Improvement in libido, mood and well-being

## Slide 42

- What if the patient becomes ill?
  - Minor illness (nausea, vomiting, fever > 100.5) will require a doubling of the glucocorticoid dose and close outpatient follow-up.
  - Major stressful events (trauma, surgery requiring general anesthesia) will require parenteral hydrocortisone 50 mg every eight hours, weaned quickly following a planned surgery, more slowly for a clinical illness.
  - Look at clinical condition and for hypotension.

## Slide 44

- Prognosis of Adrenal Insufficiency
- Patients with adrenal insufficiency must be especially cautious of stressors which may precipitate adrenal crisis.
- Most patients do okay but overall will have a decreased life expectancy.  Risk of death from any illness is more than 2x average.
- All patients with adrenal insufficiency are on medication for the remainder of their lives.

## Slide 45

- Adrenal Crisis: Why?
- A medical emergency caused by cortisol deficiency.
- Stress in a patient with mild adrenal insufficiency who hasn’t been diagnosed or failure to increase steroid in a diagnosed patient.
  - Surgery, infection, prolonged fasting, trauma
- Sudden withdrawal of corticosteroid in a patient with chronic insufficiency.
  - Prednisone, megestrol (possibly inhaled steroid too!)

## Slide 46

- Adrenal Crisis: Why?
- Temporary insufficiency due to exogenous suppression by taking oral steroids.
- Adrenal or pituitary surgery: bilateral adrenalectomy, removal of a suppressing tumor.
- Sudden destruction of the pituitary gland, Sheehan’s syndrome.
- Bilateral adrenal injury: Trauma, hemorrhage, anticoagulant therapy, thrombosis, infection or metastasis.

## Slide 47

- S/S of Adrenal Crisis
- The patient is acutely ill
    - Hypotension, especially if refractory to IV fluid resuscitation
    - Metabolic acidosis
    - Confusion and coma
    - Nausea/ vomiting and abdominal pain
    - Fever and hyperpyrexia (up to 105 degrees)
    - Cyanosis
    - S/S and laboratory findings of chronic adrenal insufficiency, if it is present.

## Slide 48

- Making the Diagnosis of Crisis
- Acute adrenal insufficiency is a medical emergency!
- Do not delay medical treatment of suspected adrenal insufficiency pending laboratory results.
- Instead, if adrenal insufficiency is suspected in the acutely ill patient, draw blood for:
    - Random cortisol level, ACTH, aldosterone and renin levels, in addition to standard labs and treat empirically with stress dose steroid.

## Slide 49

- Differential Diagnosis of Crisis
- Other causes of shock
  - Septic, hypovolemic or cardiogenic shock
- Other causes of hyperkalemia
  - GIB, rhabdomyolysis, meds (spironolactone, ACEI)
- Other causes of hyponatremia
  - Hypothyroidism, diuretics, heart failure, vomiting
- Acute abdomen
  - We will generally see a significantly increased neutrophil count with acute abdomen but not necessarily with adrenal crisis

## Slide 50

- Treatment of the Acutely Ill Patient in Adrenal Crisis
- Steroid replacement- stress dosing:
- Hydrocortisone 100mg IV bolus, then by continuous infusion (10-12 mg/hr)- this is most physiologic
- Alternatively, hydrocortisone 50-100mg IV push every 6-8 hours can be used.
- At this high dose of glucocorticoid (>100 mg/day), Cortef acts as a mineralocorticoid as well, so no Florinef is needed.

## Slide 51

- Treatment of the Acutely Ill Patient
- Stress dose steroid replacement
- Clinical improvement (BP) should be evident in 4-6 hours if adrenal crisis is the culprit.
- Over 2-3 days the amount of steroid is reduced to 100 mg/day to avoid GI ulceration and bleeding, regardless of patient condition.
- It is tapered further over the next 4-5 days to the oral dose and mineralocorticoid is begun when glucocorticoid dose is < 100mg/day.

## Slide 52

- Treatment of the Acutely Ill Patient in Adrenal Crisis
- Aggressive IV fluid replacement: to assist with correction of hypotension.
- Electrolyte correction: most commonly patients have hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis.
- Consideration for empiric antibiotics and endocrinology referral, address thyroid status.

## Slide 53

- Prognosis of Adrenal Crisis
- Rapid treatment of adrenal crisis results in an excellent outcome considering how ill the patient is from the stressor.
- However, adrenal insufficiency is frequently unrecognized and untreated.
- Mortality in these cases is dependent upon the stress of the event and the degree of adrenal insufficiency.

## Slide 74

- Cortisol Excess: Other Tests
- Salivary cortisol level: patient awakens at 11pm and collects a saliva specimen.  Cortisol levels are lowest at this time, so if high, is suggestive.

## Slide 75

- Cortisol Excess : Other Tests
- Random urinary free cortisol levels: check a random urine specimen for the amount of cortisol compared to the amount of creatinine excreted.  A cortisol level 3x the creatinine level is highly suggestive of Cushing’s.
- This test works best in patients less than 50 years of age because their excretion of creatinine is predictable- after age 50 renal function declines at an irregular rate.

## Slide 76

- Next, Localizing the Source
- Measure a serum ACTH level
  - If the ACTH is elevated, then there is either a pituitary source or ectopic secretion.
    - Recall that a pituitary source means a pituitary adenoma.
    - An ectopic source most often means an undiagnosed cancer- either small cell lung cancer or a carcinoid tumor in the lung or GI tract.
  - If the ACTH levels are undetectable then there is autonomous adrenal secretion.
    - Most likely from an adrenal adenoma or carcinoma or rarely, adrenal hyperplasia.

## Slide 77

- Third, Differentiating Between ACTH dependent Cortisol Excess- Ectopic vs Pituitary Source
- CRH stimulation testing is performed to evaluate the source of elevated ACTH levels.
  - For this test, ACTH and cortisol levels are obtained at baseline, and subsequently at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after administration of 1 mcg/kg of CRH.
- Increasing ACTH and cortisol = a pituitary source
- Constant ACTH and cortisol = an ectopic source

- Treatment of Cortisol Excess: Ectopic Sources
- Medical therapy for SCLC or another aggressive primary CA better treated by chemotherapy would be used.  These tumors are not treated surgically.
- If the source cannot be found . . . medical suppression of steroidogenesis with ketoconazole is used most commonly.  Many other drugs can be used, usually doses that are effective have some toxicities.  Imaging studies to continue to search for the source are repeated every 6 months.

## Slide 91

- Prognosis of Cortisol Excess
- Untreated Cushing’s disease is LETHAL, and M&M is usually related to the steroid excess:
- CAD complications of HTN/ dyslipidemia/ DM.
- Multisystem complications of diabetes.
- High risk of opportunistic infections- bacterial and fungal, with poor wound healing.
- High risk of perforated abdominal viscera.
- Osteoporosis and increased fracture risk.

## Slide 92

- Pseudo-Cushing’s Syndrome
- Pseudo-Cushing’s syndrome is a well-recognized condition that occurs when people who ingest large quantities of alcohol develop signs, symptoms and the biochemical abnormalities of Cushing’s.
- May also occur in obesity and depression.  These patients have dysregulation of cortisol secretion.
- Complete abstinence from alcohol will completely reverse this process.
