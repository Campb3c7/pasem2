## Slide 2

- Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone
- Renin (an enzyme) is secreted by the kidney when there is not enough renal blood flow or when hyperkalemia is sensed.
- Renin converts angiotensinogen (in the liver) to angiotensin I.
- Angiotensin I is converted to angiotensin II by ACE within the lungs and other tissues.

## Slide 3

- Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone
- Angiotensin II is a vasopressor itself and causes vasoconstriction. Angiotensin II also stimulates aldosterone secretion from the adrenal gland.
- Aldosterone causes sodium to be reabsorbed and K+ and H+ secretion into the urine via pumps in the distal renal tubule.  Water follows sodium.
- Increased volume in the blood vessels raises BP, dilutes K+ and suppresses renin release.

## Slide 5

- Hypoaldosteronism
- Deficiency of aldosterone is usually secondary to deficient renin production by a diseased kidney and not a problem with production of aldosterone from the adrenal gland.
- Therefore, this disorder is most commonly NOT due to an adrenal problem but instead due to chronic kidney disease, most often caused by diabetes and hypertension - this is hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism.

## Slide 6

- Hypoaldosteronism
- We try to prevent this with strict BP and glucose control
- Once present, hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism is permanent.
- [picture]
- Hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism is most commonly caused by diabetic nephropathy or chronic tubulointerstitial kidney disease.

## Slide 7

- Hypoaldosteronism Pathophysiology
- Patients present with hyperkalemia, hyperchloremia and a non-anion gap metabolic acidosis, why?
- Also known as type 4 renal tubular acidosis, a non-anion gap metabolic acidosis caused by normally functioning kidneys that fail to acidify the urine.
    - Plasma sodium concentration is normal
    - Plasma volume is usually decreased

> Notes: Renal tubular acidosis is when otherwise normally functioning kidneys fail to acidify the urine resulting in excess acid in the body- a metabolic acidosis.  Types 1 and 2 are caused by tubular problems, type 3 is excluded from modern classification systems and type 4 is due to aldosterone deficiency.

## Slide 8

- Hypoaldosteronism
- What symptoms will this cause?
- In most patients, not many symptoms since  significant sodium wasting will be prevented by other hormones that preserve sodium balance (angiotensin II and norepinephrine).  Is this good for the patient?

## Slide 9

- Hypoaldosteronism, Making the Dx
- Hypoaldosteronism should be suspected in the right patient when there is persistent hyperkalemia without another cause.
- If hyperkalemia is noted, repeat K+ level stat and if still high (> 6), check an EKG, this could be a medical emergency!
- Hypoaldosteronism not attributable to hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism or primary adrenal disease is best worked up by nephrology.

## Slide 10

- Hypoaldosteronism Treatment
- If primary adrenal deficiency is present, treatment is with fludrocortisone to prevent orthostasis and normalize potassium levels.
- Fludrocortisone will exacerbate HTN and edema. Therefore, despite being effective, Florinef is rarely used in hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism.
- Instead, a low K+ diet, with or without a loop or a thiazide diuretic are the mainstays of treatment in hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism.

## Slide 11

- Primary Hyperaldosteronism
- Also known as Conn’s syndrome, and most frequently caused by:
- Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia (60%)
- Aldosterone producing adenoma (40%)
- Adrenal CA is extremely rare
- Aldosterone excess results in:
  - Sodium retention causing hypertension, but not hypernatremia (equal Na+ and water retention)
  - Low-normal potassium or hypokalemia
  - Metabolic alkalosis

## Slide 12

- Primary Hyperaldosteronism
- Hyperaldosteronism accounts for up to 5% of hypertension and is potentially curable.
- Persons with PA usually present with significant HTN at 30- 50 years and F:M ratio is 2:1.
- Primary hyperaldosteronism is suspected in any patient with persistent hypokalemia (not always seen) and resistant hypertension, when both are present, 50% of persons will have PA.

## Slide 13

- Primary Hyperaldosteronism
- Patients with primary aldosteronism have an increased risk of morbidity and mortality beyond just their elevated BP.
- Their risk for certain cardiovascular outcomes is significantly higher than those with primary HTN.
    - Myocardial infarction (4% versus 0.6%)
    - Atrial fibrillation (7.3% versus 0.6%)
    - Stroke (12.9% versus 3.4%)

## Slide 15

- Hyperaldosteronism S/S
- Most of the symptoms are related to low K+
- Fatigue, weakness, nocturia, HA
- If severe, patients may present with polydipsia, polyuria, and paresthesias.
- [picture]
- If severe, patients may have intermittent paralysis, tetany, positive Trousseau’s or Chvostek’s sign
- --Trousseau’s sign- carpal or pedal spasm in response to inflation of a BP cuff on the arm or leg.  Chvostek’s sign- tapping the facial nerve (CN VII) elicits twitching of the face.  These signs are more commonly seen in hypocalcemia but may be present in hypokalemia.

## Slide 16

- Signs and Symptoms of Hyperaldosteronism
- In primary hyperaldosteronism, blood pressure may be severely elevated, causing:
  - End organ damage: retinal, renal, cardiac
  - Hypertensive encephalopathy
  - Facial flushing/ headache
- The major differential diagnosis of primary hyperaldosteronism includes other causes of secondary HTN, such as Cushing’s and renovascular hypertension/ renal artery stenosis.

## Slide 17

- Who Should Be Tested for PA?
- Patients with HTN and spontaneous hypokalemia or low-dose, diuretic-induced hypokalemia
- The following patients should undergo testing even if they are normokalemic :
  - Drug-resistant severe hypertension (not controlled on 3+ medications at maximal doses)
  - Persons with hypertension in whom an adrenal incidentaloma is found (an adrenal mass discovered incidentally on imaging)

## Slide 18

- Who Should Be Tested for PA?
- The following patients should undergo testing even if they are normokalemic :
  - Hypertension with sleep apnea.
  - Hypertension and family history of early-onset HTN or CVA at a young age (<40 years).
  - All hypertensive 1st degree relatives of persons with primary aldosteronism.
  - Younger patients with hypertension and atrial fibrillation.

## Slide 19

- Making the Hyperaldosteronism Diagnosis
- Testing is by obtaining a plasma aldosterone concentration and a plasma renin activity level, then calculating the aldosterone to renin activity ratio (ARR).
- The test is ideally done in mid-morning after the patient has been up and ambulatory for 2 hours, then sits for 15 minutes.

## Slide 20

- Making the Hyperaldosteronism Diagnosis
- Hypokalemia must be corrected before the test as this may cause a falsely low aldosterone.
- All MRAs, amiloride and triamterene are discontinued 4 weeks prior to the test.
- The diagnosis depends on finding both an inappropriately high aldosterone concentration, and an inappropriately low renin activity level.

> Notes: MRA- mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists or aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone (Aldactone), eplerenone (Inspra) and finerenone (Kerendia)).

## Slide 21

- Making the Hyperaldosteronism Diagnosis
- All positive tests require confirmatory testing.

## Slide 22

- Making the Hyperaldosteronism Diagnosis
- If aldosterone is very high compared to renin, and this finding is confirmed, then imaging is ordered to distinguish between an adrenal adenoma and  adrenal hyperplasia.
  - CT of abdomen/pelvis with iodated contrast, or
  - MRI of the adrenals with gadolinium contrast
    - Bilateral adrenal vein sampling of aldosterone levels to clarify if a patient has bilateral adrenal adenomas.

## Slide 23

- Hard Stop, Let’s Review Hyperaldosteronism
- Hyperaldosteronism is most often caused by bilateral adrenal hyperplasia or an autonomous adenoma in one of the adrenal glands.
- Young persons (2x women) with resistant HTN and hypokalemia should be suspected.
- It is important to find, because the hypertention is potentially curable.

## Slide 24

- Hard Stop, Let’s Review Hyperaldosteronism
- Hyperaldosterone is also important to find because if untreated it increases the risk of stroke, atrial fibrillation and myocardial infarctions.
- The diagnosis is made by assessing the plasma aldosterone concentration, the plasma renin activity level and the ratio between the two.
- The adrenal glands are then imaged to differentiate between hyperplasia and adenoma.

## Slide 25

- Treatment of An Adrenal Adenoma causing Hyperaldosteronism
- An adrenal adenoma is treated surgically with a unilateral adrenal resection, often performed laparoscopically.
- This will remove the source of high aldosterone secretion and allow the other adrenal gland to respond normally to renin stimulation.
- 60-70% of patients become normotensive within a year of the surgery.

## Slide 26

- Treatment of Hyperaldosteronism caused by Bilateral Adrenal Hyperplasia
- Unilateral adrenalectomy has very minimal effects on the aldosterone level and BP in a patient with bilateral adrenal hyperplasia.
- Therefore, adrenal hyperplasia is treated medically with an aldosterone antagonist to control the effects of aldosterone on the body:
    - Aldactone (Spironolactone)
    - Eplerenone (Inspra)

## Slide 30

- Normal Physiology of Androgens
- The ovaries and the adrenal glands are the two major sources of androgens in women, with the adrenal glands being the predominant source.
- Fat tissue and skin also convert weak androgens into more potent ones.  Increasing adiposity (fat mass) means greater androgen conversion.
- In men, the testes and the adrenal glands are the two major sources, with the testes being the predominant source.

## Slide 31

- Androgen Excess Etiologies
- Androgen excess is the most common endocrine disorder that occurs in women of reproductive age.
- The most common etiology of androgen excess is polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) which accounts for 80-90% of cases.
- Other rare causes include androgen secreting tumors, Cushing’s syndrome and prolactinomas.

## Slide 32

- Androgen Excess
- Androgen excess affects about 8% of women of reproductive age and occurs equally in all ethnicities.  It is rare in post-menopausal women.
- Androgen excess causes insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, elevated BP and vascular disease, leading to high rates of diabetes and CAD.
- Other manifestations of androgen excess are irregular menstrual cycles, male pattern baldness, hirsutism and acne.

## Slide 33

- Androgen Excess Diagnosis
- Initial diagnostic testing focuses on measuring levels of androgens (total testosterone, DHEAs), 17-hydroxyprogesterone, TSH, and prolactin.
- In women, a transabdominal or transvaginal ultrasound of the uterus and adnexa is ordered in all to evaluate for ovarian cysts or masses.  In men, an ultrasound of the testicles.
- If an adrenal cause is suspected after negative U/S, a contrasted MRI of the adrenal glands is obtained.

## Slide 34

- Androgen Excess Treatment
- The standard of care for adrenal tumors resulting in androgen excess is surgical removal of the mass.
- Prognosis is excellent for a benign mass, but less optimal for a malignancy.
- Non-adrenal causes of androgen excess will require different treatments.

## Slide 35

- Adrenal Androgen Deficiency
- Part of primary adrenal insufficiency.
- Loss of adrenal androgens in men does not cause clinically significant disease.  Women may experience decreased sex drive/ libido and loss of a general feeling of well being.
- Supplementation with DHEA 50mg daily is helpful for symptom relief.
- DHEA is converted peripherally into testosterone.

## Slide 36

- Incidentalomas
- These are clinically inapparent adrenal masses that are discovered on imaging done for another reason.  Incidentalomas are fairly common.
- Some are biologically active and most commonly secrete small amounts of cortisol that may not be enough to cause obvious clinical disease.
- Occult malignancy must always be on the differential in persons with an incidentaloma.

## Slide 37

- Incidentalomas
- All patients should have an ACTH level and  dexamethasone suppression testing performed when the incidentaloma is discovered.
- Hypertensive patients should also have work-up for primary hyperaldosteronism (PAC, PRA, ARR) and pheochromocytoma.
- Surgical removal is indicated if the mass is > 5cm or it is hormonally active. If it is not, repeat monitoring is recommended.

## Slide 38

- Pheochromocytoma
- A pheochromocytoma is a tumor of the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla, which secrete catecholamines like epinephrine, norepinephrine.
- 90% of pheochromocytomas are found in the adrenal medulla, but they may also occur in the mediastinum or abdomen.
    - Common extra-adrenal locations include the origin of the inferior mesenteric artery, bladder, and heart.
- 5% of persons with pheochromocytoma have bilateral tumors.

## Slide 39

- Pheochromocytoma
- 10% of adrenal pheochromocytomas and 35% of extra-adrenal pheochromocytomas are malignant.
- Benign pheochromocytomas carry a good prognosis once resected, but malignant tumors carry a poor prognosis despite resection and chemotherapy.
- The most common sites for metastasis for malignant pheochromocytoma include the bone, liver and the lymph nodes.

## Slide 40

- Pheochromocytoma
- Pheochromocytoma is rare, occurring in up to 0.2% of hypertensive individuals, so it is a much less common cause of secondary HTN.
- 10% of pheochromocytomas occur in children and the incidence peaks in persons 30-50 years of age.
- ~30% are associated with other disorders:
  - Multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) syndromes
  - von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome
  - Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)

> Notes: von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease, or von Hippel-Lindau syndrome, is a rare genetic disorder characterized by visceral cysts and benign tumors in multiple organ systems that have subsequent potential for malignant change.   Clinical hallmarks of VHL disease include the development of retinal and central nervous system (CNS) hemangioblastomas (blood vessel tumors), pheochromocytomas, multiple cysts in the pancreas and kidneys, and an increased risk for malignant transformation of renal cysts into renal cell carcinoma. 
> Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a multisystem genetic disorder that is characterized by cutaneous findings, most notably café-au-lait spots and axillary freckling, by skeletal dysplasias, and by the growth of both benign and malignant nervous system tumors, most notably benign neurofibromas.

## Slide 43

- Making the Diagnosis of Pheo
- Pheochromocytomas are best diagnosed by measuring the levels of catecholamine metabolites in the plasma or the urine.
- Use plasma free metanephrine testing in patients who are at high risk of having pheochromocytoma
  - Those with predisposing genetic syndromes
  - Persons with a family/personal history of pheo
- Plasma free metanephrine testing is more sensitive but less specific.

## Slide 44

- Making the Diagnosis of Pheo
- For persons at lower risk of having pheo, use a 24-hour urine collection measuring levels of creatinine, catecholamines, vanllylmandelic acid and metanephrines.
    - Time the test to occur during or immediately after a hypertensive crisis if possible.
- If either biochemical test is positive, obtain imaging to evaluate for the pheochromocytoma location.
        - CT of the abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast
        - Contrasted MRI of the adrenals (if CT is non-diagnostic)

## Slide 45

- Treatment of Pheo
- Hypertension that is associated with a  pheo is typically resistant to standard medications.
- Patients are begun on pre-op alpha blockade, with doxazosin then beta blockade is added if needed.  Avoid unopposed beta blockade and encourage sodium intake and good hydration.
- After 1-2 weeks, a laparoscopic or open subtotal adrenalectomy (or larger surgery) is performed.

## Slide 46

- Treatment of Pheo
- During surgery, nicardipine and/or nitroprusside are used to control hemodynamic swings from tumor manipulation.
- Post-operatively, serum free metanephrine levels will normalize within 2 weeks and should be checked yearly for ten years.  Patients with MEN syndromes will require lifelong testing.
- If the pheochromocytoma is inoperable, the patient is treated with lifelong alpha blockers

## Slide 47

- MEN Syndromes
- Multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes are inherited disorders that predispose a person to developing multiple endocrine tumors, some of which may be malignant.  Two types:
  - MEN1: caused by loss of MEN tumor suppressor gene
  - MEN2: caused by mutations of the RET gene
- A person who reaches middle age with only one endocrine tumor likely does not have MEN.
- Children of persons with an MEN syndrome have a 50% chance of inheriting the disorder.

## Slide 48

- MEN1 Manifestations
- Parathyroid adenoma resulting in primary hyperparathyroidism, affects almost all patients with MEN1 and is usually the first tumor to occur.
- Anterior pituitary tumors, may be non-functional, if functional, usually prolactinoma.
- [picture]
- Enteropancreatic tumors (most commonly gastrinomas, insulinomas) and less commonly tumors of the skin, adrenal glands or carcinoid tumors

## Slide 49

- MEN2 Syndromes
- Persons with any of the MEN2 syndromes have a 95% chance of medullary thyroid cancer.
- They also have a 50% chance of developing pheochromocytoma, but it is rarely malignant.
- [picture]
- MEN2 is divided into 3 types- MEN2A, MEN2B and FMTC (familial medullary thyroid CA)
    - Multiple other endocrine abnormalities distinguish each type of MEN2.
