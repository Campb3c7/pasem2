## Slide 13

- Metformin (Glucophage)
- Drug class: biguanides (the only drug in the class)
- MOA:
  - ↓ hepatic production of glucose
  - ↓ absorption of glucose from GI tract
  - ↑ insulin sensitivity / peripheral glucose uptake
  - Formulations: Immediate-release (IR) and Extended-release (ER)
  - Typical Max Dose: The maximum dose is 2000mg per day

## Slide 14

- Metformin (Glucophage)
- Indications: Treatment of T2DM
- Off-label uses
- Pre-diabetes prevention
- Polycystic ovarian syndrome
- Gestational Diabetes
- Elimination: renal
- Pregnancy: crosses placenta, insulin preferred
- Lactation: does enter breastmilk, relative infant dose (RID) is low

## Slide 15

- Metformin:  Advantages
- Metformin is effective
- ↓ A1C by 1-1.5%
- Wt neutral to modest weight loss
- Low risk of hypoglycemia when used as monotherapy
- FDA approved for age ≥ 10 years

## Slide 16

- Metformin: Side Effects
- GI side effects *
  - Diarrhea, N/V
  - Metallic taste
  - Minimized by slow titration and/or XR formulation
- Vitamin B-12 deficiency with long term use
  - Consider periodic B12 monitoring
- Lactic Acidosis, rare but serious (BBW)

## Slide 17

- Metformin:  Clinical Considerations
- Dosing
- Start low and titrate gradually
- Example: 500 mg daily → 500 mg BID
- XR may improve GI tolerability
- Renal
- Do not use if eGFR <30
- Starting at eGFR 30–45 is not recommended
- If eGFR falls <45 → reassess benefits/risks
- Iodinated contrast
- Hold metformin in at-risk patients
- Recheck renal function after 48 hr before restarting

## Slide 22

- GLP-1 Receptor Agonists, MOA
- GLP-1 is an endogenous incretin hormone released from the intestine in response to nutrients.
- GLP-1 receptors are present in multiple tissues, including the pancreas, GI tract, and CNS.
- GLP-1 receptor activation:
- ↑ Glucose-dependent insulin secretion
- ↓ Inappropriate glucagon secretion
- ↓ Gastric emptying
- ↑ Satiety / ↓ food intake

## Slide 23

- GLP-1 Receptor Agonists
- Liraglutide (Victoza) SQ daily
- Semaglutide (Ozempic) — SQ weekly
- Semaglutide (Rybelsus) — oral daily
- Dulaglutide (Trulicity) — SQ weekly
- Exenatide (Byetta) — SQ twice daily
- Exenatide ER (Bydureon BCise) — SQ weekly

## Slide 24

- GLP-1 RA: A1C + Renal Function
- Expected A1C reduction: ~1–2%
- Low intrinsic risk of hypoglycemia
- Renal considerations are agent-specific
  - Most GLP-1 RAs do not require renal dose adjustment
  - Exenatide has greater renal restrictions
- Monitor for volume depletion/AKI with significant GI adverse effects

## Slide 25

- GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: Adverse Effects
- GI effects are most common *
  - Nausea, vomiting diarrhea, or constipation
  - Usually greatest during initiation and dose escalation
  - Slow titration may improve tolerability
  - Counsel patients to eat smaller meals and stop when full
  - Significant vomiting/diarrhea – risk of dehydration and AKI

## Slide 26

- Acute pancreatitis
- Has been reported with GLP-1 RAs
- Counsel: persistent, severe abdominal pain ± radiation to the back
- Discontinue if pancreatitis is suspected
- Use caution/consider alternatives with a history of pancreatitis
- Injection-site reactions
- May occur with injectable agents
- Rotate injection sites
- GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: Adverse Effects

- Human relevance is unknown

## Slide 32

- DPP-4 Inhibitors
- DPP-4 breaks down endogenous incretin hormones, including GLP-1 and GIP.
- DPP-4 inhibitors ↓ incretin degradation → prolong endogenous incretin activity.

## Slide 33

- Choices of DPP-4 Inhibitors
      - Linagliptin (Tradjenta)
      - Sitagliptin (Januvia)
      - Saxagliptin (Onglyza)
      - Alogliptin (Nesina)
      - Renal dosing: Most require dose adjustment in CKD; linagliptin does not.

## Slide 34

- DPP-4 Inhibitors
- Modest glucose-lowering efficacy
  - ↓ A1C approximately 0.5–0.8%
- Weight neutral
- Low risk of hypoglycemia when used alone

## Slide 35

- DPP-4 Inhibitors: Adverse Effects
- Generally well tolerated
- Pancreatitis — rare; discontinue if suspected
- Severe joint pain — rare FDA warning
- Bullous pemphigoid — rare
- Hypersensitivity reactions — rare

## Slide 36

- DPP-4 Inhibitors: Cardiovascular Safety
- DPP-4 inhibitors have not demonstrated cardiovascular benefit
- Saxagliptin: increased risk of hospitalization for heart failure
- FDA labeling includes HF warnings for saxagliptin and alogliptin
- Other DPP-4 inhibitors have not demonstrated increased HF hospitalization in cardiovascular outcomes trials

## Slide 37

- DPP-4 Inhibitors + GLP-1 Based Therapy
- Do not combine a DPP-4 inhibitor with a GLP-1 receptor agonist or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist.
- There is no meaningful additional glucose-lowering benefit from combining these therapies.

## Slide 39

- SGLT2 Inhibitors: Mechanism of Action
- SGLT2 is a sodium-glucose cotransporter in the proximal renal tubule
- Responsible for the majority of filtered glucose reabsorption
- SGLT2 inhibition → ↓ glucose reabsorption → ↑ urinary glucose excretion
- Results in glucosuria and osmotic diuresis
- Mechanism is independent of insulin
- [picture]

## Slide 40

- SGLT2- Inhibitors: Indications / Major Clinical Uses
- Type 2 diabetes
- Heart failure — HFrEF or HFpEF
- Chronic kidney disease
- Cardiovascular risk reduction in selected patients with T2DM

## Slide 41

- SGLT2 Inhibitors: Clinical Effects
- Expected A1C reduction: ~0.5–1%
- Low risk of hypoglycemia when used alone
- Modest weight loss
- Modest reduction in blood pressure
- Intermediate glucose-lowering efficacy
- Glucose-lowering effect decreases as renal function declines

## Slide 42

- Empagliflozin (Jardiance)
- Dapagliflozin (Farxiga)
- Canagliflozin (Invokana)
- Ertugliflozin (Steglatro)

## Slide 43

- SGLT2- Inhibitors Adverse Effects
- Genital mycotic infections*
- Volume depletion / hypotension
- Polyuria
- Euglycemic DKA
  - DKA may occur with normal or only mildly elevated glucose
- Rare: Fournier gangrene

## Slide 44

- SGLT2- Inhibitors: Safety Considerations
- Euglycemic DKA risk increases with:
  - Acute illness / prolonged fasting
  - Surgery
  - Significant insulin reduction

  - Ertugliflozin: 4 days
- Renal function
- Glucose-lowering efficacy ↓ as eGFR declines

- Require functioning pancreatic β-cells
- Effectiveness may decline over time as T2DM progresses

- Glimepiride (Amaryl)
- Glipizide (Glucotrol)

  - ↑ risk of prolonged hypoglycemia

  - May cause hemolytic anemia

  - Cross-reactivity with sulfonylureas is unlikely

## Slide 50

- Sulfonylurea overdose can cause severe hypoglycemia
- Hypoglycemia may be prolonged and recurrent
- Patients require extended glucose monitoring

## Slide 52

- Thiazolidinediones MOA
- Increase insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues
- ↑ Glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue
- ↓ Hepatic glucose production
- Do not stimulate insulin secretion
- Medications:
  - Pioglitazone (Actos)
  - Rosiglitazone (Avandia)

## Slide 53

- Thiazolidinediones: Clinical Effects
- Expected A1C reduction: ~0.5–1%
- Low risk of hypoglycemia when used alone
- Weight gain
- Effects develop slowly — may take several weeks for full benefit

## Slide 54

- Thiazolidinediones: Adverse Effects
- Weight gain*
- Fluid retention / edema*
- May cause or worsen heart failure
- ↑ Risk of fractures, particularly in women
- Hypoglycemia is uncommon when used alone
- Heart Failure
- Fluid retention may cause or worsen heart failure
- FDA Boxed Warning
- Contraindicated in NYHA Class III-IV heart failure

## Slide 56

- Meglitinides

## Slide 63

- Insulin- A History Lesson
- Prior to the 1920’s patients with diabetes tried to control their disease using a very strict diet.  Unfortunately, type I diabetics generally died within a year of diagnosis as their insulin production ceased.
- In the early 1920’s MacLeod, Banting, Collip, and Best were able to extract insulin from animal pancreatic tissue and purify it to be safe in humans- MacLeod and Banting won the 1923 Nobel Prize for this discovery.

## Slide 64

- Insulin- A History Lesson
- For the next 60 years, people relied on beef or pork insulin to treat diabetes, while researchers sequenced the insulin protein (Nobel prize in 1959) and determined its crystalline structure (1969).
- Then, in 1978, researchers perfected the technique to mass produce the A chain and the B chain of insulin using recombinant DNA technology and then combined the chains using a chemical reaction to create human insulin on a large scale.

## Slide 65

- [picture]
- Biosynthetic human insulin is now made by genetically engineered bacteria containing the gene for human insulin proteins.
- Bacteria are grown in fermentation tanks, and when adequate amounts of A or B proteins have been made, the bacteria are lysed, and the insulin proteins harvested, purified and chemically combined.

## Slide 71

- Mealtime (Prandial) Insulin
- Rapid-acting
- Lispro (Humalog)
- Aspart (NovoLog)
- Glulisine (Apidra)
- Give at/around mealtime
- Faster onset, shorter duration

- Can be given IV
- Pump pearl: Insulin pumps typically use rapid-acting insulin for both basal and mealtime insulin delivery.

## Slide 72

- NPH (Humulin N, Novolin N)
- Intermediate-acting
- Provides basal insulin coverage
- Has a pronounced peak → greater hypoglycemia risk than long-acting basal analogs
- Cloudy suspension — must be gently resuspended before use
- May be given once or twice daily

## Slide 73

- Long-acting (Basal) Insulin
- Insulin glargine
- Lantus / Basaglar / Semglee — U-100
- Toujeo — U-300
- Provides relatively peakless basal insulin coverage
- Usually dosed once daily
- Do not mix with other insulins in the same syringe

## Slide 74

- Ultra-Long-acting (Basal) Insulin
- Insulin degludec (Tresiba)
- Ultra-long-acting basal insulin
- Duration of action >42 hours
- Minimal peak
- Dosed once daily
- Available as U-100 and U-200

## Slide 75

## Slide 80

- Premixed Insulin
- Premixed insulin combines basal/intermediate + mealtime insulin in a fixed ratio.
- Examples
- Humulin/Novolin 70/30: 70% NPH + 30% regular insulin
- NovoLog Mix 70/30: 70% insulin aspart protamine + 30% insulin aspart

- Advantages
- Fewer injections
- Simpler regimen
- Provides both basal and mealtime coverage
- Disadvantages
- Fixed ratio → less flexibility
- Cannot independently adjust basal vs. mealtime insulin
- Requires more consistent meal timing/intake
- Hypoglycemia risk if meals/activity vary

## Slide 84

- Complications of Insulin Therapy
- Hypoglycemia is the most important acute adverse effect of insulin.
- Higher risk with:
  - Intensive insulin therapy
  - Prior/recurrent hypoglycemia
  - Impaired hypoglycemia awareness
  - Older age/cognitive impairment
  - Kidney disease
  - Missed/delayed meals or increased activity
  - β-blocker therapy may blunt adrenergic warning symptoms
- Remember from Hypoglycemia:
  - Recurrent episodes can → impaired hypoglycemia awareness
  - Severe hypoglycemia can → confusion, seizure, LOC/coma
  - CGM/AID can help reduce hypoglycemia risk

## Slide 87

- T2DM Treatment – Where do I Start?
- Lifestyle + Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support (DSMES) for everyone
- 2. Look FIRST for compelling comorbidities
- ASCVD / high CV risk → GLP-1 RA and/or SGLT2 inhibitor with demonstrated CV benefit
- Heart failure → SGLT2 inhibitor
- CKD → SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA with demonstrated kidney/CV benefit
- Key Point: These therapies may be indicated regardless of A1C or metformin use.

## Slide 88

- ASCVD: What Do We Mean?
- Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) includes:
- Coronary disease: MI, acute coronary syndrome, coronary revascularization
- Cerebrovascular disease: ischemic stroke or TIA
- Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)
- Why does this matter in diabetes?Presence of ASCVD can directly influence medication selection.

## Slide 89

- T2DM Treatment - If No Compelling Comorbidity Drives Therapy
- 1. Determine the individualized A1C goal
- 2. How far is the patient from goal?
      - <1.5% above goal → generally start one agent
      - ≥1.5% above goal → consider initial combination therapy
- Example:Goal <7%, current A1C 9% → 2% above goal → consider combo therapy

## Slide 90

- T2DM Treatment - If No Compelling Comorbidity Drives Therapy
- 3. Choose medication(s) based on:
- Glucose-lowering efficacy
- Weight goals
- Hypoglycemia risk
- Adverse effects
- Cost/access
- Patient preference
- Metformin remains a common first-line option when there is no other reason to select a different medication.

## Slide 91

- T2DM Treatment - When to Consider Insulin in T2DM
- Consider insulin when:
- Symptoms of significant hyperglycemia (polyuria, polydipsia)
- A1C >10%
- Blood glucose ≥300 mg/dL
- Evidence of catabolism (e.g., unexpected weight loss or ketosis)
- Glycemic goals cannot be achieved with noninsulin therapy
- Important:In the absence of severe hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis, GLP-1–based therapy is preferred before insulin for initial/add-on injectable therapy.
