## Slide 3

- ULTRASOUND
- An ultrasound transducer (probe) sends a high frequency inaudible sound wave pulse and receives echoes back.
- The design of the transducer will determine the shape and the field-of-view of the ultrasound image

## Slide 4

- ULTRASOUND
- Ultrasound gel is placed between the transducer and the patient’s skin.  The gel is water-soluble and uses the ability of sound waves to travel through water to transmit the pulse.
- [picture]
- As sound waves hit an “interface” some of the sound waves pass though and some are reflected back and picked up by the probe.
- The strength of the returning echoes as well as the time it takes for them to return create the picture that we see.

## Slide 5

- The difference between adjacent structures is translated into spatial and contrast information and is viewed as a moving 2 dimensional grayscale image.
- 3D ultrasound is created by computationally combining multiple 2D US images and 4D uses 3D plus time- these are most commonly used in OB

## Slide 14

- Cellular and animal studies have shown that high-power (high intensity) ultrasound used in therapeutic and surgical applications, can cause potential biological effects in the body.
  - High frequencies can cause thermal heating or mechanical injury to tissues at the microscopic level.
- At lower levels of exposure, such as diagnostic ultrasound, there is no established evidence of any specific harmful effect
  - Commercial souvenir scans, ie. detailed 3-D and 4-D sonography (facial imaging and recording of the baby's movement in the womb) require prolonged and more intense ultrasound exposure.

- MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING

## Slide 18

- In the 1930’s and 40’s, scientists discovered that the nuclei of some atoms, when placed in a static magnetic field and stimulated by a second, oscillating magnetic field, can absorb and then re-emit some of this energy in the form of radiowaves.
- This principle is used in nuclear spectroscopy to study the structure of chemicals and the interactions between atomic nuclei in analytical and physical chemistry.
- MAGNETIC RESONANCE

## Slide 19

- Further research has allowed this chemical information to be localized in space, creating the foundation for nuclear magnetic resonance imaging.
- The nuclear was eventually dropped (marketing reasons) and MRI has evolved as an essential technique of imaging.
- MRI is best used for soft tissues with a high water content but image acquisition takes much longer than CT- so the area of the body to be studied must remain as still as possible.
- MAGNETIC RESONANCE

## Slide 20

- Most MRI systems use a superconducting magnet, which consists of many coils or windings of wire through which a current of electricity is passed, creating a magnetic field of up to 3.0 tesla.
- Maintaining such a large magnetic field requires significant energy, which is accomplished by superconductivity, or reducing the resistance in the wires to almost zero.
- To reduce the resistance to zero, the wires are continually bathed in liquid helium at 452 degrees below zero Fahrenheit (269 below zero degrees Celsius).
- MRI: THE MACHINE

## Slide 21

- There is a horizontal tube, called the bore, which runs through the superconducting magnet from front to back.  This is where the patient enters.
- [picture]
- Whether the patient goes in head or feet first is determined by what part of the body needs to be examined.
- There are several other magnets in an MRI
- machine which are less powerful, but essential in creating the magnetic field around the patient to obtain the correct views.
- MRI: THE MACHINE

## Slide 22

- Once the body part to be scanned is in the exact center, or isocenter, of the magnetic field, the MRI scan can begin.
- An MRI takes advantage of the fact that the human body is made up of large amounts of water and fat- both of which contain numerous hydrogen ions- or protons.
- These atoms are randomly spinning, or precessing, on their axis, like a child's top. All of the atoms are going in various directions, but when placed in a magnetic field, the atoms line up in the direction of the field.
- MRI: THE SCAN

## Slide 23

- Next, the MRI machine applies a radio frequency (RF) pulse that is specific only to hydrogen. The system directs the pulse toward the area of the body we want to examine.
- When the pulse is applied, the unmatched protons absorb the energy and spin again in a different direction.
- This is the "resonance" part of MRI. The radiofrequency pulse forces them to spin at a particular frequency, in a particular direction.
- MRI: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 24

- Detectors measure the different energies released by realignment from the varying tissues.
- [picture]
- [picture]
- MRI: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 25

- The MRI scanner can pick out a very small point inside the patient's body and ask it, essentially, "What type of tissue are you?"
- The system goes through the patient's body point by point, building up a map of tissue types. It then integrates all of this information to create 2-D images or 3-D models with a mathematical formula.
- The computer receives the signal from the spinning protons as mathematical data; then converts the data into an image. That’s the "imaging" part of MRI.
- MRI: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 26

- The contrast between different tissues occurs from different relaxation times and are translated to the shades of gray on the image that we see.
- Images are viewed in the same planes as in CT; axial, coronal, sagittal, and a new one, oblique.
- READING AN MRI

## Slide 27

- Manipulation of the settings on the MRI machine as it is acquiring images yields different views, called sequences.
- Sequences will brighten or darken the appearance of the tissue in order to make it easier to discriminate from surrounding tissues.
- [picture]
- There are several sequences that the modern MRI scanner can utilize.
- READING AN MRI: SEQUENCES

## Slide 28

- T1 weighted (T1W) sequences are part of almost all MRI protocols and are best thought of as the most 'anatomical' of images (historically known as the anatomical sequence), resulting in images that most closely approximate the appearances of tissues macroscopically, although even this is a gross simplification.
- T2-weighted images generally make fluid bright. Many pathologic processes increase tissue water, so edema and inflammation are often clearly visable on T2.
  - T2 → think “water bright”Useful starting rule—not every high-signal finding is simply water, and tissue appearance still depends on the sequence and pathology.
- READING AN MRI: SEQUENCES

## Slide 42

- When ordering an MRI with gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs), the patient’s kidney function and allergies must be assessed.
- Group I GBCAs – high risk of nephrogentic systemic fibrosis (NSF). These have been withdrawn from use in the US
- Group II GBCAs – low risk of NSF and generally considered safe in all patients including eGFR<30 and AKI
- Ultimately, the radiologist or hospital policy will dictate use of contrast in these patients but risk assessment is a must.
- MRI CONSIDERATIONS: GADOLINIUM CONTRAST

## Slide 43

- Allergies to gadolinium are not nearly as common as allergies to iodinated contrast; so when a patient describes a contrast allergy, they are almost always referring to iodinated contrast.
- Gadolinium contrast should be avoided in pregnancy when possible. Gadolinium does cross the placenta and its use is controversial. Long-term fetal effects are uncertain.
- MRI CONSIDERATIONS: GADOLINIUM CONTRAST

## Slide 47

- DIAGNOSTIC NUCLEAR MEDICINE: THE SCIENCE
- Nuclear medicine studies use a radioactive compound or radiotracer and the ionizing radiation from the decay is quantitatively detected, measured, and imaged with instruments.
- Nuclear medicine studies interrogate physiologic or pathologic processes at a molecular level and provide targeted therapy for a variety of diseases.
- Nuclear medicine studies are clinically used to assess most organ systems with almost 100 different types of studies or therapies performed in the US. Many more play a crucial role in research.

## Slide 48

- The amount of radiopharmaceutical prescribed for a study should be the lowest dose able to create diagnostic images for the expected patient population, termed the “as low as reasonably achievable” principle (ALARA).
- Prescribing of radiopharmaceuticals is restricted to physicians who have undergone specialized training and fulfilled the requirements for an authorized user (AU) set out by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) and the states.
| Radiation Delivered | CXR: 0.1 mSv | CT chest: 7 mSv | Stress Test: 11 mSv |
- DIAGNOSTIC NUCLEAR MEDICINE: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 49

- Radiopharmaceuticals contain radioactive atoms that are unstable and produce ionizing radiation when they decay.
- High-energy rays produced by the decay interact with special crystals in nuclear medicine cameras to produce light, and this light is used by the technology to create the images.
- The types of imaging rays from radioisotope decay can be broadly placed in two categories: gamma rays or positron emission.
- DIAGNOSTIC NUCLEAR MEDICINE: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 50

- Radioactive tracers are bound tightly to carrier molecules, like albumin, although this varies depending on the tracer and the purpose of the scan/ area of the body to be enhanced.
- Tracers can be inhaled, taken by mouth, directly injected to an organ, or (most commonly) injected intravenously.
- A patient and their bodily fluids remain mildly radioactive for a period of time after the study.  Caution must be exercised when patient is around children or pregnant females.
- DIAGNOSTIC NUCLEAR MEDICINE: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 51

- Gamma camera studies most commonly use the radioisotope technetium-99m.  The camera collects the gamma rays being emitted from the patient as the technetium decays and electronics process this information into viewable nuclear images or scintigrams.
- [picture]
- [picture]
- The gamma camera creates planar (two-dimensional) images.
- DIAGNOSTIC NUCLEAR MEDICINE: THE SCIENCE

## Slide 52

- When the gamma camera is rotated around the patient, a three-dimensional data is collected in a process termed single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and displayed in a multiplanar format—axial, coronal, and sagittal.
- [picture]
- [picture]
- SINGLE PHOTON EMISSION COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY (SPECT)

## Slide 53

- SPECT images can better localize positive findings than planar images and can aid in the visualization of lesions which would be obscured by overlying tracer activity on the planar gamma camera.
- Increasingly, gamma cameras are equipped with an integrated CT scanner allowing for SPECT-CT studies. The merging of a functional image (SPECT) and anatomic data (CT) allows for very precise localization of abnormalities.
  - CT- an enlarged lymph node
  - SPECT- a lymph node with hyperactivity
- SINGLE PHOTON EMISSION COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY (SPECT)

## Slide 54

- Positron Emission Tomography (PET)
- Decay of positron emitters, such as fluorine-18, leads to a matter-antimatter reaction, which produces a pair of gamma rays that travel 180° apart.
- A positron emission tomography (PET) machine uses a ring of crystals to take advantage of the two rays and requires near-simultaneous detection of the rays.
- The result is better spatial resolution in the image than a gamma camera study can achieve.

## Slide 55

- PET studies are three-dimensional data sets that are typically displayed in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes.
- Today, PET machines have an integrated CT scanner that provides anatomic images and quick attenuation correction. The anatomic data provided by the CT complements the functional data leading to a higher specificity and some improvement in sensitivity in detection of many cancers: PET-CT.
- PET-MRI machines have recently come into the commercial market, and their clinical role is still being defined.
- Positron Emission Tomography (PET)

## Slide 56

- PET is commonly used for oncology to detect the presence of metastasis and as part of multimodality staging
          - For example: a patient presents to the hospital with a cough and undergoes a chest CT which finds a small mass in the hilum.  A metastatic work-up with a CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast is obtained which shows no other masses.  A biopsy reveals small cell lung cancer, and a PET scan is obtained before the start of chemotherapy treatment to improve staging and detect otherwise occult hypermetabolic disease.
- Positron Emission Tomography (PET)
