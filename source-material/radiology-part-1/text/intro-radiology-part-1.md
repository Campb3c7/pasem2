- RADIOLOGY
- Imaging is a powerful tool in the pursuit of a diagnosis

  - The patient’s anatomy and any alterations to the normal anatomy.

- Image contrast is the difference in brightness between an area of interest and its surroundings. The larger the difference in brightness, the easier it is to differentiate one structure from another.

## Slide 10

- RADIOLOGY TERMS
- Attenuation: refers to the reduction in the intensity of an x-ray beam as it passes through matter due to either the absorption or scattering of photons.
- Different materials in the body will attenuate x-rays to different degrees to help produce images.
  - X-rays pass through air-filled lungs with little attenuation, but the x-ray beam is substantially attenuated when passing through bone.

## Slide 11

- CONTRAST AGENTS
- Contrast agents, such as iodine and barium, are most commonly positively enhancing an image and result in attenuation of the x-ray beam which increases image contrast.
- Contrast agents can be administered through any cavity (natural or iatrogenic) and enhance a difference between tissues that would normally have similar x-ray absorption.

## Slide 20

- Renal impairment
  - Important with IV iodine or gadolinium contrast as these are cleared by the kidneys. Evaluate the eGFR before ordering to determine benefit versus risk.
- Pregnancy
  - Generally, CT is avoided in pregnancy but can be used after risk-benefit assessment. Low osmolality iodinated contrast is considered appropriate when diagnostically needed.

## Slide 21

- GI perforation
    - Water-soluble contrast agent, such as Gastrografin, should be used when the clinician is looking for esophageal or bowel perforation, obstruction, or when a surgery is planned as Barium causes severe peritonitis
- Allergies and medications
  - Modern iodinated allergic-like reactions are uncommon (about 0.6% overall, 0.04% severe).  Reactions range from mild such as hives and itching to severe, anaphylaxis.
  - If a study must be done with contrast, an institutional premedication protocol may be used when indicated.
- CONTRAST CONSIDERATIONS

## Slide 22

  - Mild reactions occur when patients develop warmth, flushing, N/V, mild urticaria, scratchy throat.  Treatment is observation ~ 30 minutes +/- benadryl
  - Moderate reactions include severe urticaria, bronchospasm, transient hypotension and tachycardia.  These require close monitoring until the symptoms resolve completely.
  - Severe reactions include diffuse erythema, altered mental status, seizures, severe bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, severe hypotension, or sudden cardiac arrest.
- IODATED CONTRAST REACTIONS

## Slide 23

- Only about one third of patients with severe reactions have a prior history of reactions and pre-treatment to prevent these has very poor evidence showing efficacy for anything other than mild reactions.
- Contrast extravasation is also potentially severe.  Patients with extravasation will need close monitoring of skin and soft tissue. Typically, limb elevation and hot or cold compresses are used. Observation can range from several hours to overnight if needed.  Watch for compartment syndrome and consider surgical consultation.
- IODATED CONTRAST REACTIONS

## Slide 42

- WHEN DO WE USE GENERAL RADIOGRAPHY OVER OTHER MODALITIES?
- Initial diagnosis of bony structures in the outpatient and emergent settings

## Slide 46

## Slide 51

- The human eye cannot effectively differentiate between all the shades of gray that make up the full spectrum of attenuation values in a CT image (-1000 to 1000 HU).
- To make a CT easier to read, the clinician establishes a limited view of the CT by selecting those Hounsfield units that will best look at what they want to see.
- A radiologist performing an official reading of a CT will do this multiple times to look at tissues of varying densities.
- READING A CT SCAN

## Slide 52

- Here is how it works:
- A limited gray scale is established by reducing the number of Hounsfield units being displayed by setting a “window width” – for example: -100 HU to 500 HU.
- This also sets a “window level” in the exact middle of the scale, in this example the level would be 200 HU
- The window width sets parameters so that only HU in that range are included.  Those above and below the window width limits are displayed as white and black, respectively.
- READING A CT SCAN

## Slide 53

- Window width: the window width refers to the number of different HU units that will be represented by different shades of gray. Any HU value that falls below the lower value of the window width will show up as BLACK on the scan while any HU value that is above the upper value of the window width will be WHITE.

## Slide 54

- Increasing the window width will DECREASE the contrast of the image because a larger change in density will be required to change the shade of grey represented by a HU.  Decreasing the window width will INCREASE the contrast of the image.
- Note the ability to detect the muscles and fat in the subcutaneous tissues of the chest wall is significantly reduced when the window width is increased from 400 to 1,000.
- [picture]
- READING A CT SCAN

## Slide 55

- So, when a clinician reads a CT scan, they will look at the images multiple times.  Each time they will look at different structures using a window width and level that brings those areas into sharper focus.
- For example, when a radiology clinician reads a chest CT, they will look at
  - At bone windows to evaluate the ribs, sternum and spine
  - At lung windows to evaluate the lung parenchyma
  - At chest windows to evaluate the vascular structures and the air- filled bronchi/ trachea
- READING A CT SCAN

## Slide 61

- Is my patient able to hold still for the test?
  - A moving patient causes imaging artifact, creating a blurry and nondiagnostic study. Repeat of the test to obtain an optimal image exposes the patient to additional radiation.
  - An important consideration in small children, encephalopathic patients and those with dementia who may not be able to remain still.
  - Less of a concern for CT- which acquires images quickly and much more of an issue for MRI which has a longer image acquisition time and a more claustrophobic environment.
- CT CONSIDERATIONS

## Slide 62

- WITH OR WITHOUT CONTRAST?
- Obtain a CT WITHOUT contrast if:
- The patient has a contraindication to the administration of iodinated contrast- prior severe allergic-like reaction to iodinated contrast. Reduced eGFR or AKI must also be taken into account
- Contrast does not enhance the area of interest thus it is not needed for diagnosis.
  - If the examiner is looking at bone, calcifications, kidney stones
  - Lung pleura and parenchyma- except in cases of malignancy.  Lung infection can be seen without contrast.

## Slide 63

- Obtain a CT WITH contrast for:
- Enhancement of inflammatory processes
  - “Fat stranding” seen in inflammatory processes such as diverticulitis or appendicitis.
  - Evaluation of infectious process/abscess
- Evaluation of vascular structures, ie. aorta and arterial branches, order a CT angiogram or CTA- not a CT with IV contrast.
- WITH OR WITHOUT CONTRAST?

## Slide 64

- Obtain a CT WITH contrast for:
- Suspected malignancy
  - Contrast will help to differentiate normal from abnormal tissue and to identify metastasis.
  - Hypervascular lesions will readily enhance with IV contrast
  - Some lesions are hypovascular compared to surrounding structures.
- Different phases of enhancement based on blood flow are found in the liver, pancreas and kidney.  The radiologist may suggest a particular “protocol” for imaging these organs in order to take advantage of this to make a diagnosis.
